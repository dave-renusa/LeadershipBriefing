Volume 23 · Friday, July 3, 2026

Happy Friday, all.

Tomorrow is the cliff. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, a wind or solar project has to commence construction by July 4, 2026 — that is Saturday — or be in service by the end of next year, to keep the federal tax credits our clients’ economics are built on. So this is the week the industry has been sprinting toward: transformers bought, piles driven, physical work started, all to plant a flag before the holiday and lock four more years of runway. The tell for us is that the sprint doesn’t settle the siting fights — it sharpens them. Every developer racing the clock is a developer who needs a “yes” from a host community faster than that community wants to give one, and that compression is the pressure our engagement work has to absorb.

The counter-fact, as ever, is the money, and it did not blink this week. IPX Power closed roughly $4.95 billion in financing for its Darden solar-and-storage complex in Fresno County, California — 1.15 GW of solar paired with 4.6 GWh of batteries, one of the largest single-project raises of the year, from a company that only spun out of Intersect Power in March after Google bought the rest. Ten-figure checks are still clearing for the rural-host solar-plus-storage model, deadline or no deadline. What’s tightening isn’t appetite; it’s the calendar and the grid, and the projects that solved siting before the sprint are the ones pouring concrete this weekend.

Standing items, so we don’t re-litigate them. New York’s first-in-the-nation statewide data-center moratorium: still no signature and no veto from Gov. Hochul. The Illinois POWER Act: no update. North Carolina’s NCUC solar freeze: still no ruling on the reconsideration petition. Ohio’s Oak Run Solar: still in OPSB remand. Sangamon County’s buffer-rezoning gambit: still no dated, in-window outcome, so we keep holding it. Washington’s storage-moratorium wave: still widening, no new in-window vote we hadn’t already logged. Monday’s Alle-Catt nighttime-noise ruling and Wednesday’s Busti-vs-Clay split: no new development. Virginia’s HB711 solar-preemption law took effect Tuesday; we’ll carry the first denial that has to file its reasons with the state. If any breaks, you’ll get a full item.

Also On Our Radar
  • The Missing Money Finds the Meter: analysts expect power-purchase-agreement prices to climb as the clean-energy tax credits phase out, Utility Dive reported Thursday. The logic, from Crux’s Josh Price: “that missing money has to come from somewhere to make the project pencil, and that will likely be through PPA prices.” LevelTen pegs the needed bump for solar at roughly $8 to $17.50 per MWh to stay economic without the ITC; a Camelot analysis figures a 200-MW solar farm lands around $40–45/MWh with no credit. For our clients the message is blunt: after Saturday, the projects that still get built pass more cost to the buyer, which makes the host-community benefit package — the tax base, the fire money, the local hire — harder to fund and more important to defend. The engagement math tightens right alongside the finance math.
  • 445 Gigawatts, and Still No Room: consulting firm ICF projects about 445 GW of mostly-solar-and-storage nameplate capacity coming online in the U.S. through 2030 — and warns it only contributes about 191 GW to peak load, against demand it sees rising 21% by 2030 and 39% by 2035. The line that matters to every project we touch: in ERCOT and PJM, ICF says there is no spare capacity to serve new demand beyond next year. That is the whole game in one sentence. The build-out is enormous and still not enough, which is exactly why the load fight and the renewables fight keep colliding in the same interconnection queue — and why “we need this power” is becoming our strongest argument in a hostile room.
  • The Build Still Banks: Matrix Renewables closed roughly $1.3 billion for an 859-MWdc solar and 167-MWh storage portfolio spread across Texas, California, and Idaho — two projects under construction (the 457-MWdc Tormes Solar in Navarro County, Texas, and an 86.5-MWh standalone battery in Kern County, California) and two operating assets refinanced. TPG Rise-backed, with D.E. Shaw putting up $210 million in preferred equity on the construction stage. It’s the IPX story again at a smaller scale: where the land, the queue slot, and the offtake line up, the capital is not the bottleneck. The bottleneck is the permit — which is our whole job.
  • Duke Takes the Check: Duke Energy surrendered its undeveloped Carolina Long Bay offshore-wind lease back to the Interior Department for a “partial reimbursement” — the utility paid $155 million for the tract off Brunswick County, North Carolina, in 2022, and will now “refocus” the roughly $129 million Interior is paying into nuclear, natural gas, and grid work instead. It’s the ninth offshore-wind lease the administration has bought back, more than $2.6 billion across the nine, each with strings steering the money away from wind. The read for us: offshore is being quietly unwound by settlement, and the onshore solar-storage build our clients run is now carrying even more of the load-growth burden the wires were supposed to share.
  • Make the Hyperscaler Pay: New Jersey lawmakers sent Gov. Sherrill a bill directing the Board of Public Utilities to write tariffs for data centers of 50 MW and up — requiring those large loads to pay for at least 85% of the power they request over a ten-year term, to shield everyone else’s bill from the AI build-out. Watch this one closely: the “ratepayers shouldn’t subsidize hyperscalers” argument is the sharpest tool the opposition uses against every big interconnection, ours included. When a legislature turns it into a take-or-pay tariff aimed squarely at data centers, it hands our renewable clients a clean line — our project isn’t the one shifting costs onto your grandmother’s bill.
Project Updates
  • NCA North Carolina House committee advanced HB 1213 on a 12-8 vote Tuesday to repeal the state’s 80% property-tax exclusion for utility-scale solar placed in service on or after July 1, 2027, sending it to House Rules. Existing facilities keep the break. The pitch from the county-commissioners’ association: the exemption costs counties about $40 million a year in forgone revenue. The pushback came from the industry and from farmers — grower Ken Gurganus told the panel that solar lease payments help cover fertilizer and fuel in a brutal farm economy. Note the fracture line for engagement: this isn’t clean red-versus-green. It’s counties wanting the tax base versus landowners wanting the lease income, and our clients can stand with both if the deal is structured to pay the county too.
  • VAGov. Spanberger signed a law formally defining agrivoltaics in Virginia code, held the signing at the Piedmont Environmental Council’s crop-and-solar farm at Roundabout Meadows in Loudoun County, and in doing so gave developers a labeled path to pair panels with active farming rather than displace it. Virginia already lists 13 agrivoltaic projects with NREL, most grazing or habitat, the largest a 175-MWdc sheep operation. Landing the day after HB711’s solar-preemption law took effect, it’s a deliberate one-two: the state is telling localities that solar is coming and handing them a version of it — farms that keep farming — that’s far easier to say yes to.
  • WABurien’s City Council unanimously enacted an emergency one-year data-center moratorium at its June 29 meeting, effective immediately, to buy staff time to study a use the city admits it has no in-house expertise on — the interim attorney said the moratorium’s very definition of “data center” was borrowed from Seattle. It’s the now-standard shape of the pause: a small city with no rulebook freezes the category rather than rule on a project cold. For the storage and solar work that keeps getting lumped in with data centers at the counter, the lesson repeats — when a town has no definitions, it reaches for a freeze, and the developer who arrives with a model ordinance is the one who avoids the year on ice.
On the Frontlines
  • AZConcrete Before the Fourth: EVelution Energy started construction Tuesday on a 28-MW, 150-acre solar array in Yuma County to power what it bills as America’s first commercial-scale cobalt-processing plant — the first permanent steel piles are in the ground, days ahead of the safe-harbor deadline. It’s a tidy example of the week’s throughline: a roughly $450 million project the company says will drive $1.2 billion in annual economic activity, breaking dirt on schedule because it lined up the site, the offtake (its own load), and the timing. When the buyer is the project next door and the land is already yours, the siting fight mostly evaporates — and you make the deadline.
  • NJSolar on the Balcony: in two unanimous votes, New Jersey’s legislature passed the Garden State Balcony Solar Act, legalizing portable plug-in solar devices up to 1,200 watts, exempting them from utility interconnection sign-off, and — the sharp part — barring landlords and HOAs from blocking renters who want them. It makes New Jersey the tenth state to say yes to balcony solar, with an April poll showing nearly 80% support. It’s a small-wattage story with an outsized engagement lesson: while the utility-scale fights grind through county boards, the distributed side keeps notching easy, popular wins by handing power — literally — to the individual homeowner and renter. The same week Trenton moved to make data centers pay their own way, it moved to let a renter clip a panel to the railing. That is what a pro-build coalition looks like when it’s working both ends.
  • USZinc Waits in the Wings: the International Zinc Association is pitching utilities and large-load customers on zinc-based batteries as a lithium alternative — touting thermal stability and a service life about 25% longer, against lithium’s fire narrative. The honest caveat in the piece: U.S. zinc capacity is still a rounding error, most installs a megawatt or less, held back by cost and by safety codes written for flammable lithium. But the tell for our storage clients is the framing, because it’s the framing we keep having to win at the town hall: a chemistry sold on “it doesn’t catch fire the way you’re afraid of” is a chemistry built for the community meeting, not just the spreadsheet. Watch whether “non-lithium” becomes a siting argument, not just an engineering one.
Plays of the Week
  • Siting Solutions ProjectWin-Win, Put It in the Statute: in a pv magazine essay this week, University of Texas law professor David Adelman and the Siting Solutions Project’s Nelson Falkenburg made the case that the barriers to clean energy are political and legal, not technical or economic — and are best solved with state-level siting policy tools, laid out in their new white paper on improving community outcomes while giving developers certainty. The play worth stealing is the reframe: stop fighting the county-by-county veto as a series of brawls and instead push the state to standardize the deal — enforceable setbacks and safety rules, a guaranteed community-benefit floor, and a predictable timeline — so the host town gets a real win in writing and the developer gets a yes it can bank. It’s the mirror image of the opposition’s own move (race to the statehouse and write the limits); the counter is to race there with a win-win the town actually wants. For RenUSA, the value is being the party that shows up holding the benefit framework, not just defending against the moratorium.
  • Clean-Energy BuyersBuy for Delivery, Not the Sticker: with the credit expiring Saturday, clean-energy procurement is pivoting away from lowest headline price toward deliverability and execution certainty — safe-harbored equipment, bankable offtake, an EPC that can actually hit the date. The tactic, and the lesson for anyone we advise: in a post-deadline market the winning bid isn’t the cheapest, it’s the one most likely to get built, and that shift rewards developers who did the unglamorous work — locking transformers, nailing interconnection, closing the host-community agreement — before the sprint. The engagement angle is real: a community that trusts a developer to finish is now a procurement asset, because a stalled project on a contested site is exactly the delivery risk buyers are learning to price out. Reliability of the relationship is becoming reliability of the megawatt.
