RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 49 · Monday, September 7, 2026

Happy Monday, all.

The fight has moved in front of the filing. In Ohio, at least five county commissions have now passed formal resolutions against a 765-kV transmission project that has not selected a route, has not filed with the Power Siting Board, and in most of these counties has not yet held its open house — Darke County's board joined the list Monday. In New Mexico, Torrance County landowners got a PNM representative to concede on the record that the company would pursue eminent domain, three weeks before PNM files anything with the PRC. In northeast Oklahoma, landowners were organized and quoted in the press two weeks after the route hit the docket. The old sequence — file, notice, hearing, opposition — has inverted, and the Sabin Center's new count of 888 state and local restrictions across 48 states is the accumulated residue of it. By the time there is a docket to comment on, the record is already written.

Must Read

Voting on a Line That Doesn't Exist Yet

In February, PJM selected Grid Growth Ventures to develop roughly 220 miles of new transmission across Ohio — four new 765-kilovolt lines and one 345-kV line, running from the Indiana border in Darke County east to Guernsey County, with additional 765-kV segments linking Coshocton and Guernsey to Pickaway and Fairfield counties. The company has not chosen a final route. It has not filed an application with the Ohio Power Siting Board. It is still, by its own account, weighing engineering feasibility, environmental constraints, land use and public feedback before narrowing its options, and it has scheduled a public meeting in Pickaway County for Sept. 28 at White Willow Meadows in Orient.

Five county commissions have already voted against it.

Shelby County's board went first, formally opposing the route on Aug. 27, in the same week Grid Growth announced it would hold an open house there. State Rep. Tim Barhorst followed on Aug. 31. Champaign County's commissioners passed their own resolution, citing the size of the proposed structures — 175 feet tall, some of them 150 feet wide — and their inconsistency with the county's comprehensive plan. The Miami County board is fighting a 190-mile segment. And on Monday the Darke County commissioners put their objection on paper, running through impacts to dairy, poultry, cattle and swine operations, to drainage systems and waterways, to property values, future development, scenic resources and — the detail that will travel furthest — medical response times. Darke is Ohio's leading agricultural production county, with more than $1.19 billion in agricultural sales in the 2022 USDA census. The commissioners' conclusion is flat: a 765-kV corridor through their county does not serve its long-term interests.

None of these resolutions has legal force. The Power Siting Board is not bound by a county resolution, and Grid Growth can route wherever the engineering and the OPSB record support. That is exactly why the resolutions matter. Ohio is the state where unanimous local-official opposition has functioned as a veto in practice even where it carried none in statute — seven state rejections of wind and solar projects, five developer withdrawals before a decision, and a siting board that has given local objection steadily more weight. Every commission that votes now is building the evidentiary record the OPSB will read later, and it is doing it while the developer is still deciding where to put the line. The resolutions are not reacting to a route. They are trying to price one.

Compare the sequence in Texas. There, the fight ran the conventional way — letters in December, hearings all spring, an attorney general's amicus brief, legislators demanding a pause — and on Aug. 28 the PUC unanimously approved more than 400 miles of 765-kV line anyway, from Glen Rose to Reeves County, with the reporting still catching up a week later. Opponents got a full and fair hearing and lost, because by the time a certificate case is filed the question has narrowed to route selection, and route selection is a fight between counties, not against a project. The Ohio commissions appear to have learned that lesson from watching Texas. They are objecting at the only stage where the answer can still be "somewhere else entirely."

The same inversion is visible in two other states this week. In New Mexico, PNM and Pattern Energy held a virtual open house for the roughly 100-mile Mid-State 345-kV project on Aug. 24 — before the company files its three PRC applications, expected mid-September to early October — and residents used it to press on easement width, tribal consultation and wildfire risk until a PNM representative acknowledged the company would pursue eminent domain in some circumstances. Loma Parda residents objected to widening a 50-foot right-of-way to 200 feet; the Manzano Land Grant Association's president stated flat opposition. In northeast Oklahoma, OK Transco filed the NEOTEP East route with the Corporation Commission on Aug. 21, and by Sept. 5 landowners across four counties had a shared frame in the local press — that the 345-kV line is "a pass through" delivering no in-state benefit. That is a message discipline problem, and it was solved by the opposition in fifteen days.

The Takeaway

Our engagement calendars are still built around the filing date, and the filing date is now the middle of the fight, not the start of it. On any linear project — transmission especially, but increasingly gen-tie and collector routes too — the county resolutions get written during route study, which means the window that decides the OPSB or PRC record opens months before there is a docket number. Two changes follow. First, the pre-application open house cannot be the first contact; by the time it is scheduled, three commissions have already voted. Second, when a county does pass a resolution, the response is not a rebuttal — it is a route conversation, in public, that gives the board something to withdraw the resolution for. Darke County did not object to transmission. It objected to a corridor through the county's dairy barns and drainage tile, and it said so in writing, which means it also told us exactly what a different answer would need to look like.

Also On Our Radar
  • Eight Hundred Eighty-Eight: the Sabin Center published the sixth edition of its opposition survey on Sept. 3, counting 888 state laws and local ordinances restricting renewable energy siting across 48 states — 865 of them local, 23 state-level across 19 states — alongside 567 projects that have faced significant local opposition, roughly 40% of which have been cancelled. Wind draws the most restrictions at 416 ordinances, solar 294, with 123 covering both. The authors attribute much of it to mis- and disinformation, and published a companion document rebutting 38 specific false claims about solar, wind, EVs and storage. The number worth carrying into a planning meeting is the 40%: opposition that reaches the ordinance stage kills the project two times in five. The number worth carrying into a strategy meeting is the other one — the report finds early evidence that the adoption rate is slowing in states that passed streamlined siting laws. Venue reform is the only intervention in this dataset with a measurable effect.
  • Thirty-Five Hundred Acres, Four to One: the Louisa County Board of Adjustment voted 4-1 on Sept. 2 to deny a special use permit for Ranger Power's Southpaw Solar — roughly 3,500 acres in southeast Iowa, up to 450 megawatts of generation and storage, and what the developer describes as a potential $1.2 billion investment in the county. The stated objections were farmland conversion and proximity to homes, and resident Katie Lawrence named productive farmland as the central concern. Southpaw says it remains committed to working with county leaders, landowners and residents as it considers next steps. Note what the fiscal case did not do: a billion-dollar number in a county of 11,000 did not move a single additional vote, because the question in front of the board was not what the project was worth but what it displaced.
  • Same Board, Opposite Answer: Pinal County supervisors approved NextEra's Eloy Valley Energy Center III on Sept. 2 with no supervisor voting against — an industrial rezoning and planned area development overlay for 400 megawatts of solar plus a 400-MW, four-hour battery system on about 1,263 acres south of Eloy, serving Electrical Districts 2, 3 and 4 and Trico Electric Cooperative, with construction possible in late 2026 and power as early as December 2027. The record was 29 letters of support against 13 in opposition, from horse breeders worried about heat, dust, noise and water and from residents objecting to cumulative rural land conversion. This is the same board that, a week earlier, rejected a data campus 4-1 after that applicant cut itself 80% and volunteered 22 conditions. Generation with a named local offtaker cleared. Load without one did not.
  • Thirty-Eight Cents a Watt: the Section 232 polysilicon action signed Aug. 6 sets minimum import prices of $0.38 per watt on modules and $0.22 per watt on cells, plus $21/kg on polysilicon and $100/kg on ingots and wafers, and a 15% ad valorem tariff on downstream derivatives with country differentiation. It takes effect Dec. 4 and replaces the Section 201 safeguard that expired in February, reaching further upstream than anything before it. Price floors are not tariffs: they set a hard bottom on module cost regardless of world price, which turns every 2027 PPA under negotiation into a different conversation and hands every "this project only pencils with cheap Chinese panels" opponent a talking point with a federal citation behind it.
  • Six More Months: Dominion pushed final turbine installation at the 2.6-GW Coastal Virginia Offshore Wind project from June 2027 to the end of 2027. The project is past 81% complete and at least $228 million over estimate, and the company attributes the slip to its own installation vessel, weather and loadout contingencies at Portsmouth, longer jacking periods at some turbine locations, and the stop-work order issued late last year — on top of the $800 million-plus in tariff costs already booked. The largest US offshore array is still going to finish. It is finishing eighteen months into a permitting environment designed to stop it, which is the only durability argument the sector currently has.
  • Through the End of the Year: Phillips County commissioners unanimously extended three separate moratoriums to Dec. 31 on Colorado's eastern plains — one on wind permits, one on utility-scale solar and battery storage applications in the unincorporated county, one on data centers — to keep drafting regulations. Three parallel pauses adopted in one motion is the tell: the county is not making a judgment about any of the three technologies, it is admitting it has no code that can process any of them. That is a drafting problem with a deadline, and it is a place where a developer with model ordinance language is more useful than a developer with a benefits package.
  • Catlin Opens the Door: the village board in Catlin, Illinois adopted a wind and solar ordinance on Sept. 1, clearing the way for special-use applications including Earthrise Energy's proposed 1,400-acre solar project along Catlin-Tilton Road. The ordinance sets out the permit and public-hearing process and requires a detailed site plan covering setbacks, maintenance, noise, shadow flicker, wildlife and environmental impact studies. Worth watching as the counterexample to the moratorium items above: a jurisdiction that wrote the rules first now has a project application to process rather than a fight to hold off.
On the Frontlines
  • MIThirty Days to Answer: Ranger Power is moving the 375-MW Shipyard Solar project spanning Wexford and Grand Traverse counties toward Michigan Public Service Commission review under Public Act 233, the 2023 law that lets large renewable projects bypass local boards — Grant Township hired outside counsel on Sept. 1 and said it has 30 days to respond to the developer's notice, after the Wexford Joint Planning Commission voted 8-0 against the zoning amendment that would have allowed the use locally. Grand Traverse County commissioners are separately pressing for a local voice in a state-run proceeding. The township hired an attorney who has already litigated a Ranger Power case at the MPSC, which is a signal about how organized the counterparty is before the state case even opens.
  • NMThe Word Nobody Should Say First: at PNM and Pattern Energy's Aug. 24 open house on the roughly 100-mile Mid-State 345-kV project, Torrance County residents pushed until a company representative acknowledged PNM would pursue eminent domain against landowners in certain circumstances. Loma Parda residents including Linda Filippi and B Street Market owner Ann McCloud objected to widening the community's existing 50-foot right-of-way to 200 feet; Manzano Land Grant Association president Lenora Lovato Romero stated outright opposition. PNM plans to file three applications with the PRC around mid-September to early October — a right-of-way width determination, a location approval and a CCN. Conceding condemnation at an open house, weeks before the filing, is a quote that will now appear in every intervention.
  • OKA Pass Through: OK Transco filed the Delaware-to-Monett route for NEOTEP East with the Corporation Commission on Aug. 21 — a little over 100 miles of new 345-kV line through Ottawa, Craig, Nowata and Washington counties, on poles or lattice towers 100 to 135 feet tall in a right-of-way about 150 feet wide. By Sept. 5, affected landowners were in the local press asking why the project isn't following existing roads and easements, and framing it as infrastructure Oklahoma hosts without benefiting from. "A pass through" is a two-word argument that survives cross-examination, and the in-state benefit case has to be made before it lands, not after.
  • COEasement, Crossed: Bent County commissioners approved a special use permit on July 29 for Grid United's Three Corners Connector — a 300-mile HVDC line up to 525 kV — and the Southern Plains Land Trust has now responded publicly. The route crosses roughly 5.8 miles of the trust's Heartland Ranch through a 500-foot construction corridor narrowing to a permanent 200-foot right-of-way, affecting about 492 acres under conservation easement and fragmenting a corridor supporting 12 species of greatest conservation need. The trust's position is the structural problem: it says it has no legal authority to consent to the crossing even if it wanted to, and would face suit from the easement holder if it tried. A county permit cannot clear an encumbrance the county did not grant.
  • ILTwenty-Two Days: Illinois counties have until Sept. 29 to conform their ordinances to the statewide wind and solar standards amended June 1, which for the first time also set state standards for battery storage — and Whiteside County has noticed a zoning hearing to amend its wind and solar sections and add a new BESS section outright. Expect a wave of these hearings statewide in the next three weeks, most of them lightly attended, all of them writing the setbacks and standards that will govern every application filed for the next several years. A county text amendment hearing in September is worth more of our attention than a project hearing in December.
  • NYAppellate Division: the Alliance to Preserve the Finger Lakes is suing the state over the Agricola Wind permit, filing in the Appellate Division against ORES's approval of Liberty Renewables' 24-turbine, 99-MW project in the Cayuga County towns of Scipio and Venice. The group's arguments are farmland conversion and a decommissioning plan that would leave parts of turbine foundations buried; State Sen. Tom O'Mara has amplified the transparency complaint against the agency. Consolidated state siting removes the local veto and relocates the fight to the courthouse — the venue changes, the timeline stretches, and the opposition's argument gets more technical, not less.
  • CABackcountry Backlash: San Diego County is drafting a battery-storage ordinance for its unincorporated communities and took it to an information session, where East County residents pressed on permitting process, fire risk and environmental impact — the same objections the county has heard for years as solar, wind and storage have gone into the backcountry over local protest. The county is trying to answer those objections with a code rather than project by project, which is the right instinct and roughly two development cycles late.
  • IACertificate Versus Zoning: Cerro Gordo County's petition for judicial review against the Iowa Utilities Commission is pending in Polk County District Court, challenging the generating certificate the IUC issued to Ranger Power subsidiary River City Energy for a 500-MW, roughly 3,000-acre solar project without requiring compliance with county zoning. The county argues the commission departed from its own precedent, that it never actually denied a zoning permit to a generating project, and that the record should have compared solar against gas, wind, nuclear and coal on land use. Read alongside the Louisa denial above: Iowa now has a local no and a state yes running simultaneously, and the appellate answer will set the venue question for every project in the state.
  • WINo Instrument to Do It With: the Rock County Planning and Development Committee unanimously rejected a proposed 12-month countywide data center moratorium and narrowly rejected a companion 13-member study panel, after corporation counsel advised that the only lawful path would be for the county to write general zoning and land-use rules that individual towns then adopt. Committee chair RJ Sutterlin's stated concern was insurance: an unlawful land-use provision could leave the county uncovered for a loss. The moratorium wave keeps colliding with the same wall — most counties lack the zoning authority the moratorium presumes.
  • SCThird Reading, Third Time: Spartanburg County Council pushed the third and final reading of its 12-month data center moratorium to Sept. 21 before a full chamber of frustrated residents. First reading passed in June and already holds new applications in abeyance, with a carve-out for a previously approved project at the former Kohler plant on South Pine Street. The abeyance clause is the part to note: the ordinance has been functionally in effect since June regardless of whether it ever gets a third reading.
  • UTEighty-Six to One: of the 86 bills introduced this year that would have restricted solar, wind and storage siting, exactly one became law — a scaled-back Utah measure limiting state tax credits for large solar on certain farmland — out of more than 200 siting bills tracked across 40-plus states by the Siting Solutions Project, with only eight legislatures still in session. That is a sharp reversal from 2025, when comparable bills passed routinely. The restriction pipeline has moved back down to the county level, which is where the Sabin Center's 865 local ordinances came from and where our week is actually spent.

Five Ohio counties voted against a transmission line that has no route. A New Mexico utility said "eminent domain" out loud three weeks before its first filing. An Oklahoma opposition had a two-word frame in the papers fifteen days after the docket opened. In all three, the record that will decide the case was written before the case existed. If our first scheduled contact on a linear project is the pre-application open house, we are showing up to respond to resolutions that are already on the books — so this week, pull the route-study calendar for every corridor project we touch and mark the county commission meetings, not the filing dates.
