Must Read

Make Them Say Why

The most consequential thing happening to Virginia solar right now isn't a single vote — it's a change in what a county's "no" is made of. As Cardinal News framed it in a weekend analysis of the collision between the state's energy goals and its localities, Virginia's answer to years of solar fights has been to require localities to explain to the state why they turn a solar project down. On paper it reads like a transparency measure. In the room it is something else entirely, and the counties know it: "Some rural localities fear that's simply creating a record that will ultimately be used to justify state intervention on siting decisions — and they're probably not wrong."

Start with why the pressure exists. The Clean Economy Act commits Dominion to a carbon-free grid by 2045 and Appalachian Power by 2050, and that mandate — layered on top of surging data-center demand — pushed "a wave of solar development across rural Virginia." Now the politics have caught up. As Cardinal put it, "we're now bumping up against the political limits of solar power, as some counties feel they've done enough already and others don't want it at all." The tell is in the vocabulary: "if you hear someone talk about 'industrial solar,' that almost certainly means they're against it." The law was written by metro-area legislators to hit a statewide climate target; it is enforced, parcel by parcel, by rural county boards whose worldview runs the other direction — "local governments feel themselves responsible for local land-use decisions, not state energy policy." The solar-denial reporting requirement sits squarely on that fault line.

Here's why it matters for how our clients win. When a county has to file findings for a denial, the denial stops being a shrug and becomes a document — one that can be read two ways. A board that says no on a thin, grievance-driven record has just handed the state (and a developer's lawyers) the raw material to argue the "no" was arbitrary and the project met the standards. A board that builds a real record — location, drainage, buffers, fire and EMS coordination, decommissioning — has produced a defensible decision that holds up in Richmond or in court. That is the entire ballgame for community engagement: the work isn't persuading a board to like solar, it's making sure that whatever the board decides, the record supports approval on the merits rather than a reversible objection. The reporting rule quietly turns every hearing into a paper trail, and the developer who shows up having already answered each standard in writing is the one whose project reads as compliant when the state looks back at the file.

And watch the second track, because it is about to tangle with the first. The same week, state Sen. Glen Sturtevant, a Chesterfield Republican, called on Gov. Abigail Spanberger to issue an executive order freezing all new data-center approvals, arguing that "these statewide consequences cannot be adequately addressed through a series of isolated local decisions." His case leans on JLARC's finding that data-center growth is the primary reason Virginia's electricity demand could double within a decade, that a typical Dominion residential customer could pay $14 to $37 more a month because of it, and that a typical 250,000-square-foot data center employs only about 50 full-time workers, roughly half of them contractors. The through-line to solar is the one Cardinal names directly: some rural counties chase data-center tax revenue while blocking the solar that would help power it, and "at some point, the state may step in and say if you take one, you have to do the other." If Virginia links the two land uses, the community-engagement problem our clients face on a solar farm and the one they face on a data center stop being separate files.

The Takeaway

In Virginia, a county's "no" is now a document the state can use — so the record isn't a formality anymore, it's the whole case. Three instructions follow. One: build a denial-proof record before the vote, not after. The reporting requirement means every hearing generates findings; the project that survives is the one whose file already answers each standard — siting, drainage, buffers, fire and EMS coordination, decommissioning bond — so a county that wants to say no has to manufacture a reason rather than cite one. Two: don't count on the override, and don't fear it either. A thin denial is now appealable evidence, but a well-built local approval is also the thing that keeps the state out of it; either way the leverage sits in the quality of the record, which is exactly the work we do. Three: treat the data-center moratorium talk as a solar story too. If Sturtevant's "decide whether more are justified" framing gains traction and the state ties data centers to solar, our clients' two hardest rooms become one linked engagement — and the counties chasing server-hall tax revenue while blocking solar are the first place that knot gets pulled tight.

Also On Our Radar
  • Bring the Fire Marshal, Texas Edition: proof the product still gets built where the safety work is done up front. Ørsted put its 250-MW/500-MWh Old 300 battery into operation in Needville, Texas, fully integrated into the ERCOT grid as of Aug. 5. Three details worth stealing for a hostile board. First, it's co-located — Old 300 Storage sits alongside the 430-MW Old 300 Solar farm (operating since 2024 under a Microsoft PPA) but is "operated independently," the answer to neighbors who conflate the panels and the cells. Second, the numbers: Ørsted says the combined solar-and-storage site will deliver roughly $110 million in property-tax revenue for local infrastructure, schools and emergency services. Third — and this is the one that decided rooms all summer — Ørsted "collaborated with the Needville Fire Department to provide education on the latest BESS technology and safety response protocols." Co-located storage, real tax dollars, and a first-responder partnership before the trucks roll: that's the template, and it just closed in ERCOT.
  • Queue the Good News: the backlog that strands our clients' projects just got a federal green light to move. On Friday, July 31, the D.C. Circuit Court of Appeals upheld FERC Order No. 2023, the interconnection-queue overhaul that grid operators and utilities had challenged. The reform scraps first-come, first-served for cluster studies that award connection based on readiness, adds transparency and survey requirements, and raises the financial commitment to enter and stay in the queue. The stakes are our clients' pipeline: there was roughly 2,600 GW of capacity waiting in U.S. interconnection queues at the end of 2023, "almost all of which were solar PV, wind power and energy storage projects." The utilities argued FERC's late-study fines overstepped; the three-judge panel disagreed. Earthjustice's attorney called it "a win for ratepayers, competition, and the environment," and for once that's not spin — the single biggest bottleneck between a signed lease and a built project just survived its biggest legal test.
  • Batteries, by the Billion: a signal of where the storage capital is pooling. Austin's Base Power raised a $1 billion Series D at a $13 billion valuation and launched a home battery it now builds in Texas. It's a distributed-fleet play, not a utility-scale one, but the read-across matters: even as standalone utility-scale storage costs tick up on lithium-ion tariffs, megacap money is racing into the residential and behind-the-meter side of the same technology. For any client weighing where storage siting fights go next, watch the distributed model — thousands of small installs aggregated into a virtual plant draw a very different (and quieter) permitting profile than one 500-MWh site at a folding-table hearing.

This is the same Botetourt where anti-solar and anti-data-center yard signs are multiplying alongside a Google data center headed for the county business park — so the where-is-solar-allowed map being redrawn tonight is the live version of the state-versus-local collision in this issue's Must Read.   • VASpotsylvania Votes on the Freeze Tomorrow: the data-center-moratorium politics reach a live vote. At Tuesday's (Aug. 11) meeting, Supervisor Chris Yakabouski will put forward a resolution backing state Sen. Glen Sturtevant's call for a statewide data-center moratorium, and Supervisor David Goosman has said he'll support it. Spotsylvania already tightened the screws in February — requiring a special-use permit for every data center and barring the use of drinking water or wells for cooling — and now has eight approved data centers, eleven pending applications, and, notably, none approved this year. The renewables angle is the one Goosman says out loud: the county "can't restrict them based on power usage," so a moratorium is the blunt instrument left. If a Virginia locality formally asks Richmond to freeze data centers, it strengthens the "take one, take the other" logic that could rope solar into the same statewide bargain — track it.
