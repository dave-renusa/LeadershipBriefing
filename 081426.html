Friday morning, team.

Two things happened in Washington this week that our clients will feel in a substation, not a headline. On Monday a federal judge granted the administration's request to send the Atlantic Shores offshore-wind approval back to the agency that issued it, staying the case while the Bureau of Ocean Energy Management reconsiders a permit both sides now concede is built on an incomplete record. A day later the Department of Energy canceled the three National Interest Electric Transmission Corridors the prior administration had proposed to speed the wires that renewables ride on. Neither move stops a single local project. Both quietly remove a federal backstop the industry had been counting on — the offshore approval that was supposed to be final, and the transmission fast-track that was supposed to route around the states. When the top of the stack gets less certain, the bottom of the stack — the county room — gets more important, not less. Radar has both.

And the county room kept doing what it does. In one Chautauqua County, New York town, the board passed four moratoriums in a single motion — wind, storage, green hydrogen, and data centers — then went looking for residents to staff the committee that will write the permanent rules. In Quincy, Illinois, a city council spent a night tangled over two solar permits before being told, on the floor, that if they voted no they would have to say which condition the project failed. And in Botetourt, Virginia — the county we flagged Monday before last — the planning commission took up its solar ordinance and declined to advance it, punting to September and asking for more time on the clock. Project Updates has New York; the two Plays have the tactics out of Illinois and Virginia, because both are worth stealing.

Also On Our Radar
  • The Approval That Wasn't Final: the offshore setback that tells every developer a federal sign-off is a floor, not a lock. On Monday, Aug. 10, U.S. District Judge Jia Cobb granted the government's motion for a voluntary remand of the Construction and Operations Plan approval for Atlantic Shores, the ~2.8-GW project off the New Jersey coast, and stayed the litigation while BOEM takes another look. Crucially, the judge did not vacate the approval — she sent it back, reasoning that agencies should be allowed to "cure their own mistakes rather than wasting the courts' and the parties' resources reviewing a record that both sides acknowledge to be incorrect or incomplete." For an onshore solar-and-storage shop that's a distant fight, but the principle travels: a permit is only as durable as the record under it, and a thin or contested record invites reconsideration long after the vote. The projects that survive changes in administration are the ones whose files were airtight the day they were signed.
  • Corridors, Canceled: the federal fast-track for renewable transmission just went away. On Wednesday, Aug. 12, DOE halted the designation of the three National Interest Electric Transmission Corridors the prior administration had proposed — the tool meant to cut interconnection costs, boost reliability, and, in the department's earlier framing, enable the clean-energy transition. Killing the corridors doesn't kill any specific line, but it removes the mechanism that would have let favored transmission projects claim federal siting and financing leverage over state and local objections. Translation for us: the wires that carry solar and wind to market will keep getting built the hard way — corridor by corridor, county by county, easement by easement — with no Washington override waiting in the wings. The transmission bottleneck our clients' projects sit behind just lost its shortcut, which makes the local land-use work on every line that much more of the whole game.

  • NYFour Pauses, One Motion: a clean example of the omnibus moratorium — the "pause everything at once" move that's spreading across New York's town boards. The Town of Carroll board (Chautauqua County) unanimously approved moratoriums on battery energy storage, large wind, green hydrogen, and data-center development in a single vote at its latest meeting. The tell for anyone with a parcel in the county is what came next: Supervisor Tim Burkett said he's now recruiting residents who understand — or are willing to learn about — these technologies to form a committee that will recommend the permanent ordinances. That committee is where the setbacks, decommissioning terms, and fire standards our clients will have to meet actually get drafted, and it's being staffed right now by whoever shows up. A town that pauses four land uses at once and then opens the pen to volunteers is a town where early, credible engagement is worth more than any filing made after the rules harden.

Plays of the Week
  • Quincy (IL) City CouncilMake Them Name the Condition: a procedural play worth keeping in the binder — and this time the pro-approval side ran it. At Monday's Quincy City Council meeting, two solar facilities proposed on rural-zoned land near the city needed the tougher "special permit for planned development," and the room got tangled: the first request failed on a 7-7 vote to concur with the Plan Commission, even though the commission had recommended it overwhelmingly. Here's the move. Before the no votes could harden into a denial, an alderman asked — though the rules didn't require it — whether any of the members voting no would name the specific condition they believed the project failed to meet. The city attorney had already warned that going against the Plan Commission could force the council to justify that reversal, "potentially even in court." Faced with having to put a reason on the record, two aldermen flipped on reconsideration, and both projects advance to first reading Aug. 17. The lesson for a developer standing in that room: a "no" with no stated basis is the weakest kind of no, and simply asking opponents to name the unmet standard — out loud, on the record — can turn a reflexive rejection into a defensible approval. Make them say why, and the vote often changes.
  • Botetourt (VA) OppositionTurn the Water Line Into the Data Center: a study in how organized opponents widen a fight — and how a commission uses the clock as a soft moratorium. At Botetourt's Aug. 10 planning meeting, commissioners took up the county's proposed solar-ordinance rewrite and declined to advance it, scheduling a dedicated solar review for September and moving to extend the decision timeline past the November deadline — the "we need more time to get it right" posture that functions, in practice, as a pause without ever using the word. The tactic to study, though, was in the room next door on the agenda: a routine Western Virginia Water Authority request for a 3,800-foot water line to serve 40-odd households drew a packed, distrustful crowd because opponents reframed it as infrastructure for a proposed Google data center — never mind the authority's insistence that the line runs the other direction. The commission deadlocked 2-2. The transferable insight cuts both ways for our clients: opposition energy is portable, and a well-organized group will attach an unrelated, sympathetic-sounding project to the thing it actually fears to seed doubt and pack a hearing. The counter isn't to argue the map after the fact — it's to establish trust and a factual record before the crowd arrives, because once "this is really for the data center" takes hold, no engineering diagram unwinds it.

Washington pulled two supports this week — the offshore approval that wasn't final and the transmission fast-track that's now gone — and the message underneath both is the same one the county rooms keep sending: the record is the only thing that holds. Build it before the vote, name the reasons on the floor, and get in front of the crowd before someone else tells them what your project is really for.
