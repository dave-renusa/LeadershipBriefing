RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 51 · Monday, September 14, 2026

Happy Monday, all.

Take the decision away from the local board and the opposition does not go home — it goes shopping for a venue. In Michigan, where Public Act 233 moved large renewable siting to the state, a township is now suing a developer in federal court over the statute itself and three township officials face recall petitions for the crime of voting yes on a battery ordinance. In Ohio, where the Power Siting Board holds the pen, residents in eighteen communities put data centers on the November 3 ballot by charter amendment. In California, the city of Imperial and the Sierra Club skipped the county board entirely and won a CEQA ruling that voids a million-square-foot approval. In Oregon, opponents did not argue impacts at all — they forced a zoning-verification determination and let the code do the work. And in West Virginia, the fight is inside the agency: a PSC engineer filed testimony telling his own commission to deny a $1.16 billion 500-kV line in full. Five states, five different instruments, one pattern: preemption relocates the argument, it does not settle it.

Must Read

The Recall Is the New Public Comment

About a hundred people packed the Drenthe Grove Dozeman Center in Zeeland Township on Thursday night, and more than two dozen of them got up to speak against RWE's Silver Maple Solar Farm — a $300 million, 200-megawatt array across roughly 1,900 acres of agricultural-zoned land in eastern Zeeland and western Jamestown townships, Ottawa County. RWE says it would power 34,000 homes and deliver $31.6 million in local government revenue over its life. Development manager Joe Brochu told the room the company's Michigan Public Service Commission application is paused while it completes additional outreach, that this was the first of two required public meetings, and that RWE plans to reapply "within the next month or so," targeting construction in fall 2027 and commercial operation in mid-2029.

That is the ordinary part. Here is the rest of the file.

RWE sued members of the Smallegan family in June over a lease covering roughly 30% of the project land. At the microphone Thursday, James Smallegan asked Brochu directly: "are you going to be in court next Friday with us, since your company's suing us?" Zeeland Township, for its part, sued RWE Americas in July arguing that Public Act 233 — the 2023 law that lets large renewable projects bypass local boards and go straight to the MPSC — is unconstitutional. RWE removed that case to the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Michigan in August; the township has moved to send it back. Township Supervisor Kerri Bosma put the stakes in one line: the fight "is bigger than solar. This is about local control and decision making."

Ninety miles northeast, in Ingersoll Township, Midland County, the instrument is a ballot rather than a docket. New recall petitions against Supervisor Kim Heisler, Treasurer Jim Terwillegar and Trustee Ron Garrett cleared their appeal window on Sept. 8, and Midland County Elections Director Ann Manary confirmed signature collection could begin Sept. 9. The stated ground for recall is their votes approving Ordinance 78 — the township's battery energy storage systems ordinance. Petitioner Alicia Marolf's first attempt failed certification over how circulators wrote their addresses; this one did not. The language stays valid 180 days, signatures must be gathered inside a 60-day window, and the threshold is valid signatures equal to at least 25% of township votes cast in the last gubernatorial election. The backdrop is DTE's proposed Poseyville Solar Park: the township planning commission denied the special-use permit in April, and DTE sued over the denial in June.

Read the two together and the shape is clear. Michigan did not eliminate the local fight when it moved siting authority to Lansing. It eliminated the local vote, which was the one instrument that produced a decision and ended the argument. What replaced it is slower, more expensive and harder to negotiate with: a constitutional challenge in federal court, a recall campaign against officials who voted for a storage ordinance rather than against a project, and a developer as plaintiff against its own landowners in the middle of a public outreach process it is legally required to run. None of those venues has a host-community agreement on the table. None of them can be closed by improving the project.

The tell is Ingersoll. Those three officials did not approve a battery project. They approved the rules for one — the kind of proactive code-writing that every developer asks townships to do, and that every playbook calls the constructive path. They are now facing recall for it. If ordinance adoption becomes personally risky for township officials, the supply of jurisdictions willing to write a code before an application lands drops, and the moratorium becomes the safe political choice by default.

The Takeaway

On every PA 233 project — and on the equivalent state-siting pathways in New York, Illinois and California — we should be running a second track alongside the state application, aimed at the venues preemption creates rather than the one it closed. Three practical moves. First, litigation posture is community posture now: a developer that is suing landowners cannot credibly run an outreach meeting in the same month, and the sequencing of those two things is a decision somebody is making without reference to the other. Second, when a township writes an ordinance at our request, the officials who vote for it are exposed, and they should know going in what that looks like and have air cover — a record, a factual answer to the fire-safety question, a named third party willing to speak. Third, the state application is the floor, not the goal. Zeeland Township's answer to losing the zoning vote was to attack the statute; assume that response is now standard and build the record accordingly.

Also On Our Radar
  • Two Hundred Thirty-Four Percent: the Commerce Department issued final affirmative antidumping and countervailing determinations on crystalline silicon cells and modules from India, Indonesia and Laos, closing the Commerce phase of the Solar IV cases. Indian producers face a 123.04% dumping margin plus a 126.09% countervailing rate; Indonesian exporters face 94.36% dumping with CVD rates from 73.2% to 173.7%; Laos exporters face 65.43% dumping with CVD from 82.03% to 153.67% — combined margins as high as 234%, 178% and 103%. These are case-specific orders under the Tariff Act of 1930 and stack on top of the existing executive tariffs rather than replacing them. The ITC's final injury vote is Oct. 14, with duty orders issuing Nov. 2 if affirmative; a negative finding terminates the cases and refunds deposits. Cell sourcing has already moved on to South Korea, the Philippines, Kenya, Nigeria and Ethiopia. Every 2027 PPA still in negotiation now has two federal cost floors under it — the Section 232 minimum import prices and this — and neither one moves when world prices do.
  • Twenty-One, Then Eighteen: EIA's Short-Term Energy Outlook projects U.S. solar generation grows 21% in 2026 and 18% in 2027, with wind up 7% and 5%, against total electricity consumption rising 2% in each year to a record 4,211 billion kWh in 2027. Coal generation falls 8% then 6%. The commercial sector drives 63% of the 2026 sales increase and 56% of the 2027 increase. Two regional numbers matter for siting: PJM accounts for nearly 45% of total U.S. generation growth across the forecast, and the solar growth is concentrated in ERCOT and MISO — an added 18 billion kWh in ERCOT and 13 billion in MISO this year, then 20 billion and 11 billion next year. The build is going where the queue moves fastest, not where the load is loudest.
  • Sloopy Runs Out of Innings: the Ohio Power Siting Board denied Invenergy's application for rehearing on its rejection of the Sloopy Solar project — 108 MW across nearly 1,900 acres in Harmony Township, Clark County. The board's original June decision found the project did not meet all statutory criteria, citing overwhelming public opposition; Clark County commissioners had passed a formal resolution against it. The only remaining avenue is an appeal to the Ohio Supreme Court. Ohio's siting board has now built a multi-year record of treating organized local objection as dispositive on the "public interest, convenience and necessity" prong, and a rehearing denial is the board declining to soften it. On any Ohio file, the county resolution is not a political problem to be managed around — it is a finding of fact in the making.
  • Ninety-Seven Percent Forest: the Massachusetts Supreme Judicial Court ruled that the Petersham Zoning Board of Appeals improperly denied a special permit to Sunpin Energy Services and sent the application back for further review. The project would clear about 14.3 acres of mostly wooded land on New Athol Road for a 4.3-MW array and a 2-MW battery. The town's solar overlay district, where large-scale solar needs no special permit, covers 5.16 acres. The 2021 denial turned on one vote — member Maryanne Reynolds objected to the extent of tree clearing, and the board required unanimity. The court's reasoning is the transferable part: because 97% of Petersham is forested, denying a project on generalized tree-clearing grounds would effectively bar large-scale solar townwide, though towns may still weigh forest preservation when the concerns are tied to the specific site. Sunpin lost in Land Court in 2023 and won at the Appeals Court in 2025. Five years, three courts, 4.3 megawatts.
  • Staff Says Deny: West Virginia PSC technical analyst Daniel Egnatoff filed direct testimony recommending the commission deny NextEra's MidAtlantic Resiliency Link application in full, concluding the grid analysis supporting the $1.16 billion, 107.5-mile 500-kV line was "outdated before the application was filed" and answering "No" on whether it carries material benefits for West Virginia. His stated view of the alternative: "the most likely outcome of the MARL Project not being built is the energizing of fewer data centers in Virginia." Egnatoff visited roughly 80 properties along the 58.9-mile in-state route through Monongalia, Preston, Mineral and Hampshire counties and reported landowners who felt "trapped"; despite route revisions, one home still sits inside the right-of-way between mileposts 104.2 and 104.3. Cathy Kunkel, for West Virginians Against Transmission Injustice, put the cost to state ratepayers at more than $210 million through construction and the first 15 years. A staff recommendation is not an order, but it is the document intervenors will quote in every other state where this line is pending.
  • From Ten Years to One: USDA's proposed overhaul of the Agricultural Foreign Investment Disclosure Act would pull utility-scale solar explicitly into the regime and cut the leasehold reporting exemption from ten years to one, meaning virtually every multi-decade ground lease, option period and easement involving foreign capital or international fund management triggers a mandatory filing. Per a Norton Rose Fulbright analysis, foreign entities holding as little as 5% to 10% interest must disclose beneficial ownership through the full capital stack, and filers must submit open-source geospatial files with exact parcel boundaries identifying arrays, inverter blocks and access roads. USDA would replace its flat 0.1%-per-week penalty cap with a schedule scaling to 2.5% of land fair market value for every seven days of non-compliance. Comments have closed. The community-engagement consequence is the geospatial piece: parcel-level boundaries for every leased tract, published in an open format, is an opposition research product the government would be building and distributing for free.
  • Seven Point Three, Expedited: MISO opened the fifth cycle of its Expedited Resource Addition Study with 15 projects totaling roughly 7.3 GW across its North, Central and South regions — a mix of solar, wind, battery storage and gas, all targeted online by 2029. Since ERAS launched in 2025 the pipeline has grown to 58 projects and about 29 GW, of which 38 have completed generator interconnection agreements. Aubrey Johnson, MISO's vice president of system planning and competitive transmission, framed it as bringing "reliability-critical generation online faster while we work toward long-term improvements in the interconnection process." The constraint worth tracking: the program is capped at 68 projects and sunsets in August 2027. Ten slots left, eleven months, and the eligibility screen is reliability need — which means the siting conversation for an ERAS project starts with a compressed calendar and no queue excuse.
Project Updates
  • KYEight to Seven, Second Reading: the Lexington-Fayette Urban County Council voted 8-7 on Sept. 10 to allow intermediate and large-scale solar as a conditional use in Agricultural-Rural and Agricultural-Urban zones, reviewed by the Board of Adjustment. Conditions require vegetative ground cover and a land management plan, Stormwater Manual compliance, maintained agricultural production subject to annual review, and a conservation and water-quality plan approved by the Fayette County Conservation District. The ordinance caps large-scale ground-mounted solar in agricultural zones at 1% of county land — roughly 1,800 acres — and allows ground-mounted solar in any zone if sited entirely on brownfields. Mayor Linda Gorton has 10 days to approve or veto, and an override needs three-fifths of the 15-member council. A one-vote margin with a veto window open is not an approval yet.
  • CASixteen Thousand Acres, Countywide: the Santa Barbara County Board of Supervisors passed first reading 4-1 on a policy package setting new standards for solar and battery storage — a 50-foot setback between projects of a half-acre or more and sensitive receptors such as school campuses and residential neighborhoods, and a 16,000-acre countywide cap on large-scale solar. Fourth District Supervisor Bob Nelson was the lone no, wanting a lower cap split between North and South County: "I think we're going to get 'solar-ed' over." Planning and Development Deputy Director Alex Tuttle confirmed clarifying text was added so the sensitive-receptor rule would not trigger on household battery packs. Grower-Shipper Association President Claire Wineman asked for an 8,000-acre cap. Second and final reading is Sept. 22.
  • COTwenty-Five Thousand Eight Hundred, Depending Who Counts: a joint public hearing before the Weld County Planning Commission and Board of County Commissioners is set for Sept. 23 on Chalk Bluffs Energy — 380 MWac across two solar facilities generating 425 MWdc, two substations, two battery systems, 10.8 miles of 115-kV line and 15.9 miles of 230-kV line on agricultural land in northern Weld County. The company says 97% of the project land is under long-term private leases and projects $305 million in direct and indirect economic impact, including $183 million during a two-year construction period, plus $1.57 million in annual property tax against $10,100 today. Participating landowner Scott Salo says the project would use 18 acres of his ranch; the opposition website counts 25,800 affected acres and calls it "an industrial takeover of the High Plains." Same footprint, two numbers, and the larger one is winning the framing.
  • INNo Offtaker, No Project: the Jay County Council terminated its tax abatement with Hodson Energy on Wednesday after county attorney Wes Schemenaur received an email saying the company would not proceed with the New Jay Solar project — 39 MW on 311 acres in Richland Township near Dunkirk, first pitched in December 2023. "They were not able to secure a buyer for their electricity," Schemenaur said. The abatement would have waived $4.4 million in property taxes over 10 years against $2.156 million in economic development payments. Voting to terminate were Matt Minnich, Cindy Bracy, Bryan Alexander and Faron Parr. Three other Jay County solar projects remain live — Scout Clean Energy's Sun Chief, which won commissioner approval the same day for a decommissioning bond of more than $5 million, Invenergy's Skycrest, and Leeward's Rose Gold.
  • WICounsel Says You Can't: the Columbia County Planning and Zoning Committee approved an amended resolution titled "Temporary Pause and Coordinated Review of Major Energy Infrastructure" at its Sept. 1 meeting, sending it to the full County Board on Sept. 16. Corporation counsel had already issued a six-page memo concluding the county "does not have the authority to adopt a blanket moratorium on solar and/or wind energy facilities or their related permits," warning of litigation exposure. Planning and Zoning Director Kurt Calkins told the committee developers routinely bypass county zoning once the state PSC approves a project, citing American Transmission Company's refusal to seek a county permit for a laydown yard. Eleven residents spoke, and committee member Tess Carr said she would file an ethics complaint against the resolution's sponsor. This is the third county in six weeks where the moratorium wave has hit the same wall: the authority the moratorium presumes does not exist.
  • ILA Multitude of Asks: the Sterling City Council voted unanimously Sept. 8 for a 180-day moratorium on processing applications for data centers and battery energy storage systems. Building and zoning superintendent Amanda Schmidt said the city has fielded "a multitude of asks" from BESS developers, including proposals to site facilities inside downtown buildings, and no data center inquiries at all. The Plan Commission meeting was moved to 6:30 p.m. Sept. 24 to take up both uses and possible comprehensive plan amendments. City planner Dustin Wolff's 23-page draft ordinance defines each use by size and capacity with water, sewer and electricity standards; City Attorney Tim Zollinger warned the framework "can't just be no" under Illinois zoning law and said electricity, not water, is the binding constraint. Note what triggered it — not a project, but a pattern of inquiries with no code to route them through.
  • NYFive Megawatts on Maxon Road: a 5-MW lithium-ion battery system goes to the Schenectady Planning Commission for site plan approval on Sept. 16, proposed by New Leaf Energy on Maxon Road Extension in the city's industrial zone beside the Freemans Road Bridge. Developer Tyler Krupa said the system would take 18 months to two years to reach operation, would not be paired with solar, and would absorb excess power and discharge at peak. The site falls squarely inside the city's large-scale BESS allowance under a zoning code adopted earlier this year and already connects to National Grid infrastructure. Contrast the same developer's experience in New Scotland, where three proposed systems have drawn organized resistance — Planning Board Chairman Jeffrey Baker called the noise and fire concerns legitimate but the rest "inchoate fear." A code written in advance is the difference between a site plan review and a two-year fight.
  • NJOnly on Our Land: the Egg Harbor Township Committee held a special meeting Thursday on battery storage ahead of the public hearing and final vote on its BESS zoning ordinance, set for Sept. 16. As introduced Aug. 12, the ordinance would permit battery storage only on township-owned property, at least 100 feet from any residential property, barred from environmentally sensitive lands, with a required fire safety and emergency response plan, controlled-access fencing, and mandatory decommissioning after a year out of service. The ordinance text states such facilities "may pose concerns related to fire safety, environmental impacts, proximity to residential uses, and long-term maintenance and decommissioning." Municipal-land-only is not a setback rule, it is a ban with a courtesy exception, and it is the most restrictive live BESS text we are tracking in the state.
  • PASheep First, Studies Later: the Mount Joy Township Zoning Hearing Board in Lancaster County opened hearings Sept. 9 on what would be among the area's first solar grazing operations — two separate applications from Abundant Solar Power for 3-MW arrays on farms owned by Clair Mummau and his son Kevin, with sheep grazing for vegetation management. The board heard only the first, for a 35-acre Schwanger Road farm, issued no decision, and continued to Oct. 7 for glare, noise and environmental studies; the second case was pushed to the same date after the hearing ran past two hours. Abundant told the board it would plant six-foot evergreens to block sight lines from neighboring properties and Route 283. A resident raised a recent Commonwealth Court decision from West Lampeter Township holding that an agrivoltaics operation failed the ordinance's requirement that non-farming activity be a secondary accessory use — which is now the controlling question in every Pennsylvania dual-use case.
  • VAThird Route, Same Month: Fluvanna County Attorney Dan Whitten told the Board of Supervisors Wednesday that Valley Link Transmission is expected to file its 115-mile, 765-kV Joshua Falls-Yeat application with the Virginia SCC "as early as this month." Whitten disclosed a third potential route through Fluvanna, a shift in one previously proposed route, and an increase in maximum tower height from about 160 feet to 175 feet since April. All three routes would require new 200-foot easements across an estimated 76 to 120 Fluvanna parcels, and none would follow an existing transmission easement despite the county's adopted policy favoring existing corridors. Fluvanna will participate as a formal party through outside counsel shared with Louisa, Goochland, Buckingham and Orange; Louisa and Buckingham have each authorized up to $250,000. Whitten flagged the leverage point: the Joshua Falls and Yeat substations each require local special-use permits the SCC could not override.
On the Frontlines
  • WAOne Hundred Sixteen and Counting Down: Scout Clean Energy filed another revised plan for the Horse Heaven Wind Farm with the Energy Facility Site Evaluation Council, cutting the project to 116 turbines — 30 fewer than its last plan and nearly half what it first pitched in 2024. Scout is relocating supporting infrastructure including the main transmission line to interconnect with Bonneville Power Administration's grid, and the revised layout now requires a high-voltage crossing above Interstate 82. The Yakama Nation, the Benton County commissioners and Tri-Cities C.A.R.E.S. sued over EFSEC's November 2024 approval; the case was argued before the Washington Supreme Court in mid-June and a ruling is pending. Attorney Kenneth Harper, for the county, urged EFSEC to halt further consideration, saying it "must engage with and acknowledge the procedural errors"; Yakama Tribal Council Chairman Gerald Lewis wrote that EFSEC "should decline to allow any version of the project to move forward" until cultural resource harms are mitigated. Three rounds of voluntary downsizing have not bought a single withdrawal of opposition.
  • NYNo Zoning, No Planning Board, No Say: Bedrock Renewables, through subsidiary Azalea Wind, wants 14 to 16 turbines across a 250-acre patchwork in eastern Catharine and Hector, Schuyler County — 100 MW, 600-foot turbines, construction targeted between 2028 and 2030. It would be the first major wind project in the heart of the Finger Lakes. Most of it sits in Hector, population about 4,900, which has no zoning law and no planning board after abandoning a draft zoning law in 2022 following five contentious years — so permit review falls entirely to the state. Supervisor Justin Boyette said the decision "is to some degree or to a large degree removed from our hands," and board member Anthony Mansfield called learning the turbine height "pretty shocking." Enfield residents who fought the cancelled Black Oak Wind Farm showed up to the Aug. 27 informational session to share their experience. A town with no code has no seat at the table and no mechanism to trade one for concessions.
  • TNNobody Will Say How Big It Is: Canary Media counted 720 Tesla Megapack containers at SpaceXAI's Colossus 2 campus in July satellite imagery — roughly 2.8 GWh — and on Aug. 20 a company energy developer told the TVA board the battery holds 3.3 GWh, "enough to power all of Memphis for two hours." The TVA board approved a direct grid hookup later that day. On Sept. 2, Memphis Light, Gas and Water CEO Doug McGowan called it "the world's largest grid-connected battery system," describing 2,000 MW of batteries behind the meter used as backup during curtailment. For scale, the largest known operating U.S. grid battery is Edwards & Sanborn in Kern County at more than 1,066 MW and 3,287 MWh. MLGW, TVA and SpaceXAI all declined to clarify the official size, and the project proceeded with no announced utility contract; regional storage executives first heard of it at the August board meeting. A gigawatt-scale battery arriving with no docket, no contract and no number is the siting precedent nobody in our business got to comment on.
  • TXSix Weeks to Three Hundred Twenty: SMT Energy and integrator FlexGen started operations at the 160-MW, 320-MWh Houston IV facility, which the companies say was commissioned in six weeks, "cutting the time of deployment by 3x from the original timeline." Irby Construction handled EPC; SMT secured $135 million for the project in February 2025 and broke ground with CenterPoint Energy in May. ERCOT set a new hourly peak load record of 91.1 GW on July 22 — 6% above the 2023 record — met with 48% gas and 32% solar. The number to carry into a 15-year procurement conversation is the six weeks: when a battery can be built inside a construction season, the community engagement window compresses to match, and the outreach plan has to be finished before the equipment ships.
  • WIA Statewide Candidate Picks a Side: Milwaukee County Executive and gubernatorial candidate David Crowley announced Sept. 10 that he opposes the BECI 765-kV project in the Driftless Region: "I oppose the proposed 765 line in its current form, and Wisconsin must fully consider less disruptive alternatives before moving forward," adding that "any project of this scale must demonstrate clear public benefits and community buy-in before regulators give it the green light." The Midcontinent Grid Solutions line would run from Crawford County to Walworth County and on to the Illinois border, crossing a nine-county study area on steel towers about 175 feet tall in roughly 180-foot easements. A county board resolution is a record. A gubernatorial candidate adopting the opposition's framing on a project with no application on file is a different order of problem, because it converts a routing dispute into a statewide campaign issue before the PSC ever opens a docket.
  • WVZinc on a Coal Mine: Google will buy the energy, capacity and clean attributes of MN8 Energy's Mammoth Solar project on a former coal mine site in Kanawha County — 86 MW of solar, 10 MW/100 MWh of zinc-based long-duration storage from Eos Energy Enterprises, and 70 MW/280 MWh of lithium-ion, inside PJM. Solar reaches commercial operation in 2028, storage in 2029 and 2030. The zinc piece is one of the largest deployments to date; most operational zinc installations are 1 MW or less. MN8 and Eos signed a supply agreement last October for up to 750 MWh of Eos Z3 modules. Two things travel from this: reclaimed mine land keeps clearing siting review faster than greenfield, and a non-lithium chemistry answers the fire-safety objection in the room before it is raised.
  • IDThe Ban Comes Off: Bannock County's three commissioners will vote this fall on an ordinance that would reverse the county's 2024 ban on utility-scale solar and wind and also allow nuclear; the draft is in public review. The original ban passed 2-1 in March 2024, with Commissioner Jeff Hough casting the lone no — Hough is now board chair and leads the repeal. Commissioner Ernie Moser, who backed the ban and retires at year's end, says his fears about solar and wind are "not as big" as two years ago. The ban stalled Balanced Rock Power's 300-MW Harmon Solar Project with its 1,200-MWh battery and drove Hecate Energy out of the county entirely after it had optioned roughly 400 acres. Bannock hosts the Populus substation serving Idaho, Utah, Wyoming and the Pacific Northwest. Bans are reversible, and the mechanism is turnover plus two years of nothing happening — worth tracking in every jurisdiction we have written off.
  • OHEighteen Ballots: voters in 18 Ohio municipalities and townships will decide data center measures on Nov. 3, after a Sept. 4 charter-amendment petition deadline. Nine — Conneaut, Defiance, Granville, Oregon, Pataskala, Piqua, Sunbury, Trenton, Upper Sandusky — mirror the statewide Conserve Ohio effort to ban facilities drawing over 25 MW a month; Urbana votes on a 7.5-MW threshold and Hubbard on a full ban. Fourteen of the 18 have received formal proposals. Two tactics are worth reading closely. Grove City organizers deliberately avoided a data-center-specific ban, citing litigation risk, and instead petitioned to subject any new heavy industrial project to a citywide vote — a broader frame that is harder to challenge and that would catch generation too. And in Piqua, organizer Alisha Lange stacked four instruments at once after Meta announced a project needing roughly 1,400 MW: a 25-MW ban, a full ban, recalls of the mayor and two commissioners, and a petition to void the utility agreement powering the facility.
  • CAPiecemeal, Prejudicial: Imperial County Superior Court Judge L. Brooks Anderholt ruled Wednesday that county officials illegally shielded a nearly million-square-foot data complex from CEQA review when they approved it in April: "the county prejudicially abused its discretion by approving the lot merger without first completing the CEQA review required for the whole project." The complex would include a battery system, a bank of natural gas generators and a new substation directly beside the Victoria Ranch neighborhood. The suits were brought by the city of Imperial and the Sierra Club; county lawyers never opposed the August tentative ruling. Developer Sebastian Rucci will seek a new trial and appeal, citing roughly $12 million in projected annual tax revenue. The transferable hook is the theory, not the outcome: challenging a lot merger as segmentation of the whole project is available against any phased approval, including ours.
  • ORE Is for Employment: the City of Eugene determined in a Sept. 8 letter that a data center proposed for the former Hynix plant is prohibited in the site's E-1 Campus Employment zone. The code does not list data centers but allows IT services including computer networking and technical support centers; the prospective buyer's attorney argued the project qualified. The city disagreed, writing that the "E" stands for employment and that data centers are "almost self-operating." The determination covers employment zones only, not industrial zones, and is appealable to the Land Use Board of Appeals. Separately, Gov. Tina Kotek directed state agencies to pause work on unapproved data center proposals involving state-owned land through July 1, 2027. The play: opponents never litigated impacts. They forced a zoning-verification determination and put the burden on the developer to prove the use was permitted, which turned low job counts from a talking point into the disqualifying fact.
  • KYPermanent, Not Temporary: the Woodford County Fiscal Court voted unanimously Tuesday on an ordinance permanently banning landfills, data centers and large battery storage systems countywide — the first permanent ban the Kentucky Association of Counties is aware of in the state. Judge-Executive James Kay said the court chose permanence over a moratorium after hearing universal resident opposition, and that no developer has pitched a specific project: "We are a tight-knit community with a finite, irreplaceable amount of land. Every single acre matters here." County seat Versailles passed a temporary moratorium in July. A National League of Cities tracker counts roughly a dozen local governments nationwide with permanent bans this year. Note the drafting: large battery storage is inside the same ordinance as landfills and data centers, which is how BESS keeps getting banned in jurisdictions that were never asked about it.
  • NATIONALEmergency, Defined Down: the D.C. Circuit vacated DOE's emergency order requiring Consumers Energy to delay retiring the 1,420-MW J.H. Campbell coal plant in Michigan, holding that Federal Power Act section 202(c) is "a limited backstop mechanism" and that DOE's reading "invites frequent federal interventions that are unsupported by the statute and threaten the stability of the energy market." The court noted the Michigan PSC and MISO had approved the retirement after extensive review, and that it is the states that bear responsibility for reliability planning. DOE has issued similar orders affecting six other plants, all but one coal-fired, and has reissued each before expiry; Consumers reported a net compliance cost of $259 million through June 30. It is the first of these challenges to reach a decision, and DOE could appeal to the Supreme Court. Every megawatt of coal held open past its retirement date is a megawatt of capacity our clients' projects were selected to replace.

A Michigan township is suing a developer over the constitutionality of the statute that took its vote away, and three officials in another township face recall for writing a battery ordinance. Eighteen Ohio communities put the question on a ballot. A California city sued its own county and won. Eugene answered with a zoning letter. Woodford County banned three uses permanently before anyone applied. The common thread is that none of these venues has a negotiation in it — you cannot improve a project into a favorable recall election. So the ask for this week is to map the second track on every active file: where does this fight go if the siting decision stops being local, who carries it there, and what is the last moment we can still be in a room with them.
