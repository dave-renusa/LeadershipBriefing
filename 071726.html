Friday morning, team.

The recess is still thinning the calendar, but the dockets that cleared our seven-day freshness bar this week rhyme with each other, and the rhyme is worth naming. Opposition has stopped trying to vote our projects down and started trying to zone them out by the numbers. In North Dakota, a county tripled its wind setback to a full mile — and the fight's own critics, including one of its commissioners, said the arithmetic "effectively kills" any future turbine in the county. In Louisiana and Michigan, two boards reached for the same tool from opposite ends of our sector: a pause button on new solar and battery permits while the lawyers rewrite the rulebook. No project was denied on the merits this week. The setback table and the moratorium clock did the work instead.

The good news sits in the mirror image. This week's sharpest wins came from actors who grabbed the terms first — a Virginia battery developer that over-built its setback nearly five times over and then went over the planning commission's head, and a Texas county opposition campaign that manufactured a "no-welcome mat" with no zoning power at all. Both are in Plays, and both are lessons in the same truth we sell: whoever writes the terms early wins the room late. One data-center approval landed in Indianapolis, too — proof the concede-before-the-vote move still clears a hostile board.

Standing items, so we don't re-litigate them. Loudoun's Golden-to-Mars monopoles: no new vote — the school board's July 20 deadline to volunteer an alternate corridor lands Monday. Texas's $33B STEP 765-kV pause: no update. New York's statewide data-center moratorium: still no signature or veto from Gov. Hochul. North Carolina's NCUC solar freeze: SELC's reconsideration petition still pending. Valley North's 765-kV open houses roll on next week — Harrison and Preston on July 21, Gilmer on July 22 — with no route chosen. Starlight Solar in San Diego heads to the County Board of Supervisors September 16; Comstock Township's storage information session in Michigan is July 23. The one that has moved — the WV PSC's Mon Power / Potomac Edison hearing — is now underway, and it's in Frontlines below. If any of the rest breaks, you get a full item.

Also On Our Radar
  • Ruled Out by the Mile: Williams County, North Dakota didn't ban wind — it just moved the fence line until the ban builds itself. On a 3-1 vote, commissioners tripled the required turbine setback from 1,400 feet to a full mile, and the numbers tell the story: the no-build buffer around a single home jumps from roughly 141 acres to about 2,010 — fourteen times larger. A retired attorney speaking against it put the developers' case for them, warning the change would "effectively kill any further wind power projects in Williams County," because a company will site a turbine somewhere easier before it fights through a variance to get near a willing landowner. The commissioners who prevailed flipped the burden on purpose — as one put it, the person who wants the project should do the footwork, not the neighbor who wants to stay out of it. That's the whole Midwest local-control playbook compressed into one ordinance line: you don't need to say no when the setback says it for you. The counter, same as ever, is to be in the room while the number is still soft — a mile is a policy choice made at a committee table, not a law of physics.
  • Parish Presses Pause: the Washington Parish, Louisiana Council voted unanimously to adopt a 90-day moratorium (Resolution 26-1506) on new commercial solar and battery-storage permits, buying its attorneys time to rewrite an outdated solar ordinance and to write, for the first time, any rules at all for storage — a gap the council admitted it currently has no language to fill. A packed special meeting ran the familiar litany: lithium-ion fire risk, emergency response, property values, transmission corridors, and the demand for real notice before a project appears. The sharpest moment, though, was the developer-relations lesson. Parish President Ryan Seal read aloud a letter he'd sent Origis Energy correcting what he called "misinformation" that the parish had already granted the company tax abatements — stating flatly that no abatement was committed and no permit had even been filed. The read for us: in a vacuum, the misinformation writes itself, and a project's own rumored "sweetheart deal" becomes the opposition's opening argument. Get the real terms on the public record before someone else invents them.
  • Blox Clears the Board: the update to Wednesday's Indianapolis item — the vote landed, and the developer's subtraction worked. The Metropolitan Development Commission approved DC Blox's scaled-back east-side campus 6-1 on July 15, over dozens of protesters outside the City-County Building in "Block DC Blox" shirts. It's the final local approval the project needed. The only thing that changed between a hostile room and a lopsided yes was the plan the developer walked in with — two buildings instead of three, 25 fewer diesel generators, a wider buffer along the Pennsy Trail. We flag it here for the pattern, not the sector: the concede-visibly-before-the-vote move cleared a data center this week for exactly the reasons it clears batteries, and the moratorium the full council is still weighing didn't stop the campaign already in the pipe.

  • MIPlainwell Reaches for the Freeze: Gun Plain Charter Township adopted Resolution 2026-9, a temporary moratorium on future battery energy storage systems, and posted it alongside a draft Ordinance 196 and a public-hearing notice on July 13 — the full paper trail of a township that has decided it has no rulebook for storage and won't take an application until it writes one. It's the same reflex now spreading township by township across southwest Michigan, and the same exposure for our storage clients: a freeze drafted in a hurry rarely troubles to define what it's freezing, so a well-sited battery gets caught in language aimed at "energy projects" writ large. The window to shape Ordinance 196 is open now, while it's still a draft — which is the only time the setback and safety terms are still negotiable.
On the Frontlines

Light this week by design — the recess kept most in-geography contested hearings dark. The one that reconvened is a docket we previewed Monday and Wednesday and promised to carry when it moved. It moved.

  • WVMaidsville Takes the Mic: the WV PSC hearing we've flagged twice is now live. Concerned residents, coal miners, and clean-power advocates packed the July 15 public-comment session on Mon Power and Potomac Edison's roughly $2.47 billion, 1,200-MW gas plant at Maidsville — a docket that also carries about 70 MW of new solar on reclaimed mine land and a storage component, with an evidentiary hearing running July 16 and into today. What's new since Wednesday is simply that the room finally filled: the same odd-bedfellows coalition that has trailed this proposal turned out in force, and the renewable pieces of the filing are riding through the process bolted to a fossil anchor. No decision — the companies expect a ruling within the year — but the testimony is now on the record, and the solar-and-storage rider's fate is tied to how the commission weighs the gas plant it's stapled to. We carry the outcome when it lands.
Plays of the Week
  • East Point EnergyOver-Build the Setback, Then Go Over the Commission: East Point Energy lost the room and kept the project. Its 150-MW Mill Stone battery plant in Chesapeake, Virginia drew a 7-1 planning-commission denial even though city planners recommended approval — the "no" driven by an affluent Woodards Mill neighborhood raising fire, noise, and "character," the now-standard NIMBY trident. Rather than fold, East Point is advancing to the full city council on July 21, and it walks in with a record built to make the denial look unreasonable: a setback of 1,431 feet against a required 300, a fence and sound barrier, roughly 80 acres left permanently undeveloped, a written ban on data centers on the parcel, and a ~$25 million tax-revenue figure over the project's life. The tactic to steal is two-part. First, over-comply loudly — when you clear the ordinance by nearly five times, "it doesn't meet our standards" stops being available to the opposition. Second, remember the planning commission's vote was advisory; the elected body decides, and elected officials weigh a documented tax base and a by-the-book application differently than a packed hearing room does. The wind at East Point's back is policy, too: Virginia's new storage law (effective July 1) orders Dominion and Appalachian Power to a combined 21,000 MW of storage, a sevenfold build-out — and the same session's solar law now forces localities that deny a project to justify it to state regulators. The terms are tilting toward the developer who documents everything.
  • Eastland County, TXNo Zoning Power? Pass the Resolution Anyway: here's the opposite tactic, run by the other side, and it's worth studying precisely because it shouldn't work. Eastland County Commissioners have no land-use authority over privately owned property — they cannot zone a solar farm out. So they did the next most effective thing: on a unanimous vote they adopted a formal resolution opposing solar farms, on the strength of a 250-signature resident petition, with the county judge making a point of saying the court would extend no support and grant no abatements. Legally, it's symbolic. Practically, it's a "no-welcome mat" nailed to the county line — a signal to developers and their financiers that this ground will be a slog, and a pre-loaded talking point for every future hearing in the region. The lesson runs both directions. For opposition, a resolution plus a petition manufactures the appearance of settled community will long before any binding vote exists. For our side, the counter is to read these symbolic resolutions as the early-warning system they are: by the time a county passes one, the petition already has 250 names and the narrative is already written. Getting there first — before the resolution, before the petition drive — is the only move that beats it.

Whoever writes the terms early wins the room late.
