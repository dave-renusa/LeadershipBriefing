RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 56 · Friday, October 2, 2026

Friday morning, team.

Two courts weighed in on county solar denials this week, and both asked the same question: what is in the record? An Allen County special judge upheld Marshall County, Indiana's denial of Invenergy's Tamarack Solar as "reasonable and supported by evidence," while an Oklahoma district judge is weighing whether Wagoner County's denial of a 5,000-acre project had any evidentiary support at all. In Michigan, where the state can override a township, townships are hiring Public Act 233 lawyers and, in one case, signing settlements instead of fighting. Counties that deny build a record, and developers that sue need one.

Also On Our Radar
  • The Record Held: An Allen County special judge ruled this week to uphold the Marshall County, Indiana Board of Zoning Appeals' denial of Invenergy's Tamarack Solar Energy Center, covering more than 1,400 fenced acres in West and Union townships near Culver. The judge found the late-2024 denial "reasonable and supported by evidence." Invenergy said the project "met all standards required in the Marshall County ordinance" and has 30 days to appeal. County Commissioners President Stan Klotz said the fight "has taken a lot of time and money frankly, that we could have spent elsewhere."
  • Speculation or Evidence: In Wagoner County, Oklahoma, Persica Solar is asking a district court to overturn commissioners' 2025 denial of a 5,000-acre, $336 million project near Porter, even though the planning commission had recommended approval. The company calls the stated reasons "speculation" with "no evidentiary support" and cites $31 million in ad valorem taxes, 85% of it for Porter schools, and 300 construction jobs. The county answers that solar is a conditional use and the company failed to prove benefit to the surrounding area. Judge Douglas Kirkley gave both sides 45 days to submit findings and a decision is expected in October or November.
  • Fourth Extension, Fourteen Votes: The Will County, Illinois Board voted 14-7 to grant a fourth and final extension to a 4-megawatt solar farm on about 57 acres near Peotone. The permit was approved in 2023 and the project has since changed hands to Standard Solar. Some members questioned whether special use permits should transfer with a sale, but others noted that forcing a restart would erase the 15 conditions the board had attached.
  • Financed Through the Fight: Jupiter Power closed $1.4 billion in financing across four transactions from April to July for ten battery projects in Texas and Michigan, 1,500 MW and 3,600 MWh in all. The July $536 million facility is its largest project financing to date, and its total since inception now tops $3 billion. Lenders are underwriting storage in Michigan, where state siting law now sits behind the local process.
  • Dirt Moves in Roosevelt County: Ørsted began construction on the 200-megawatt Blackwater Solar project between Portales and Clovis, New Mexico, with commercial operation expected in late 2027. The project uses First Solar panels, sells into Southwest Power Pool territory and is projected to pay nearly $18 million in property tax to Roosevelt County over its life.
  • A Year to Think in Mesa: Mesa County, Colorado commissioners voted unanimously for a one-year moratorium on data center land-use applications in the unincorporated county. Speakers raised cooling water in a drought-stricken area and whether the energy supply is adequate. The county will study permanent rules with a decision expected in 2027.
  • Baltimore County Extends: The Baltimore County Council voted Sept. 23 to extend its data center moratorium through December 2027, moving up from a January 2027 expiration. Councilmember Izzy Patoka said "there is broad based support to pause data center development." A 51-page Planning Board report recommended keeping data centers out of residential areas.
On the Frontlines
  • MIHiring the Specialist: Grant Township's board voted Sept. 2 to hire an attorney whose firm specializes in Public Act 233, as Ranger Power's Shipyard Solar moves toward the state. The project is 375 megawatts of solar on about 1,800 acres across Wexford and Grant townships, plus a 375-megawatt battery in Grant. The joint planning commission voted against a zoning amendment in July, and Grand Traverse County commissioners voted Sept. 3 to meet with the developer and counsel. A Grant trustee said the township is "learning as we go."
  • MIRecommended Over Reservations: The Fabius Township Planning Commission unanimously recommended approval of EDP Renewables' Three Lakes project, 178 megawatts of solar with 122 megawatts of storage, after a record turnout. One commissioner said she had concerns but believed the board had to proceed given state limits on local control. Construction would begin in 2027 if the township board approves. In July the same township board passed resolutions opposing state control and banned officials from signing developer NDAs.
  • WILocked Out of the Local Vote: NextEra's Fox Solar in Oconto County and Akron Solar in Adams and Wood counties need Public Service Commission approval, and the towns of Morgan and Rome are intervening. Morgan's board chair said nearly 70% of residents opposed Fox in a 2023 referendum, and Rome's chair said "we are locked out on the whole thing." A Republican-backed bill would require local approval for solar and wind projects of 15 megawatts or more before the state acts.
  • OHRunning Out the Clock: The Ohio Power Siting Board has asked the Ohio Supreme Court to dismiss the Kingwood Solar appeal as moot because the 175-megawatt project's PJM interconnection agreement has expired. The board denied the permit in December 2022. The developer's response: "Why did you run out the clock?" The Ohio Environmental Council said the court's own delay contributed.
  • ILSmallest Footprint, First in McLean: PureSky Energy and Ecology Action Center put the McLean 1 community solar project into full operation near Downs, along Interstate 74. The 1.98-megawatt array serves income-eligible Ameren households through Illinois Solar for All, which receive 50% off bill credits.
  • AZZoned for Employers, Not Servers: On Sept. 30, Pinal County supervisors unanimously approved industrial zoning for 4,696 acres of State Trust land along State Route 79, with no public comment recorded. Future occupants may generate their own solar-plus-storage power only as a secondary use, and the approval prohibits third-party server hosting. The rezoning follows community rejection of the Silver King Energy Center on overlapping land.
  • USThe Count Behind the Fights: The Sabin Center's latest report counts 888 state and local laws restricting renewable siting across 48 states and 567 projects that have faced significant local opposition, about 40% of them since cancelled. It also sees signs the rate of new restrictions may be slowing, particularly in states like Michigan with streamlined siting. A separate Daily Yonder and Canary Media analysis found more than 60 counties with renewable bans, moratoriums or barriers, over 60% of them rural.
Plays of the Week
  • Ranger PowerSettle With the Townships: The Michigan PSC approved a settlement for the 90-megawatt Acceleration Solar project across Leslie, Onondaga and Vevay townships in Ingham County, the state's first solar siting certificate and first settlement under Public Acts 233 and 234. Every party signed, including all three townships and PSC staff, and the terms cover noise, screening, lighting, complaint resolution, decommissioning assurance and tree clearing. The same developer is now facing a very different room in Wexford and Grant townships.
  • ØrstedPay the Place It Touches: With Blackwater Solar starting construction, Ørsted announced $100,000 to the Playa Lakes Joint Venture for playa wetland conservation near Melrose, New Mexico, alongside the projected $18 million in county property taxes. The gift is local, specific and tied to the landscape the project sits in.
  • Solar Energy Industries AssociationMake It a Property-Rights Case: SEIA head Tim Pawlenty frames solar as a "land rights" issue, appealing to conservative principles about what landowners may do with their land. In Idaho, a Republican commissioner who helped write a ban is organizing to overturn it on the same grounds.
  • Persica SolarPut the Reasons on the Record: Persica's court filing attacks the commissioners' stated reasons as unsupported "speculation" and points to studies showing solar projects don't diminish property values. Marshall County's win this week shows the other side of that approach: the judge there upheld a denial because the county's reasons were supported by evidence.

Marshall County's denial survived because its reasons were on the record. Wagoner County's is being tested on exactly that point. Michigan townships are hiring lawyers because the state can override them, and one developer is settling with three townships at once. On every active file, write down now what the likely denial reason would be and what evidence would support or refute it.
