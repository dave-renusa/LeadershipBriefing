RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 34 · Wednesday, July 29, 2026

Good morning, team.

Two Mondays ago the Town of Wawarsing, New York, held a public hearing on whether to freeze every new battery-storage application inside its borders. Not one resident spoke. The board voted the moratorium in anyway. That detail — an empty microphone, a six-month pause, several live applications suddenly parked — is the single most important thing in this issue, and it is why today's Deep Dive is about New York.

Because New York is no longer one state among several with a storage problem. It is the storage problem: more than 80% of every active BESS moratorium in the United States now sits inside its borders, more than the other eight states with active moratoriums combined. That is not a policy climate. That is a contagion with a map, and the mechanism that spreads it is procedural, cheap, and almost entirely unopposed. We spend a lot of time preparing clients for the hostile hearing. New York is teaching us that the far more expensive meeting is the one nobody attends.

Elsewhere the pattern held and the follow-ups landed. Kalamazoo Township adopted both of its moratoriums Monday, as we flagged. Austin County, Texas passed its BESS-and-data-center freeze unanimously the same day — the fire-marshal lockout is now real law. North Dakota's Supreme Court cleared a transmission line over its landowners on Monday. Illinois's Tazewell County board takes up its rewritten renewable ordinance and the Mackinaw solar denial today. And Henry County, Virginia's supervisors voted yesterday on the acreage cap that Richmond is watching; no result had posted as of this morning, and we'll carry it Friday. Radar has the counterweight: money is still closing, and a state appellate court just told Michigan's local governments that the override is real.

Must Read

Empire State of Pause

Start with the number, because it is genuinely hard to believe the first time you read it. New York State accounts for more than 80% of all active battery-storage moratoriums in the United States — more than the other eight states with active moratoriums combined. The runner-up, California, has four. Carina Energy's tracker counted 124 moratoriums logged in New York as of June, 86 of them still active, spread across 34 of the state's 62 counties. Chautauqua County alone carries nine. An independent count published in March put New York at 97 of roughly 150 nationwide across 17 states — about two-thirds of the country's total, and rising since.

July did nothing to slow it. On July 14, the Town of Newcomb — population under 500, deep inside the Adirondack Park — adopted Local Law #1 of 2026, a twelve-month freeze on battery storage, in what its own coverage described as a "proactive" move against an issue springing up "not just in the Adirondack Park, but statewide." The same night, the Town of Potsdam tabled its own moratorium — not because the board opposed it, but because the drafted law was so broad it would have banned residents from installing a Tesla Powerwall on their own house. Two weeks later, on July 27, Wawarsing approved roughly six months. Upstate in Chautauqua County, the Town of Ellery went further still, passing six-month moratoriums on commercial solar, wind, and storage at once, with the town attorney explaining that solar regulations were "two or three months away from being finalized." At the Ellery hearings, as at Wawarsing's, no public comment was received.

Understand the mechanism, because it is not what most developers assume. New York's Office of Renewable Energy Siting can override local zoning — including a moratorium — for projects above 25 MW. But that authority reaches standalone battery storage only when the battery is co-located with a qualifying solar or wind facility over the threshold. A standalone BESS, however large, is a purely local land-use decision. Bills to close that gap — S5506 and its Assembly companion — would extend ORES jurisdiction to standalone storage; both have sat in committee since February 2025 and passed neither chamber. So the state has a climate mandate that requires storage at scale, a siting authority explicitly built to clear local obstruction, and a carve-out that exempts precisely the asset class facing the obstruction. A developer who hits a moratorium has three options and none of them are good: wait it out, chase a variance, or move the site.

Now the part that should change how we staff these. Look at what actually triggers a New York moratorium: usually no project, no applicant, no controversy. Wawarsing was the exception that proves it — the town had already received several applications for sizable battery projects, and Supervisor Joe Stoeckeler noted that both the county and town planning boards backed the pause. The board's stated intent, per local reporting, was simply "to get its head around what they're dealing with before applications move forward," with the study period aimed at fire safety, water supply, and emergency response. That is not hostility. That is a small board, without technical staff, being asked to referee thermal runaway — and reaching for the only tool that reliably buys time. Fire departments across the region are now taking specialized BESS training, which is genuinely good news and also, politically, a standing reminder to every board member that these things are understood locally as a fire story first and an energy story second.

And a moratorium is not a decision. It is a drafting window. Every one of these towns spends six to twelve months writing the ordinance that governs storage permanently. Ellery said so out loud: the pause exists because the regulations are ninety days from done. That ordinance gets written whether or not a developer is in the room — and in New York right now, overwhelmingly, no developer is.

The Takeaway

The empty hearing room is the whole ballgame, and we are selling the wrong meeting. Clients budget for the packed, angry rezoning hearing — the one with the sign-waving and the three-hour comment period. New York shows the cheaper and far more consequential fight is the moratorium hearing that draws zero speakers and passes in four minutes, freezing a site for a year and, worse, opening a drafting window our client will not be invited into. Three instructions follow. One: monitor for the pause, not the project. Somebody on the account should be reading town-board agendas across the whole target county, not just the host town — these spread by neighbor emulation, and Chautauqua's nine did not happen independently. Two: show up during the study period, not the vote. A board that just voted to "get its head around" batteries has publicly announced it wants information and has none; the firm that arrives with a fire-marshal briefing, a decommissioning bond, and a peer town's ordinance in month two writes the rules that bind everyone else in month eight. Three: brief every New York client on co-location as a siting strategy, today. The ORES threshold is not a technicality — it is the difference between a project a town can freeze and one it cannot. When a standalone battery can be paired with qualifying generation above 25 MW, that pairing buys jurisdiction, and jurisdiction is the only thing in New York currently immune to an empty room.

Also On Our Radar
  • Michigan's Override Holds: the counterweight to everything above, and the most useful precedent our Midwest clients have. The Michigan Court of Appeals has largely upheld the Public Service Commission's renewable-siting process built under Public Act 233, the 2023 law that caps how far local governments can go in restricting wind, solar, and storage and empowers the MPSC to step in when they overreach. Local governments challenged the MPSC's implementing order; the appellate court left its key architecture standing. Set that next to the New York picture and the contrast is instructive — Michigan built a backstop that actually reaches the assets being blocked, and a court just declined to dismantle it. The engagement lesson is not "the state will save you." It's that in a PA 233 state, the local negotiation happens in the shadow of a real alternative, which changes what a township is willing to trade for. Know which kind of state you're standing in before you decide how hard to push.
  • Close of Business: while boards paused, capital didn't. Heelstone Renewable Energy reached financial close on July 20 and started construction on three Illinois solar projects totaling 86 MW — Two Blues (40 MW), Snapdragon (28 MW), and Pike (18 MW) — with Stonehenge Capital on tax equity, ING as lead arranger, and long-term corporate PPAs with Meta taking the full environmental attribute output. Commercial operation is targeted for the end of this year. Two things worth carrying into a host-community conversation: the offtaker is a hyperscaler buying renewables rather than a data center demanding grid power, which is the version of that story boards almost never hear; and the timeline is brutally tight, which is the industry-wide condition right now — projects face a hard placed-in-service deadline of December 31, 2027 to claim any federal credit at all. Every month a moratorium runs is a month off a clock that does not stop.
  • Somebody Pays: the cost story that makes renewables the affordable answer, if we frame it. State ratepayer advocates told FERC on July 17 that the commission is failing to shield PJM consumers from transmission costs driven by data centers, and PJM's own market monitor found data centers drove roughly $6.3 billion of the latest capacity-auction costs. Capacity prices have climbed from $28.92/MW-day in 2024/25 to $329.17/MW-day for 2026/27, and the auction fell short of its reliability target for the third straight time. Here is the pivot for our decks: in a PJM state, the ratepayer anger aimed at large load is recruitable. Generation that clears quickly and locally is the thing that relieves the pressure everyone is furious about. A solar-plus-storage project is not another burden on the bill — it is the supply side of the exact equation the county is mad about. Say it in those words.
On the Frontlines
  • MIKalamazoo Closes Both Doors: the outcome we promised Monday. The Kalamazoo Township Board unanimously adopted both moratoriums on July 27 — Ordinance No. 646 freezing new battery-storage applications while the Planning Commission studies possible amendments, and Ordinance No. 647 imposing a one-year pause on data centers. Both take effect on publication. Note the asymmetry buried in the language: the data-center pause has a defined twelve-month term, while the BESS freeze runs until the Planning Commission finishes studying. Open-ended is worse than long. This is also the second Kalamazoo County jurisdiction to move — Texas Township adopted its own twelve-month data-center moratorium in June — which is the neighbor-emulation pattern from our Must Read playing out in a PA 233 state. Watch whether the storage half gets tested against the MPSC backstop.
  • TXFire Marshal, Locked Out: Austin County commissioners approved the moratorium unanimously on July 27, becoming one of the first Texas counties to adopt one. As drafted, no county officer — including the fire marshal — may approve any permit, review, or related document for a new BESS or data center until a comprehensive health-and-safety review is complete, and the county may seek injunctive relief against anyone who starts building without it. It followed a July 13 hearing that drew more than 100 residents. The precedent to watch isn't the freeze, it's the durability: a Texas Hill Country county rescinded its own data-center moratorium in June after being sued. Austin County has now built a broader version of the same instrument. Storage is again riding shotgun in an ordinance written about server halls.
  • NDLine Cleared, Landowners Out: the North Dakota Supreme Court on July 27 rejected an appeal from landowners and townships fighting the Otter Tail Power and MDU transmission line from Jamestown to Ellendale. The opponents argued the Public Service Commission failed to comply with state notice-and-hearing requirements; the court found the notices adequate and held the district court properly ruled it lacked jurisdiction. The PSC's approval stands. File this next to the Texas 765-kV landowner petitions still pending at the PUCT: procedural-notice challenges are becoming the standard opening move against transmission, and they are mostly losing on the merits — but they cost years. The cheaper answer remains the one nobody wants to fund, which is real corridor engagement before the route is filed.
  • ILTazewell Takes the Vote: the county board acts today on the package its Land Use Committee recommended on July 16 — two amendments that would consolidate and replace the county's separate commercial wind and solar rules with a single comprehensive renewable-energy ordinance, alongside a recommended denial of a solar petition in Mackinaw. The committee also took data-center ordinance language from a Logan County zoning official, which tells you where the drafting template is coming from. This is the New York dynamic in Illinois clothing: the denial makes the news, the ordinance rewrite decides the next decade. Illinois's state solar-siting law limits how far a county can go, so the interesting question is whether the consolidated ordinance survives contact with it — Will County is already testing that line at an airpark.
  • VAHenry's Ceiling, Pending: supervisors were scheduled to vote yesterday on lowering the county's solar-acreage cap from 2,445 acres to 1,754 — the exact total already approved, which converts a cap into a closed door. No outcome had posted as of this morning; we'll carry it Friday. The stakes are unchanged and worth restating: only 633.9 of those approved acres are actually built, and county administrator Dale Wagoner concedes most approved projects have "made little progress" — so the county is capping against paper, not panels. Henry has a 76% solar approval rate, among Virginia's highest, which makes it an awkward villain and a very effective test case. Under the new "ban the bans" statute every denial goes to the state with written justification, and Sen. Schuyler VanValkenburg has already promised a "heavier hand" if localities flout the spirit of it.
  • MDFive Is Too Many: just off our map but exactly our fight. Worcester County commissioners voted 7-0, with no discussion, to oppose a 4.95 MW solar project outside Pocomoke City — rejecting the developer's request for a favorable county review before the applicant was allowed to present. Commissioner Caryn Abbott's stated reason was saturation: four solar farms already sit within two miles, and this would be the fifth. The detail that should sting is that the site is zoned I-1 Light Industrial, where utility-scale solar is permitted by right. When by-right isn't by right, the ordinance was never the protection — the relationship was. And "we've already done our share" is now the most common objection in mature solar counties; it is a math argument on the surface and a fairness argument underneath, and it has to be answered as the latter.
  • IDDownsized by the Wires: a useful reminder that not every shrunken project was shrunk by opponents. Developers of the Husky Howl Solar Facility in south Fremont County went before the planning and zoning commission Monday with a project cut from 150 MW to 95 MW — not over local objections, but because of transmission-line capacity limits. The commission granted the agricultural rezone and held the permit. Two things to steal: transmission scarcity is quietly doing more resizing than opposition is, and a developer who arrives having already right-sized to the grid walks in with a credibility asset most applicants never have. "We made it smaller before you asked" is worth more in a hearing room than any mitigation package bought later.

The hearing nobody attended is the one that cost the most. Read the agendas.
