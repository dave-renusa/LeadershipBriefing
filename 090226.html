RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 48 · Wednesday, September 2, 2026

Good morning.

Two projects on opposite ends of the country spent real money on their host communities in the last week and got nothing durable for it. Ørsted's Badger Wind Farm gave Wishek, North Dakota a firehouse, a swimming pool and roughly $1.3 million a year in county revenue, and four months after it energized, McIntosh County passed a wind moratorium. In Pinal County, Arizona, a developer cut its data campus by 80 percent, capped its own water use at 600 acre-feet and volunteered 22 new conditions — and the board rejected it 4-1 while residents told commissioners the concessions were negotiating theater. The tell in both cases is the same: neither county had a code that made any of it binding, so generosity read as an admission that the project needed forgiving. Contrast Wyoming, where a siting council approved 139 turbines on Tuesday with 26 written conditions attached. Conditions bought a permit. Gifts bought a moratorium.

Must Read

A Firehouse, a Pool, and a Moratorium

Ørsted's 92-turbine, 250-megawatt Badger Wind Farm came online in January outside Wishek, North Dakota — a town of about 850 people in McIntosh County, and the largest economic development project in its history. By the standard playbook, the engagement work was exemplary. The county expects roughly $1.3 million a year in wind generation taxes across a population of about 2,500, money that goes to roads, bridges, schools, the hospital, senior care and fire and ambulance service. On top of the tax base, Ørsted wrote $150,000 in direct donations to the city, which paid for a new firehouse, a public swimming pool and repairs to the community center. Former mayor Brenda Dohn described a developer that kept asking what the town needed: "Ørsted was very much like, 'Let us know what you need help with … because we want to have a good relationship with the city.'"

In April, four months after the project started generating, McIntosh County adopted a one-year moratorium on new wind development.

The reason is not that the money was insufficient or that anyone thinks it was a bribe. The reason is that the money answered a question nobody was asking. Former mayor and council member Larry Wald put the actual grievance plainly: "We used to live in open country, right? Now, these towers are the first thing you see when you come into Wishek and the last thing you see on your way out." One resident emailed the reporter a single line — "Welcome to my nightmare." Even George Wolff, a farmer who hosts two 3-megawatt turbines across 3,500 acres and collects about $5,000 per turbine per quarter, faults the siting rather than the terms: "They should have stayed further away from some of the nonparticipating residences, particularly the ones close to town." A participating landowner, paid on schedule, is arguing the setback.

Here is the structural failure, and it is not Ørsted's. Wishek had no siting authority over the project at all. The city could accept a check; it could not write a setback, condition a viewshed, or negotiate turbine placement relative to the town approach. So the only lever available to the developer was money, and the only lever available to residents afterward was a countywide ban. A benefits package delivered into a jurisdiction with no regulatory standing does not convert into consent, because there is no instrument for it to convert into. It arrives as a gift, and gifts are revocable in the mind of the recipient in a way that permit conditions are not.

The state-level scoreboard shows what that costs at scale. North Dakota brought 38 wind farms online between 2005 and 2020 and only four since, even though landowners have collected $25 million to $30 million a year in payments since 2020. The lone approval so far this year came on Aug. 12, when the Public Service Commission cleared Minnesota Power's roughly $800 million Longspur project north of Bismarck. PSC Chair Randy Christmann, pressing a rancher in May testimony, argued the transition is arithmetic rather than ideology — "Would it surprise you to know that in about the last 20 years, about 35 or 40 percent of the coal capacity in America has come off the grid?" — while Rep. Julie Fedorchak, who sponsored 2025 legislation to phase out wind and solar tax credits, offers the frame that is actually winning locally: the buildout "has been designed to help bring in the power needed for states like Minnesota to meet their goals. And those aren't the goals in my state of North Dakota." Extraction, not investment. That frame beats a firehouse.

The comparison that should sit with us all week is Wyoming, where the Industrial Siting Council permitted the 650-MW Laramie Range Wind Project on Tuesday after roughly 18 hours of testimony — and attached 26 conditions. Opponents lost, and they are furious, and they also have 26 enforceable commitments on paper that they can hold the developer to for the life of the project. Wishek has a swimming pool. The engagement lesson is not to spend less. It is to spend into a document.

The Takeaway

A benefits package is only as durable as the instrument that carries it. Where the host jurisdiction has no siting authority, our first move is not to write a bigger check — it is to get the community a seat in the venue that does have authority, and to convert every voluntary commitment into a permit condition, a development agreement, or an ordinance provision somebody can enforce. Unconverted generosity reads as guilt, and it expires the moment the turbines are visible.

Also On Our Radar
  • Four Hundred Miles, Unanimously: after an hours-long hearing dominated by landowner testimony, the Public Utility Commission of Texas voted unanimously to approve amendments to two Oncor certificates authorizing more than 400 miles of new 765-kV line — one running southwest from Bosque County, one northwest from San Antonio, both into the Permian Basin across at least 30 counties. Commissioners tabled a third route, the Howard Solstice line through South Texas. Attorney General Ken Paxton filed an amicus brief that morning backing the landowner group American Liberty Stewards against Oncor, AEP Texas and CPS Energy, and state Sen. Kevin Sparks and Business and Commerce Chair Charles Schwertner had both urged a delay until the Legislature reforms siting. Commissioner Courtney Hjaltman addressed opponents directly from the dais — "They're about your land … your heart" — and voted yes anyway. Hill Country Preservation Coalition vice president Amanda Griffin promised the sequel: "we're prepared to clean house in November." When the commission acknowledges the grievance and approves regardless, the fight moves to the ballot, and it moves fast.
  • Twenty-Six Conditions Later: the Wyoming Industrial Siting Council voted unanimously Tuesday to permit the 650-MW Laramie Range Wind Project — 139 turbines across 41,220 acres about 20 miles north of Cheyenne — after roughly 18 hours of testimony over two days. ConnectGen Laramie County LLC, a Repsol Renewables North America affiliate, is back with a scaled-down version after Laramie County commissioners rejected a 56,000-acre, 170-turbine plan 3-1 in September 2025; the company sued over that denial while pursuing the state route. The 1,876-page application projects $32.8 million in county sales and use tax during construction and about $194 million in property and production taxes over 35 years on roughly $1.1 billion of investment, with build-out in two phases and both operational by the end of 2029. DEQ's Jillian Scott says the order takes effect in 45 days. Adjacent rancher Wendy Volk: "We are deeply disappointed by the Industrial Siting Council's decision." One procedural note worth logging: the hearing's Zoom livestream was shut down after a reported hacking and referred to the Wyoming Division of Criminal Investigation. Remote participation is now an attack surface, and a compromised livestream is a due-process argument waiting to be filed.
  • Twenty Point Two: the U.S. added 6.7 GW and 20.2 GWh of battery storage in the second quarter, the largest quarter on record, per SEIA and Benchmark Mineral Intelligence. Utility-scale carried about 17.9 GWh of it across seven individual gigawatt-hour-scale projects — four in Arizona, two in California, one in Utah — with Arizona alone adding a record 6.2 GWh, ahead of Texas at 3.8 and California at 3.6. Fifty-six percent was standalone, 44% paired with solar; first-half installations hit 30.8 GWh, up 23% year over year, and Benchmark raised its 2030 forecast 11.5% to 683 GWh. SEIA's Tim Pawlenty framed it as reliability rather than climate: storage "strengthens our energy security, meets rising demand, and puts downward pressure on electricity bills." The distributional detail is the one to carry into a hearing room — Energy-Storage.News notes that more than 74% of second-quarter capacity landed in states Trump won in 2024, and Benchmark's Shan Tomouk put it directly: "Energy storage is no longer just a California and Texas story anymore." Residential, meanwhile, fell 27% to 657 MWh after the 25D credit went away.
  • Forty-Six Cents: the Section 232 action announced Aug. 6 and effective Dec. 4 has already pushed module spot pricing to roughly 46 cents per watt, levels last seen during the 2022-23 anti-circumvention scare. The scheme stacks a minimum import price — 38 cents per watt for modules, 22 for cells, with polysilicon and wafer equivalents at 3.7 and 12 cents — on top of a 15% ad valorem tariff and existing Section 301 duties. Intertek CEA's Christian Roselund expects domestic module assembly to "start to dominate the market in 2027," with vertically integrated firms best positioned: "the higher the degree of vertical integration, the more benefit this has." Joseph C. Johnson sees a floor near 30 cents once the market adapts, but warns the policy churn is itself the constraint on new capacity — "It's very difficult for manufacturers to expand in that type of environment." For our purposes the number matters because it changes the argument in the room: a county asking why a project needs the acreage it needs is going to hear a different cost stack for the next two construction seasons.
  • Sacramento Sues the Buyout: California Attorney General Rob Bonta and the California Energy Commission sued Interior in federal court on Friday over the cancellation of the 2-GW Golden State Wind project off the central coast. Interior disclosed in April that it had agreed to pay developer Ocean Winds $120 million to abandon the project and put the money into fossil fuel projects instead; the complaint says the state also loses more than $30 million in workforce and community funds on top of more than $100 million already spent upgrading ports and transmission. Bonta's theory is spending power, not energy policy: "The Judgment Fund is not the president's piggy bank. The Constitution gives Congress, not the president, the power to decide how taxpayer dollars are spent." CEC chair David Hochschild called the cancellation "both reckless and unlawful." Roughly a half-dozen early-stage offshore projects have now been cancelled — and the community-benefit and workforce dollars that went out the door with them were, in most cases, exactly the commitments local coalitions had been organized around.
  • The Other ITC: a super PAC named the Invest in Tomorrow Coalition — founded by CleanChoice Energy's Tom Matzzie, Ripple co-founder Chris Larsen and former Sierra Club head Michael Brune, and named to echo the dead solar investment tax credit — has assembled roughly $20 million and gone five-for-five against House Freedom Caucus Republicans this primary season. The tactic worth studying is that its ads almost never mention clean energy. Against Rep. Andy Ogles in Tennessee it spent $2 million running a "Lyin' Andy" credibility attack while separately building challenger Charlie Hatcher's name ID, cutting the share of GOP primary voters who had never heard of Hatcher from about half to about a quarter in three weeks; Ogles lost by more than six points despite a Trump endorsement. Against Rep. Chip Roy it attacked insufficient Trump loyalty. "We're going to use whatever works," Matzzie said. Not everyone is on board — SEIA's Tim Pawlenty: "We're in the business of making more friends, not more enemies" — and the split matters to us, because a sector that is simultaneously running scorched-earth primaries and asking county boards for trust is going to get asked about it in a hearing room eventually.
  • Emergency, Filed: Oklo filed an emergency complaint at FERC on Aug. 28 asking the commission to order PJM to reinstate a 750-MW mixed-technology project at its original queue position, after the RTO withdrew it on Aug. 3 partly because the company never showed the project could ride through a sudden voltage drop. Oklo says the withdrawal costs it at least 14 months and calls PJM's review "unjust, unreasonable, and unduly discriminatory." PJM's Jeffrey Shields notes nearly 90% of the 811 new service requests met application requirements. The number underneath the dispute is the one to keep: of the 715 projects and 200-plus GW that qualified for the first cycle of the reformed process, 60 GW is storage, 11.8 GW solar, 7.5 GW hybrid solar-storage and 3.9 GW wind — against 100 GW of gas. Renewables are still a minority of what is trying to get in the door in PJM, which is worth knowing before we cite queue totals as evidence of momentum.
Project Updates
  • MIPreemption Ends in a Settlement: a 90-megawatt solar facility spanning three townships in southern Ingham County became the first project to clear the state after invoking Public Act 233, the 2023 law letting renewable developers bypass local zoning. The developer was the first in Michigan to ask the Public Service Commission to review plans that had run into local objections — and then never needed the hearing. Leslie, Onondaga and Vevay townships negotiated directly instead, and under the agreement approved Thursday they split $180,000 proportionally by how many panels fall inside each boundary, with noise and lighting requirements attached. Ingham County separately waived its right to intervene in exchange for $180,000 of its own. The siting law is still being contested in court. Read the sequence carefully, because it is the template: filing at the state did not preempt the townships, it repriced them. The threat of preemption is worth more at the table than preemption is at the commission.
  • CAA Thousand Pages, One Address: the California Energy Commission held its final in-person hearing Tuesday on NextEra's Corby battery storage project near Vacaville, and drew hours of opposition after staff recommended approval in July on the strength of a 1,000-plus-page environmental review. Opponents were disciplined about what they were arguing: "It isn't about whether or not to have clean energy or not. This is about where to site the facility," said Michael Gellar, who has lived about 1,000 feet from the Kilkenny Road site for nearly 40 years. Solano County Supervisor Mitch Mashburn drew applause with "Why are we considering this project at this site?" — the county has pushed to relocate the project to the Lambie Industrial Park, and CEC staff responded that the alternative sites carry similar environmental impacts but "do not advance the project's goals." Executive director Drew Bohan insisted the hearing was real: "This is not just a check-the-box exercise." The commission votes Nov. 18 in Sacramento. Note the framing gap that will decide this: staff answered a significance question, and the county asked a siting question, and those are not the same record.
  • VAThe Line Starts Here: Campbell County supervisors signaled Monday night that they will formally oppose the $1 billion, 115-mile 765-kV line that begins in their county, taking the next 30 days to draft a resolution for the State Corporation Commission. That puts the origin point of the Dominion–FirstEnergy–Transource route on the same side as the counties it crosses. Supervisor Matt Cline made the load-source argument rather than the aesthetic one: "If Northern Virginia needs power, Northern Virginia can generate power without having to use this transmission project," adding that the power "is going to be potentially generated or at least transferred through Campbell at a very large substation, and it's going up to feed data centers in Northern Virginia." Landowner Shelly Evans named the second-order fear that is doing most of the organizing work in Virginia right now — that the line "will also bring more infrastructure and more data centers to our area." Transmission opposition has stopped being about towers. It is about what the towers are understood to invite.
  • ALWho Signs the Contract: the Alabama Attorney General's office filed comments in a Public Service Commission docket arguing that commissioners, not staff, must publicly vote on large-load utility contracts, calling the commission's 1996 staff-delegation order "ultra vires and invalid" in the words of consumer-interest chief Olivia Martin. The live example is a renewables project: Clearview Ranch, 1,400 acres carrying 200 MW of solar and 200 MW of two-hour storage across Macon and Bullock counties, approved through the staff process on Aug. 4 to serve Meta's Montgomery data center. Energy Alabama executive director Daniel Tait says he was never notified before that approval. There is a real trap here for our side. A staff-level approval feels like a clean win right up until somebody reopens it, and a solar-plus-storage project that never had a public vote is the easiest possible target for an argument that is actually about the data center it serves.
  • AZEighty Percent Off, Still No: the Pinal County Board of Supervisors voted 4-1 to reject Vermaland's La Osa Energy Center outside Eloy — 11 buildings, a 500-MW gas plant and battery storage serving a gigawatt of demand — after the developer cut the plan roughly 80% from its original scale and added 22 conditions between the May hearing and the August vote, including a self-imposed 600 acre-foot fresh-water cap. Turnout forced five overflow rooms across three buildings. The concessions read as tactics: "probably planned all along," said Hidden Valley resident Robin Davis. "Ask for more than you want and settle for what you really wanted." Mary Eileen Flanagan testified on subsidence — "Many of my neighbors' wells have gone dry … It currently costs about $70,000 to drill a well to 900 feet. The problem is, for many, water is at 1,200 feet." Mark Seidel named the enforcement problem: "I don't want stipulations that are unenforceable or not enforced." Pinal County has no data-center definitions in its zoning code at all, which is precisely why every protection on offer was voluntary — and why none of them counted.
  • WYFifty-Three Hundred Acres, Recommended: the Platte County Planning and Zoning Commission voted unanimously to recommend rezoning 5,344 acres of Goertz family ranchland in the Goshen Hole area northeast of Wheatland from ranch and agricultural to industrial, opening it to generation, storage and a data center campus. Three conditions came with it: reversion to the old zoning if construction does not start within five years or upon decommissioning, presentation of any industrial subdivision to the commissioners before recording, and a special-use permit for any actual development. Chairman Marty Shepard worked hard to keep expectations low — "Just because these people are here does not mean a project is going to happen" — and earlier, "If we rezone to commercial industrial and we give these companies free rein, we're not in favor of that." Developer Blake Marcus pledged closed-loop cooling and under 15 acre-feet of water a year. The engagement failure is instructive: adjacent landowner Amy Miller says a certified letter to the developer came back unopened and alleges a surveyor trespassed on her land. Rick Teeters, whose family has been there 85 years: "There is no water in Goshen Hole." The county commissioners took it up at 1 p.m. yesterday.
  • NMThe Court Keeps the Pause: the New Mexico Supreme Court unanimously denied Yucca Growth Infrastructure's request to partially resume the Environment Department's air-quality permit proceedings for Project Jupiter, the four-building data center complex under construction in Doña Ana County. The court had halted the Sept. 14 hearing in August after New Energy Economy argued it was premature, because the natural gas pipeline needed to fuel the complex has not cleared its own review and is now delayed to 2027. Yucca's lawyers warned the pause makes the department's Nov. 23 permit deadline "impossible to meet." Mariel Nanasi's answer is the line to remember, because it will be aimed at renewables projects too: "A developer cannot manufacture an emergency and then use that emergency to strip the public of meaningful participation. Project Jupiter chose its technology, chose its schedule, and chose to pursue construction while critical governmental approvals remained unresolved." Building ahead of your permits converts every schedule risk into a public-participation argument.
  • SCNot for Public Use: the South Carolina Public Service Commission ruled unanimously that Valara Holdings' 450-MW self-generation plant is not subject to the state Utility Siting Act, because "the facility will not export power generated to the electric grid for public use." Commissioner David Britt, a former Spartanburg County Council member who backed the $2.8 billion project, recused himself and left the room. The scale drift is the story: the site on 300 acres of a former Kohler plant was originally permitted for roughly 50 MW across 24 gas generators, and in March the company asked state environmental regulators for five 17-MW and six 54-MW combustion turbines. SELC senior attorney Kate Mixson drew the conclusion for everyone: companies "can build power plants of any size without any oversight from the commission." A separate SELC suit over the county's "minor" development classification is still live, and the county council is weighing a one-year moratorium. Behind-the-meter generation is now a bigger siting loophole than any renewables exemption on the books, and it is going to get closed in a way that catches our projects too.
On the Frontlines
  • IABigger Year Coming: the Siting Solutions Project's State of Siting 2026 report, presented Wednesday, tracked more than 200 renewable siting bills across 2026 sessions — 43% restrictive, 36% deployment-friendly — and found that fewer than 30 were enacted, only two of them restrictive. Introduced bills skewed "very restrictive" in Missouri, Oklahoma, Mississippi and Alabama, including outright and de facto moratoriums; Iowa rated neutral, with seven bills and none enacted. Report author Nelson Falkenburg of Clean Tomorrow says Iowa policy was "turned on its head" when the Iowa Utilities Commission granted a generation certificate to a Cerro Gordo County solar project, preempting the county's ban on wind, solar and storage — and expects 2027 to be spent defending that preemption authority. Analyst Zona Martin reads Iowa, Arizona and Oklahoma as "backlash." The gap between bills introduced and bills enacted is our working margin, and it closes the year the introductions become organized.
  • OKUnder Advisement: Wagoner County District Judge Douglas Kirkley heard oral arguments Monday and took the Persica Solar appeal under advisement without ruling from the bench. NextEra's subsidiary is challenging the county's denial of a conditional use permit for a nearly 5,000-acre, $336 million array near Porter along the Arkansas River, which commissioners rejected 2-1 in June 2025 after more than three hours of public opposition, saying the company had not shown the facility would benefit the surrounding area. Persica's counter is fiscal — roughly $31 million in county ad valorem revenue over 30 years. A decision is expected soon.
  • NVStayed on the Merits: the Interior Board of Land Appeals granted a stay Tuesday of BLM's approval letting Townsite Solar 2 build a data center on 80 acres of federal land near I-11 and U.S. 95 outside Boulder City, finding the appellants likely to succeed on their claim that BLM's environmental analysis was "insufficient" and that harm to members' recreational interests outweighed harm to the developer. Boulder City, the Sierra Club and the Center for Biological Diversity filed in July; the city argues BLM never consulted it or ran adequate public process. The defect is the reuse: Townsite held a 2023 federal approval for solar generation and storage on the parcel and sought an amended right-of-way in 2025 to build something else on the same analysis. A renewables NEPA record is not a transferable asset, and every attempt to treat it as one makes the next solar right-of-way harder to defend.
  • CAA Gigawatt, Unfunded: California's final budget, published Friday ahead of the Aug. 31 end of session, authorized no new money for the Demand Side Grid Support program, the largest virtual power plant in the country. Senate Democrats had proposed shifting $70 million from another Energy Commission program and the administration rejected it, though the budget did drop Newsom's plan to move the program to the CPUC. DSGS has enrolled roughly 130,000 battery-equipped homes and nearly 75,000 with smart thermostats and flexible loads, and delivered about 476 MW over two hours in a July 2025 test that PG&E called the largest of its kind in California. Advanced Energy United's Brandon García: "There is no guaranteed funding for the program for next year, at the very least it wasn't outright gutted." His warning about enrollment attrition is the operative one — "if they wait until June, I don't know how many participants will be around."
  • TXRestring, Don't Rebuild: a modeling study from Evolved Energy Research finds that in a high-load ERCOT scenario through 2040, $10 billion of reconductoring avoids $30 billion of greenfield transmission, a net $20 billion saving — with $35 billion of new-build still required on top. The firm modeled ERCOT's roughly 5,000 buses and 7,000 lines individually and argues zonal models cannot answer the question at all: "The moment you merge physical lines into a zone, you lose the information needed to choose between reconductoring and building new." The line for our purposes is the firm's own caveat, delivered as a boast — because ERCOT is "unusually well-suited to long-distance buildout," Texas is the conservative case, and "where greenfield transmission is harder and slower to permit, reconductoring matters even more." Every county fighting a new corridor is about to ask why the existing one was not restrung first, and it is now a fair question with a number attached.
  • AZOne Gigawatt to 1.6: Waaree Energies' board approved roughly $37 million in capital spending to re-equip its Arizona module plant, replacing existing lines with higher-efficiency equipment and lifting annual capacity from 1 GW to 1.6 GW. That takes the company's total U.S. module capacity to 4.8 GW — 3.2 GW in Texas, 1.6 GW in Arizona — financed through a mix of debt and internal accruals. Read it against the Section 232 arithmetic above: this is what "domestic assembly starts to dominate in 2027" looks like as a capital decision, in a state that just posted the largest single-state storage quarter on record.
  • NCSix Hundred Forty-Nine Generators: state environmental regulators issued separate air permits to Amazon and Duke Energy at Amazon's $10 billion hyperscale campus in Hamlet, Richmond County — 592 diesel emergency generators for Amazon, 57 more for Duke to supply primary power until interconnection, 649 on one site. SELC senior attorney Caroline Cress argues the two-permit split was engineered to avoid Clean Air Act aggregation and says the state "failed to protect people's health and the air we all breathe." The project is 21 buildings of about 200,000 square feet on nearly 800 acres and will become Duke's largest customer; the county conveyed 646.866 acres at a meeting immediately following a public hearing SELC says was inadequately noticed. Note this is an advocacy filing, not a neutral account — but the permitting structure it describes is public record, and "split the application to duck the threshold" is a criticism that will be aimed at phased solar and storage next.
  • GAThirty-Two Hundred Megawatts, One Customer: Sen. Raphael Warnock called for a statewide data center moratorium after meeting Effingham County residents fighting OpenAI's planned coastal Georgia facility — days after state regulators approved Georgia Power's contract to serve it at 3,200 megawatts, the largest customer contract the utility has ever reported, with added provisions meant to shield residential and small-business ratepayers from cost shifts. Savannah has passed a 155-day moratorium and Richmond Hill says its development ordinance already bars the use. A sitting U.S. senator endorsing a state moratorium is a threshold event; the affordability frame has now cleared local politics entirely.
  • MTEighteen Days Too Late: Butte-Silver Bow County District Judge Frank Joseph ruled against the citizens group 406 People First in filings dated Aug. 28, keeping off the November ballot an initiative that would have required approval by two-thirds of registered voters before any data center could be built or expanded. The election administrator accepted 3,652 signatures on July 31 and then reversed, citing a new state law setting a local counting deadline a month ahead of the state deadline — making the real cutoff July 13. Joseph held that a misstatement of law is not a misstatement of fact and cannot support equitable estoppel, and noted both sides had the means to consult counsel and both miscalculated. Group president Denise Kelly: "I was handing in signatures every week … so we already knew on July 31 that we had hit our goal." The group says it will appeal. Ballot-initiative deadlines moved this year in more than one state, and the side that reads the amended statute first wins without ever arguing the merits.

A wind farm bought a firehouse and a swimming pool and got a moratorium. A data campus cut itself by 80 percent, capped its own water and got a 4-1 no. A wind project that took 26 written conditions got a permit. The variable is not how much a developer gives, it is whether anyone with authority wrote it down. Before we build another benefits package this quarter, find out whether the jurisdiction we are giving it to has the standing to turn it into a condition — and if it doesn't, get it standing first.
