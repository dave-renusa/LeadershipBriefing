Must Read

The Extension Cord

A third 765-kilovolt line is now drawing opposition in the mid-Atlantic, and this one is ours to watch: the Valley North Project, a 260-mile spine that would cross as many as 15 West Virginia counties on its way from Putnam County, WV to Frederick County, MD. It is a joint venture — Valley Link, owned by FirstEnergy Transmission, Dominion Energy, and AEP's Transource — built to answer reliability concerns raised by PJM. And it is repeating, county by county, the exact mistake that has cost every big wire this year: the affected landowners learned they were on the map from a letter, not a conversation.

In Barbour County, one third-generation farmer says a proposed route runs within 30 feet of her home and swallows 70 acres of her land; she has already organized her neighbors and is asking whether they're too late. In Tucker County, families on 33- and 410-acre parcels turned out to a July open house to say the same thing — that the line ruins their property, their water, their woods, and does it to send power to Northern Virginia. Valley Link is running its mandated open houses through the month — Barbour on July 15, Lewis and Upshur on July 16, Harrison and Preston on July 21, Gilmer on July 22 — and the company stresses no route has been chosen. But the opposition has a message already, and it's a gift: "We're literally being used as an extension cord between these data centers." The spokesperson's rebuttal — that the grid strain is really about electrification, retiring generation, and data centers — is true, nuanced, and exactly the kind of three-part answer that loses a kitchen-table argument to a four-word slogan.

Notice what this is and isn't. It isn't a clean-versus-dirty fight — solar, wind, and gas all ride this wire, and it's PJM's reliability case, not any one plant, that's driving it. It's a notice fight, the same failure mode that paused Texas's STEP buildout and blindsided Loudoun: routes disclosed by mail, to people who were never in the room when the study area was drawn. For our clients, whose projects increasingly can't reach load without new PJM backbone like this, that's the whole exposure. A financed, permitted generation project stranded behind a 765-kV line that can't clear its right-of-way is just as dead as one the county denied.

The Takeaway

The corridor is the campaign, and the first open house is already late. When a project depends on new PJM wire, treat every route option as a notice event: get to the landowners in the study area before the map hits their mailbox, and put the necessity-and-benefit story on the table in their language — because "extension cord" is the frame opposition will hand them if we don't. Necessity arguments with three moving parts don't beat a slogan. A single clear reason, delivered in person, before the letter, does.

Also On Our Radar
  • Over Their Heads: when Santa Cruz County, California wouldn't move fast enough, the developer stopped asking. New Leaf Energy withdrew its ~$200M, 90 Minto Road battery project from county review and refiled directly with the California Energy Commission under the state's opt-in certification program — a 2022 pathway that supersedes local land-use authority for storage above 200 MWh. Days later the county Board of Supervisors gave up and voted to defer its own draft BESS ordinance, which it will now hand the CEC as a non-binding "reference document" of local concerns. The fire narrative was the usual driver — post-Moss Landing fear, an orchard turned "industrial," neighbors at the fence line — but the tell is the venue shift: the CEC has now cleared two solar-plus-storage projects and one standalone BESS through opt-in, and developers facing a hostile county increasingly route around it entirely. The read for our storage work: the escape hatch is real, but it trades a county's goodwill for a state agency's stopwatch (a 270-day clock and at least two local hearings). Engagement doesn't end when the venue moves to Sacramento — it just gets harder to control.
  • Homework Assigned: in Comstock Township, Michigan, the opposition did the technical work before the developer got to the mic. Ahead of a July 23 information session and an August 13 public hearing on Copenhagen Infrastructure Partners' "Swan and Cygnet" storage proposal — roughly 140 acres along East Main Street, near an elementary school and a future STEM academy — the local school board has already commissioned its own independent risk analysis and formally voted to oppose the project on safety grounds. The township's engineer and fire department will present fire-risk and groundwater findings at the session, and the ~500-page application packet is already posted publicly, all while a township-wide moratorium on solar, wind, and battery ordinances runs through year-end. This is the opposition playbook maturing: bring your own expert, get the risk questions on the record early, and force the developer to answer facts instead of fears. The developer that walks into July 23 without its own fire-response and groundwater case already on the table is playing defense from the first minute.
Project Updates

Editor's note: under our seven-day freshness rule, an item runs only when the proceeding traces to a dated, in-window page. A second straight holiday-and-recess week left in-geography renewable local dockets nearly bare — this week's storage and transmission fights are up in Must Read and Radar, where they belong. The verified local proceedings that did land were, once again, data-center moratoriums. We're carrying the freshest three because they keep landing on our storage and solar work in the crossfire: these freezes are written to stop server halls, but the ordinance language rarely bothers to tell a battery from a data center. Frontlines returns Wednesday as the boards come back.

  • NCDavie Draws the Line: commissioners voted unanimously July 6 to adopt a one-year data-center moratorium — with no application even pending — after a hearing so packed it was moved into a high-school auditorium. One commissioner made the grid-cost case directly, citing PJM's own report that spot power in data-center-heavy Northern Virginia spiked to $2,500/MWh in an emergency against roughly $40 normally: "when you hear it's not going to affect your power bills... this indicates that it will."
  • GATerrell Gets Proactive: the county commission voted unanimously July 7 to adopt a six-month data-center moratorium, again with no proposal on file, explicitly to write setback, buffer, and utility standards "before developers come calling." The chair was candid about the pull — a large center could bring "$20 million or $30 million a year in property tax," potentially more than doubling county collections — but weighed it against water, noise, and grid strain. The proactive-ordinance model, spreading county to county across southwest Georgia.
  • MTMissoula Hits Pause: commissioners adopted an interim data-center freeze July 9 covering the unincorporated county (the city of Missoula excepted) — a national data point on how far outside our footprint the pause reflex has now traveled, and a reminder that the "no rulebook, so freeze everything" instinct doesn't stop at state lines or at the server-hall door.
