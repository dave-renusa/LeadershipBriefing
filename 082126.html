RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 43 · Friday, August 21, 2026 · Version 2

Friday morning, team.

Something shifted this week, and it isn't in our favor even though it looks like winning. The votes went our way in more rooms than usual — a Washington County board overrode its own zoning panel 8-6 to approve a 185-megawatt battery, a Knox County plan commission recommended storage through a packed and hostile room, and a Lexington council moved to crack open a solar ban its planning commission had just voted unanimously to keep. And none of it matters on the schedule our clients actually care about, because the thing standing between an approved project and a paid project is no longer the county — it's the wire. Southern California Edison alone is holding up 13 gigawatts of finished generation and storage on upgrades it hasn't built. Watch the second thing that moved this week too: in Tewksbury, in Du Bois Township, in Hadley, opponents who couldn't get to no spent their energy writing the conditions instead — 100 here, 38 there, every one of them a permanent operating cost. The fight has moved past whether. It's about the terms and the timeline now, and both of those are being set by people who aren't in the room with us.

Also On Our Radar
  • Cleared to Wait: the most important number in the industry this week has nothing to do with a zoning board. Batteries are piling up in interconnection queues coast to coast because the grid upgrades needed to plug them in aren't finished — and at Southern California Edison, unfinished upgrades have delayed roughly 13 gigawatts of new generation and storage, per state officials. Nationally the storage queue runs to something like 750 gigawatts. The bottleneck is physical and boring: transformers, circuit breakers, high-voltage equipment on multi-year lead times, chasing decades of stagnant investment. For our work this reframes the whole host-community conversation. Every benefit package we negotiate is priced against a construction start, and if that start slips three years past approval, the county's tax projections slip with it — and the supervisor who voted yes gets to explain an empty field to constituents who were promised revenue. Put the interconnection timeline in the host agreement narrative up front. The alternative is having it discovered.
  • Everything Must Go Through the Audit: Texas just froze the front of its own queue, and the knock-on lands squarely on renewables. Following Gov. Abbott's Aug. 3 directive, ERCOT told the Public Utility Commission it will audit roughly 250 to 300 pending projects — overwhelmingly data centers — before any additional large load is approved to connect, a process expected to take several months against a pending large-load book of about 474 gigawatts. BNEF's read is that the review could delay 49.8 GW of planned load and cost projects as much as $15 billion. The tactical read for us: the "phantom load" argument — that a large share of queued data-center requests are speculative duplicates shopping the same project across three utilities — has now been adopted by a Republican governor and a state grid operator. That argument is about to show up in county hearings in every one of our 30 states, aimed at our clients' load forecasts and at the utility studies our projects rely on. Know which version of it is true before someone else explains it.
  • Ban to Bandwidth: the cleanest example this year of a total prohibition getting negotiated down to an ordinance, and it happened in a county that had just voted the other way. Lexington's planning commission voted unanimously to uphold Fayette County's ban on rural solar; days later the Urban County Council moved a zoning amendment onto the docket that would allow solar on roughly 1% of county land with added restrictions, sending it to first reading Aug. 27. The live application behind it is a 797-acre Silicon Ranch proposal in east Fayette County. Study the frame, because it is the transferable part: not "let us build," but "let us build on one percent." A hard numeric cap gives a nervous elected official something to defend at the grocery store — it converts an unbounded fear about the county's character into a bounded, auditable number. Where we are staring at a ban, the cap is usually the way through.
  • Late Study, Real Penalty: a federal appeals court just left FERC's interconnection deadlines with teeth, over the objections of the people who have to meet them. The D.C. Circuit rejected arguments from grid operators and utilities that FERC's penalties for late interconnection studies were excessive and beyond the commission's authority, upholding the Order 2023-era reforms that put transmission providers on the clock. Read it next to the item at the top of this section: FERC can penalize a utility for studying a project late, but no order compels a circuit breaker to arrive faster. The reforms fix the paperwork half of the queue while the steel half gets worse. Useful in a hearing when someone claims the federal government has already solved interconnection — half of it, on paper.
  • Preemption's Bill Comes Due: Illinois is about to test what happens when a state takes local control away and the locals refuse to accept the trade. Will County has spent 2026 losing to its own state law — ordered by a circuit court to issue permits for six solar projects, then approving Earthrise Energy's 600-MW, 6,100-acre Pride of the Prairie project on a 12-8 vote in May, then rejecting another solar facility near an airport in July anyway. Now counsel for 16 Pride of the Prairie neighbors says he will challenge the constitutionality of the state law itself, and the county has formally petitioned the legislature to give local authority back. This is the cost of winning by preemption. Our clients get the permit and inherit a permanently hostile host community with nothing invested in the project's success — and a live constitutional case hanging over the asset. Where the statute does the work, the engagement still has to happen. Otherwise we are just building in enemy territory with a court order.
  • Money Still Says Yes: against every headline about tariffs and stalled queues, distributed solar just cleared a big financing. Dimension Energy locked in $857 million in additional capital — a $200 million corporate credit upsize led by Nuveen Energy Infrastructure Credit and HPS, plus a $657 million construction-to-term debt and tax equity package with MUFG, First Citizens, ING Capital and National Bank of Canada — supporting a 149-MW portfolio of 29 distributed projects across Illinois, New Jersey, New York, Pennsylvania and Virginia, against a target of 1 GW operating by 2028. Twenty-nine projects across five states is twenty-nine host communities, most of them small. Distributed solar tends to skip the community-engagement budget because each site is modest; the aggregate political exposure is not modest at all, and one bad siting fight in a Pennsylvania township travels to the other twenty-eight.
  • Ban Becomes Blueprint: Emery County walked back from the edge, and the mechanism is worth noting. After a 2-1 vote in May directed staff to start drafting an ordinance prohibiting new utility-scale solar county-wide — which drew a letter of reprimand from the Utah Attorney General's Office over how the meeting was handled — the commission has shifted from an outright ban toward detailed ordinances setting development requirements instead. Two lessons. First, the procedural challenge did real work here — a county that gets its hand slapped for how it reached a decision tends to reopen the decision. Second, "we'll regulate it hard" is the face-saving exit for a commission that has already told constituents it would ban it, and handing an elected official that exit is often more productive than beating them.

On the Frontlines
  • ILOverride in Du Bois: the Washington County Board approved the 185-MW Gatehouse Energy Storage Project on an 8-6 vote after its own zoning panel recommended denial, attaching 38 county conditions. The site is roughly 50 acres of a 60-acre agricultural parcel on Howard School Road in Du Bois Township, with 740 MWh of four-hour capacity. A board overriding its zoning commission is rare and worth reading closely: the conditions are what made the override defensible.
  • KYOne Percent Solution: Lexington's council docketed a zoning amendment allowing rural solar on about 1% of Fayette County land over a unanimous planning-commission recommendation to keep the existing ban, with first reading set for Aug. 27. Silicon Ranch's 797-acre east-county proposal is the project in the background.
  • MIBlendon Digs In: residents packed an Aug. 18 public hearing to oppose a battery storage system proposed at 8284 88th Ave. in the township's northwest corner, with testimony centered on fire risk and groundwater contamination — and on evacuation scenarios reaching schools, churches and local employers. The evacuation frame is doing more work than the fire-science frame in these rooms; it converts a technical risk into a community-disruption story that needs no expert.
  • MIOshtemo Extends: township officials moved to extend the local moratorium on energy storage construction while the board and planning commission work through standards, against the backdrop of Michigan's state siting law and the MPSC's role. Michigan remains the most active theater in the country for this specific maneuver — the rolling extension that never has to call itself a ban.
  • INKnox Says Yes Anyway: the Knox County Area Plan Commission voted to recommend approval of a battery storage system attached to an already-approved commercial solar project in Harrison Township, over strong resident opposition on lithium-ion fire risk and property impacts. Note the specific objection that nearly carried: that bolting storage onto an approved solar project is a material change to what the county originally said yes to. Any client adding a BESS to a permitted solar site should expect that argument and answer it before it is raised.
  • MAHadley Round Two: a stand-alone battery project on Breckenridge Road that planners rejected 18 months ago is back before the Planning Board while the developer's lawsuit over the original denial is still pending, with the hearing continuing Aug. 18. Litigating and re-applying at the same time is a legitimate strategy and a brutal one for community relations — the board knows it is being sued by the applicant standing at the podium.
  • PACorry Closes the Door: after the planning commission split 3-2 against recommending approval, Corry City Council unanimously denied Solar Flats LLC's conditional-use application for a 3-MW AC facility on about 16 acres along South Shady Avenue. The denial motion cited incompatibility with surrounding residential property and property-value impact — the two findings most likely to survive appeal, and the two our filings most often fail to rebut with evidence.
  • OHVinton on the Calendar: the Ohio Power Siting Board has scheduled proceedings on Hamden Energy LLC's proposed hybrid solar-and-storage facility of up to 149 MW in Vinton County. Ohio remains the state where roughly 5.3 GW of wind and solar has been blocked, and where a state-level approval is the beginning of the fight rather than the end.
  • UTEmery Reverses: the county commission has moved off an outright utility-scale solar prohibition toward a requirements-based ordinance, following an Attorney General's reprimand over the May meeting that launched the ban effort.
  • ILMadison Sets the Room: the county has scheduled a solar energy facility public hearing for Aug. 25 — worth watching given the county board's recent record of voting down a solar facility near Granite City, and given that the state law constraining these votes is now itself under constitutional attack a county away.
  • NYStill the Epicenter: New York continues to carry the large majority of active battery storage moratoriums nationally, with a fresh round of one-year pauses seated in Hamilton, Saratoga and Onondaga county towns running into mid-2027. The pattern to plan around: these expire on a calendar, and the six months before expiration is when the permanent ordinance actually gets written.
  • TXStorage as the Answer: one under-covered consequence of the ERCOT large-load audit is that it hands storage a new argument. A data center that can pair with or contract from batteries to blunt its grid impact has a materially better path through the review than one arriving with raw incremental load, which turns BESS from a nice-to-have into an approval strategy — and gives our clients' storage projects a customer story that plays in a Texas hearing room.
Plays of the Week
  • Tewksbury (MA) NeighborsIf You Can't Beat It, Write It: the single most instructive opposition performance we have seen this year, and it ended in an approval. Residents of Emerald Court, a 55-plus community beside the proposed 125-MW Hillman Energy Center, spent months filing testimony and public comment on fire risk, emergency response and proximity — and lost the top-line vote when the state siting board approved the project. Then look at what they actually walked away with: more than 100 conditions governing fire safety, emergency response, environmental protection, noise, construction and ongoing operation, roughly 50 of them lifted directly from an agreement the town had negotiated separately. The town manager's own framing was that residents banding together is what produced the condition list. The play, and it is spreading: a sophisticated opposition now treats the permit vote as a foregone conclusion and spends its capital on the condition set, because conditions are permanent, enforceable, and cost the operator money every year of the asset's life. The counter is not to fight conditions harder. It is to arrive with our own condition package already negotiated with the town, so the enforceable terms are ones our client can actually live with — the alternative is having them drafted by people whose goal is to make the project uneconomic.
  • Washington County (IL) BoardThirty-Eight Reasons to Vote Yes: the mirror image of Tewksbury, run by the applicant's side. Facing a zoning panel that had recommended denial — normally close to fatal — the county board approved the 185-MW Gatehouse project 8-6 and attached 38 conditions. Those two facts are one fact. A board member overriding their own zoning commission needs cover, and a long, specific, enforceable condition list is exactly that: it lets a yes vote be defended as "we didn't just approve it, we controlled it." The lesson for our clients is to stop treating conditions as damage. Going into a close vote, a pre-negotiated condition package handed to the swing members is the most reliable tool we have for converting a maybe — it gives them a script, and it takes the improvisation out of a room where improvisation kills projects.
  • Fayette County (KY) CouncilCap It to Crack It: a ban is a wall until someone gives it a number. Lexington's council took a unanimous planning-commission recommendation to preserve a rural solar prohibition and answered it with an amendment permitting solar on roughly 1% of county land with added restrictions, moving it to first reading Aug. 27. The mechanism is the percentage. Opposition to solar in an agricultural county is almost never about a single project; it is a fear about cumulative conversion of farmland, and that fear is unbounded, which is why it wins. A hard cap bounds it. It converts "they'll pave the whole county" into an arithmetic question an official can answer publicly and be held to. Where we are working a jurisdiction that has already said never, the percentage cap deserves to be the first offer, not the last concession.
  • Will County (IL) OppositionEscalate the Venue: a case study in what organized opponents do after they run out of local remedies — and a warning about how preemption wins age. Beaten at the county level by a state law that strips local boards of the power to refuse compliant solar, counsel for 16 neighbors of the approved 600-MW Pride of the Prairie project is moving to challenge the constitutionality of the statute itself, while the county petitions the legislature to restore local authority and keeps voting no on other projects regardless of what the law says. Three venues, one campaign: courts, legislature, and continued local defiance that forces every developer to litigate. Our clients should read this as the true cost of a permit won purely on preemption — the approval is real, the hostility compounds, and the asset carries legal risk for years. Where the statute gives us the right to build, the engagement is what gives us the ability to operate.

Approvals came easier this week than they have in months, and not one of them shortened a schedule. The counties are learning to say yes with conditions attached; the grid is saying not yet regardless; and the opposition has figured out that the condition sheet outlives the vote. Bring our own terms to the table before someone else writes them, and put the interconnection date in the room before a supervisor finds it on their own.
