Friday morning, all.

The moratorium has stopped reading the difference between a solar farm and a server hall. On Thursday in Lyon County, Kansas, commissioners paused new utility-scale solar in the same sitting they paused data centers and batteries — and they did it with an Evergy solar project presenting to the board minutes before the vote. That is the clearest case this week of renewables getting swept into a data-center backlash they didn't cause, it leads Radar below, and it is the seam we keep telling clients to work: the pause is only a blanket until someone on the dais has to explain why a farmer's solar lease got lumped in with a hyperscaler. Nobody in Emporia made them separate the two. That's on us to force.

Hold that against the mirror image out of Illinois. Where a state has stripped local boards of the power to say no to solar, opponents have stopped arguing the merits and started hunting for the one lever the statute left on the table. In Will County they found it — aviation safety — and a 15-6 "no" that would be plainly illegal on farmland-character grounds may well survive because the legislature never imagined a solar array at the end of an airpark runway. Both of this week's Plays turn on the same insight from opposite sides: whoever reads the rulebook most carefully, for its gaps or for what it expects of you, wins the room. One side is reading it for the exemption. The smart developers, per a new study out of Indiana, are reading it for the engagement it demands — and showing up two years early. Both are in Plays below.

Also On Our Radar
  • Guilt by Association: the moratorium that can't tell a battery from a server hall added solar to its list this week. Lyon County, Kansas commissioners voted unanimously Thursday to pause new utility-scale solar, battery storage and data centers — a six-month freeze on storage and data centers through Jan. 22, and a separate solar moratorium through Dec. 1 — minutes after Evergy's VP of development pitched the board on a proposed Emporia Solar Project promising more than $60 million in property tax over 30 years, no eminent domain, minimal water use and no new overhead transmission. The tell that this is a renewables story, not a data-center one, was a solar developer in the room: Jason Edwards of Ottawa praised the county's existing solar code and asked, pointedly, why solar was folded into a data-center pause at all. Resident Anna DeLong gave the freeze its motto — "We want the zoning to guide the development. We don't want the development to guide the zoning." A defensible instinct aimed at the wrong target: the county has already drafted 16 pages of data-center rules and concedes its solar code is fine. This is the sweep to fight upstream — a fully-financed, low-water, no-eminent-domain solar project stalled six months by language written for a threat it isn't. When the pause is drafted, be the voice that makes the board carve solar back out of it.
  • Poles Apart: the West Virginia transmission fight we've tracked through two issues of open houses just converted crowd anger into government action. The Lewis County Commission voted Tuesday to formally oppose Valley Link's 260-mile Valley North line, and — the part that matters for the docket — Commission President Agnes Queen said the county intends to become an intervenor if Valley Link files with the state Public Service Commission, giving it standing in every future hearing. Queen's framing is the opposition's whole case in one line: "we're sending all of our resources to another state… the citizens of our county and our state have to flip the bill." Lewis didn't move alone: the Weston City Council passed its own resolution Monday, and the Upshur County Commission adopted one last week — three governments stacking formal opposition into the record before a single route is filed, all citing the same complaint that the open houses "left residents with unanswered questions." This is the renewables story most developers still forget to staff: no wires, no interconnection, no project. The corridor is soft, the filing is a year out, and the opposition is already assembling the intervenor roster it will bring to the PSC in 2027. Whoever wants that line built needed to be in Weston in June.
Project Updates

Editor's note: under our seven-day freshness rule, an item runs only when the proceeding traces to a dated, in-window page. The recess kept in-geography renewable local dockets thin again — this week's live renewable fights are carried up in Radar and Plays, where they belong (the Kansas solar sweep, the West Virginia transmission fight, and the two engagement lessons below). What did move in-footprint was the data-center pause wave, and it moved in three states at once. Three verified proceedings below, each traced to a July 21–22 page. The through-line: the pause is now the reflexive first move even for a project that arrives carrying its own clean generation.

  • NMSpaceport, Grounded: Sierra County commissioners walked into a Tuesday meeting to vote on a resolution opposing large data centers and walked out having amended it, on the spot, into an 18-month moratorium — a surprise hardening after more than an hour of near-unanimous public opposition. The trigger was a proposed solar-powered data center at Spaceport America, on state trust land, and the detail is the lesson: even a project packaged with its own clean generation now draws the same reflexive freeze, because the load is what the room fears, not the fuel. Sierra joins Santa Fe and Socorro on New Mexico's growing pause list. For any developer pitching a co-located solar-plus-load deal as the friendly version of the story, Truth or Consequences just showed the friendly version gets paused too.
  • NCPilot Mountain Pauses: Surry County commissioners unanimously extended a June 60-day data-center pause into a full two-year moratorium Monday night, and required the board to seat a steering committee to study the issue — visit a data center, talk to power providers — before it writes an ordinance. County Attorney Howard Jones' framing was studiously neutral: "a complicated issue… a lot of polarized information out there." Neighboring Yadkin and Alamance counties have both set August 17 hearings on their own pauses. A two-year clock is long enough to matter to a pipeline, and the steering-committee model is the one to watch: it's where a county's permanent posture on all large load, renewables included, actually gets drafted.
  • GAThirty, Then One-Eighty: Cherokee County commissioners unanimously approved a 30-day data-center moratorium Tuesday with no application pending, and Community Development Director Brantley Day was candid that it's a placeholder: staff will return in August to request a 180-day extension carrying the pause into mid-February. Metro Atlanta's Chamblee City Council paused data-center applications the same day. The tactic here is the honest one — a short freeze declared explicitly to buy time to draft, with the real duration telegraphed in advance. When a jurisdiction with no rulebook and no pending project moves this deliberately, the ordinance drafting is the project, and the engagement window is open now, while the committee is still reading other counties' codes.

Plays of the Week
  • Indiana's Solar DevelopersBe in the County Before You Need Anything From It: the most useful finding in this week's Loyola study of Indiana solar siting isn't about panels or tax base — it's about presence. Indiana was the nation's No. 3 state for new solar last year, behind only Texas and California, yet researcher Thomas Brelage found that the variable separating approvals from denials in his survey of 22 local officials was how the developer showed up. The officials singled out the developer who went "above and beyond, showing up at county fairs, county hearings, trying their best to be involved… not just this developer you hear from once in a while." His conclusion, and the play: "it's important to be focused on the educational aspect, so that when it is time for your county hearing, it isn't the first time you've stepped foot in the county." The tactic to steal is a calendar, not a checkbook — Brelage was blunt that developers who "just throw money around" still lose. Map the fair season, the FFA banquet, the county commission's open comment periods, the local paper's letters column, and be a known quantity there for eighteen months before a rezoning is on any agenda. In a home-rule state where Solar United Neighbors counts 11 counties with outright solar bans and only 24 genuinely favorable, familiarity is the entire product. The developers winning in the nation's No. 3 solar state are the ones who stopped treating the hearing as the first meeting.
  • Meadow Creek AirparkFind the Exemption the Mandate Forgot: here's the opposition running the same rulebook logic from the other side, and it's the sharpest anti-preemption play we've seen. Illinois's siting statute was written to take denial off the table for rural solar — county boards can't say no on the usual grounds. So the neighbors of the Meadow Creek Airpark in Monee Township stopped fighting on the grounds the statute closed and went hunting for one it left open: aviation safety. Their argument wasn't "we don't want it" — that loses — it was "the legislature never contemplated a solar array at the end of an amateur runway, and a crash landing into panels is a hazard the mandate doesn't speak to." The FAA had signed off; it didn't matter. The pitch worked well enough to produce a 15-6 board rejection and, more tellingly, a record the county's own assistant state's attorney concedes the statute "probably never considered." The lesson runs both directions. For opposition, the play is to read a preemption law not for what it forbids but for the categories it omits — schools, airstrips, floodplains, aquifers — and to build the whole case inside the omission. For our side, the counter is to pressure-test every contested parcel against the statute's silences before you option it, because that silence is where the next organized "no" gets built. A clean setback won't save a project the opposition can reframe as a safety exemption the mandate forgot to foreclose.

When the mandate has a gap, the fight moves into the gap. Read the pause for what it sweeps in.
