RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 55 · Wednesday, September 30, 2026

Good morning, team.

On Monday, Montgomery County, Indiana commissioners voted 3-0 to deny ENGIE's Copper Box Solar rezoning, and the motion quoted the county's comprehensive plan. The same kind of document decided several other rooms. Lexington's mayor vetoed farmland solar that the council had passed 8-7 and pointed to the stated purpose of the agricultural zone. Two Kent County, Delaware commissioners abstained on a solar farm because they believed they could not legally vote no. Darlington County, South Carolina rejected a moratorium 5-3 and started tightening its setbacks the same night. And Vanderburgh County, Indiana approved a $200 million battery beside a substation with no fight at all.

Deep Dive

Boxed Out

On Monday, Sept. 28, the Montgomery County, Indiana Board of Commissioners voted 3-0 to deny Copper Box Solar's petition to rezone more than 2,600 acres of farmland in Madison and Union townships to the county's agricultural overlay district. The developer is ENGIE North America. According to the Journal Review, the project covered about 2,620 acres, with up to 825 of them holding panels or related equipment. It was described this summer as a 200-megawatt, roughly $300 million investment with about 250 construction jobs.

This was ENGIE's second attempt. In March the county's Advisory Plan Commission voted 6-2 against the first petition after more than three hours of public comment. ENGIE came back with a revised application: a new site layout, more screening, more sound mitigation, and a tax case that it said more than doubled the project's payments to the county after a change in state depreciation law. In August the plan commission voted 6-2 against it again. The margin did not move.

The commissioners' reasons are the part to study. Commissioner Dan Guard's motion to deny cited specific parts of the comprehensive plan, including its goals of preserving farmland and discouraging large industrial solar farms. The motion also said the rezoning was inconsistent with the county's rural character and agricultural assets, and that statements from nearby landowners supported the conclusion that the project would not conserve property values. Guard told WCDQ the request was "inconsistent with the comprehensive plan" and that productive agriculture remained "the most desirable use" of the land. Commissioner Jim Fulwider said the plan makes clear the proposed use is not wanted in Montgomery County.

ENGIE's revisions answered the objections a neighbor raises: what it looks like, what it sounds like, what it pays. None of them could answer the objection the commissioners actually used, because that objection was already written into the county's planning document before the first application was filed. ENGIE's lead project manager, Tom Schoder, called the vote "a missed opportunity for the county to get significant economic development." Under plan commission rules, ENGIE has to wait a year to refile on the same property unless the proposal is substantially changed.

We have seen the same document work in the other direction. A month ago a Virginia judge overrode a 5-1 solar denial because that county's comprehensive plan said yes. A plan that supports solar can carry a project past a hostile board, and a plan that discourages it gives a board a reason to deny that does not depend on anything the developer did. Both outcomes came from reading the plan as the governing text.

The Takeaway

Read the comprehensive plan before the first landowner meeting, not before the hearing. If the plan names large solar as something to discourage, a better site layout will not change the vote, and the work that matters is either amending the plan or choosing a different route to approval. If the plan is silent or favorable, quote it in the application and make the board explain why it is departing from its own document. Copper Box also shows what a second attempt is worth when the first loss was on policy: the plan commission voted 6-2 both times. Before a client spends money on a revised filing, we should be able to say which vote the revision is meant to change, and why.

Also On Our Radar
  • One Vote Short in the Bluegrass: On Sept. 18, Lexington Mayor Linda Gorton vetoed a zoning amendment the council passed 8-7 on Sept. 10 that would have allowed solar installations over five acres as a conditional use in Fayette County's agricultural zones. The ordinance capped such projects at 1% of county land, about 1,800 acres, and required the sites to keep supporting farming or grazing. Gorton said "once that door is opened, it will be hard to close." Her office noted that the Planning Commission and planning staff had both opposed it as contrary to the purpose of the agricultural zone. An override takes nine of 15 votes, one more than the ordinance got, and the council next meets Oct. 6.
  • Abstaining Instead of Voting No: Kent County, Delaware's Levy Court approved Sunrise Solar's 25-acre community solar project south of Kenton with four yes votes, two abstentions and one absence. The two commissioners who abstained cited legal concerns about voting no, and two of the yes votes said they had reservations. State Sen. Dave Lawson and about 10 residents opposed it over soil and water contamination, noise and the rural setting. The project engineer argued the soil improves after 25 years without herbicides and pesticides. When the code leaves no lawful basis to deny, an unhappy board member has few options besides abstaining.
  • Eight Days of Testimony in Peoria County: The Peoria County Zoning Board of Appeals is back in session this week on Four Creeks Wind, Repsol Renewables' application for up to 113 turbines across about 27,000 acres in Brimfield, Millbrook, Princeville and Jubilee townships. Hearing days ran Sept. 15-18 and resumed Sept. 28, scheduled through Oct. 1, in a hotel ballroom with a livestream. Supporters led with union construction jobs and school tax revenue. Attorneys for 34 landowners argued the application is incomplete, without final turbine specifications or road-use agreements, and residents raised shadow flicker and noise from 657-foot machines. The zoning board makes a recommendation and the county board decides.
  • Next to the Substation, No Fight: Vanderburgh County, Indiana commissioners this week approved the agreements for a roughly 200-megawatt battery beside the Elliott Station substation in Scott Township. The developer is an affiliate of RWE Clean Energy and plans to invest about $200 million. The Board of Zoning Appeals had already approved it 5-0, and nearby residents were notified and briefed before the hearing. RWE will pay the county $250,000 for local development projects and cover the county's negotiating costs. Construction has to start by December 2029 or the agreement ends. The county attorney's description: batteries "need to be next to a substation, which this one is."
  • The Judge Says No to the Rules: On Sept. 25, Pulaski County, Arkansas Judge Barry Hyde vetoed the data center permitting ordinance the Quorum Court had passed 11-4 three days earlier. He wrote that it would put the county in "unnecessary legal and financial risk," that it conflicts with state law and bypassed the planning commission, and that the county is not equipped to run the permitting system it creates. An override takes nine votes. The sponsor, Justice of the Peace Julie Blackwood, told the Arkansas Times, "we'll override the veto."
  • REAP What They Froze: On Sept. 28, Earthjustice, the Environmental Law & Policy Center and installer Wolf River Electric sued the USDA in federal court in D.C. over its August 2025 decision to stop funding solar through the Rural Energy for America Program. The complaint calls the policy arbitrary and capricious and cites an internal email in which an agency administrator, asked to support the department's anti-solar claims, wrote "let's let the comms shop find . . . the rationale behind their statistics." Nearly 70% of REAP awards from 2011 through early 2025 went to solar. This is the week's one federal item.
On the Frontlines
  • SCNo Pause, Tighter Rules: On Sept. 10, Darlington County Council voted 5-3 against a nine-month moratorium on new solar and wind, then passed first reading of an ordinance setting a 100-foot setback from property lines, 600 feet from homes, vegetative buffers and a decommissioning bond of at least $50,000 or 125% of costs. The trigger is Palladium Energy's 417-acre Stripe Solar project in Kellytown. Participating landowners carried the room: "This land has been passed down through generations, and we want to preserve it for our children." One of them warned that new restrictions "could make our project unworkable."
  • OHStorage on the Solar Site: On Sept. 17, the Ohio Power Siting Board authorized Hecate Energy Highland to build a battery of up to 300 megawatts alongside its existing solar facility in Highland County. The project sits on 89 leased acres, with permanent facilities on 13, and includes noise barriers and a stormwater basin.
  • AZFrom 1,500 Feet to 100: Surprise City Council voted 7-0 for a variable battery setback of 100 to 500 feet from homes, decided project by project, replacing a 1,500-foot requirement. Surprise is where the 2019 McMicken battery explosion injured firefighters. Speakers mostly supported the reduction and argued newer systems are safer.
  • NYPause With an Application Pending: On Sept. 16, the Dunkirk Common Council unanimously approved a six-month moratorium on new battery storage applications at the request of its planning board, which is reviewing a Gridi Development proposal behind a Brigham Road warehouse. The city will use the time to review the state's model battery law. One council member said the pause is "not intended to stop the process they have entered into, but make sure we have our ducks in a row."
  • PAChallenging the Ordinance Itself: ForeFront Power took its appeal to the Washington Township, Northampton County zoning hearing board on Sept. 28. The zoning officer rejected its plan for 8 megawatts across about 80 acres in March, ruling that solar can only be an accessory use in the agricultural zone. ForeFront is asking for a special exception and arguing the ordinance unlawfully excludes solar. The township rewrote the ordinance in April to allow solar as a principal use, after the application was rejected.
  • MDThe Sign Was the Notice: Howard County Council will take up Bill 59-2026, a one-year pause on new solar approvals, on Oct. 5. The sponsor says it responds to Maryland's 2025 Renewable Energy Certainty Act, which lets smaller projects bypass some county review. The resident who prompted it said she learned about a project near her home from a posted sign. The county executive's office warned that a moratorium could halt projects already underway.
  • MOAll Four, No Project: On Sept. 8, Cedar County commissioners imposed a 12-month moratorium on large solar, wind, battery storage and data centers outside city limits, covering anything over 25 kilowatts, with a possible six-month extension. No project has been proposed. Residents objected that the county has no voter-approved planning and zoning, and all three commissioners declined interviews.
  • IDSecond Thoughts on a Ban: Bannock County, which banned utility-scale solar and wind 2-1 in March 2024, is set to vote this fall on a new ordinance that would allow solar, wind and nuclear. Commission Chair Jeff Hough, who dissented on the ban, said, "We cannot deny the entire county the opportunity to exercise their property rights." A commissioner who backed the ban says his concerns about solar are "not as big" as two years ago. Idaho Power projects a gigawatt of new demand by 2030.
  • WVThe Hearing Date Holds: The West Virginia Public Service Commission refused an 11-week delay in the MidAtlantic Resiliency Link case, which NextEra and commission staff had jointly requested after route changes affected landowners who had not been on the line before. The evidentiary hearing stays on Oct. 26 to Nov. 2.
  • VAThe Board Overrules Its Planners: On Sept. 23, Caroline County supervisors voted 4-2 to rezone 916 acres from rural preservation to industrial for Luck Stone's Carmel Technology and Enterprise Center, a month after the planning commission recommended denial 3-1 because no end user had been named. The proffers include a church community center, $300,000 for a park splash pad, $100,000 for career and technical education and a five-mile public trail. A local bishop told the board, "Reedy Church doesn't need to be managed into stagnation."

Montgomery County denied a 200-megawatt project by quoting its own plan. Lexington's mayor vetoed solar by citing what the agricultural zone is for. Two Delaware commissioners abstained because their code gave them no way to say no. For each active file this week, pull the comprehensive plan and the zone's purpose statement, and find the sentence the board would read aloud.
