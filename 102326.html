RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 63 · Friday, October 23, 2026

Friday morning, team.

An Oklahoma judge put the week's most useful sentence on the record. Wagoner County cannot deny a conditional-use permit just because a solar farm is not "affirmatively beneficial" to the community, he ruled, when the applicant meets the ordinance's conditions. That standard cuts both ways. Where a county has written its conditions down, a developer that meets them can win in court. Where it has not, the board keeps its discretion. Almost everything else this week was a fight over what gets written down: two New York boards and three Midwest counties paused batteries until they have rules, an Iowa county is rewriting its solar ordinance to fit a deal it already signed with a utility, and in Ohio a campaign that outspent the other side 20 to 1 still could not undo a county ban.

Also On Our Radar
  • Beneficial Is Not the Test: District Judge Doug Kirkley ordered the Wagoner County Commission to grant a conditional-use permit for Persica Solar's Porter Solar Farm, nearly 5,000 acres and an estimated $336 million near Porter, Oklahoma. The commission had put the request on its agenda five times before denying it in June 2025. The county may still impose reasonable conditions. NextEra Energy Resources, which is tied to the project, welcomed the ruling.
  • Fourth in Line: The Justice Department on Tuesday asked a federal court in Washington to send New England Wind's construction plan back to Interior, in a suit brought by the Nantucket group ACK for Whales. Avangrid's project would have up to 129 turbines and 2,600 megawatts, and construction has not started. It is the fourth offshore project the administration has targeted this way. Sen. Sheldon Whitehouse warned the move could sink September's bipartisan permitting deal, and Interior says it is following a briefing schedule, not changing policy.
  • Hidden Valley Says No Again: Pinal County's planning and zoning commission voted 5-3 on Oct. 15 to recommend denying the amendments needed for Horseshoe Energy, a 500-megawatt battery and gas plant about 10 miles southwest of Maricopa, Arizona. The commission rejected an earlier version on Sept. 17. Developer Epic Star Energy says it will return with a revised plan.
  • Starlight, Served: The Back Country Community Alliance for Responsible Development sued San Diego County and the developers of Starlight Solar on Oct. 15. The project covers 588 acres near Boulevard with an 868-megawatt-hour battery, and supervisors approved it in September. The suit says the environmental review did not adequately study battery fire, evacuation routes or water supply. The project's land use consultant says the suit lacks merit and the parties will try to settle.
  • Maryland Buys 440, Passes on 500: Maryland's Public Service Commission conditionally selected 440 megawatts of storage for its first round of capacity-credit awards: the 400-megawatt Chalk Point project in Prince George's County and the 40-megawatt Jade Meadow III in Garrett County, on flat 15-year terms. It declined the 500-megawatt Oystercatcher project in Harford County on cost-effectiveness and ratepayer grounds and encouraged it to apply again in Round 2.
  • Vermilion Votes Yes, Twice: The Vermilion County Board in Illinois approved two 5-megawatt community solar facilities, near Rossville on 18-1 and near Georgetown on 14-5, while also voting on a series of amendments to its solar and wind ordinances that drew mixed reviews.
On the Frontlines
  • NYLake Placid Follows North Elba: The Lake Placid Village Board voted 4-0 on Oct. 20 to adopt a one-year battery storage moratorium matching the town's. The two share a planning board and land use code. The village has received no applications, and officials say the pause is not opposition to green energy.
  • IAThe Ordinance Follows the MOU: Page County supervisors are amending the county's solar ordinance to conform with its memorandum of understanding with MidAmerican Energy for the proposed Nodaway Valley Solar Project. Hearings are set for Oct. 27, Nov. 5 and Nov. 10. Supervisor Todd Maher, who voted against the MOU, says the county is losing local control.
  • INKnox Signs Over Objections: Knox County commissioners on Oct. 20 approved economic development agreements for Edwardsport Solar Park, 3-0, and Unser Solar near North Knox High School, 2-0 with one abstention. Terms include 300- to 500-foot setbacks, emergency management training, set construction hours and road work. Nearly every resident who spoke was opposed.
  • NERules for One, a Pause for the Other: Custer County supervisors on Oct. 13 adopted data center zoning rules and a 12-month moratorium on battery storage applications. The battery resolution cites fire safety, emergency response and setbacks. The data center rules require community benefit agreements for facilities of 10 megawatts or more.
  • KSRiley Adds Six Months: Riley County commissioners on Oct. 15 extended their moratorium on data centers and battery storage to May 14, 2027. It had been set to expire Nov. 12.
  • ILCharleston Pairs the Pause: Charleston's city council approved a temporary ban on data centers and battery storage 5-0, putting both uses under a single pause.
  • ILWill County Asks for the Pen Back: The Will County Board amended its state legislative agenda on Oct. 7 to ask the General Assembly to "return local control in siting solar and battery storage facilities."
  • KYStarfire Gets Its Hearing: The state Siting Board holds a public comment hearing tonight at 5 p.m. at the Perry County Fiscal Court in Hazard on Starfire's 200-megawatt merchant solar facility across Perry and Knott counties, moved from Oct. 15.
  • VALoudoun Waits Until November: Loudoun supervisors moved a proposed pause of up to 12 months on legislative applications for data centers and substations from Oct. 20 to Nov. 17, after the county attorney advised that a moratorium on approvals would not be legal.
  • USREAP, Rewritten: USDA's new rules for the Rural Energy for America Program "disincentivize solar and wind systems on cropland" and withdrew promised grants from farmers. The Environmental Law and Policy Center is a co-plaintiff in a suit filed in late September, and a few grants have since been reinstated.
Plays of the Week
  • Richland County Citizens for Property Rights and Job DevelopmentThe Money Became the Story: The coalition trying to overturn Richland County, Ohio's wind and solar ban in 11 townships took more than $400,000 in outside cash and in-kind support, including $250,000 for ads from the NRDC Action Fund, and spent more than 20 times what the other side did. Voters kept the ban with nearly 53 percent. The farmland PAC on the other side raised about $18,000. In a county fight, large outside spending can turn into a local argument against you.
  • Persica SolarArgue the Standard, Not the Panels: The developer won in court by arguing that once it met the county ordinance's conditions, the commission could not deny the permit on the ground that the project was not affirmatively beneficial. Every condition a client meets should be documented on the record, because that record is what a judge will read.
  • MidAmerican EnergySign the MOU First: MidAmerican reached a memorandum of understanding with Page County, and the county is now rewriting its solar ordinance to conform to it, with setbacks the biggest change. Once the deal is in place, the ordinance hearings are about matching its terms. The cost is that a dissenting supervisor now has a local-control argument.
  • Back Country Community AllianceSue on Fire, Exits and Water: The group's suit against Starlight Solar targets battery fire, evacuation routes and water supply in the environmental review rather than solar itself. Those are the three issues to cover in detail, with the fire department on the record, before any battery-paired project reaches a vote.

The Wagoner ruling and the battery pauses point the same way: the county's written rules decide more of these fights than the hearing does. Get into the room while those rules are being drafted, and make sure every condition a client meets is on the record.
