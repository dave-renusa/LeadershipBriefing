Volume 26 · Friday, July 10, 2026

Friday morning, team.

Slow week on the calendar, loud week in the signal. The July 4 holiday and the mid-summer board recess kept most of our 23 states' planning commissions dark, so the docket of contested renewable hearings thinned to a trickle. But the stories that did land all point the same direction, and it's the one we keep selling: on a hostile site, the project that wins is the one that did the engineering on the community, not just the megawatts.

Three renewable wins prove it this week. In Arizona, a battery project that died 2-3 last year came back and cleared the board 4-0 — because the developer subtracted its way to yes, dropping the solar, shrinking the footprint, and buying the town's utility into the deal. In Colorado and New Mexico, a small tribe brought 270 megawatts of solar and 180 of storage online despite a federal permitting freeze, on the strength of a fifteen-year plan and a permit locked in the last days before the window shut. And in Texas, the money kept flowing into batteries — a 920-MWh ERCOT portfolio moved to construction even as the credit cliff spooked the rest of the market. Both plays are below.

Standing items, so we don't re-litigate them. Loudoun's Golden-to-Mars monopoles: no new vote since Wednesday — the school board still faces its July 20 deadline to volunteer an alternate corridor. Texas's $33B STEP 765-kV pause: no update. New York's statewide data-center moratorium: still no signature or veto from Gov. Hochul. New York's BESS-moratorium lead: no new in-window town vote we hadn't logged. North Carolina's NCUC solar freeze: SELC's reconsideration petition still pending, no ruling. If any breaks, you get a full item.

Also On Our Radar
  • The Money Still Likes Batteries: while the credit cliff froze the solar side, storage kept clearing capital. Frontier Power USA exercised its selection right on four Texas BESS projects — Blanquilla, Aransas Pass, Nash and Wallis, roughly 230 MW / 920 MWh — converting Stella Energy Solutions' late-stage ERCOT pipeline onto its platform on July 7, backed by Cerberus, Hudson Bay, and KKR-structured financing and American-made Eos long-duration cells. OBBBA left storage's tax credit intact, and it shows: the flexibility that batteries sell to a stressed grid is exactly what the capital is chasing. For our storage clients, the read is that the offtake and the money are there — the binding constraint is the same one it always is, the permit and the wire.
  • Industrial Land, Not Panels: Worcester County, Maryland commissioners voted 7-0 on July 7 to oppose a 4.95-MW solar project near Pocomoke City — cutting off the applicant's engineer before he could present, because the motion to reject was already on the floor. The tell isn't the vote; it's the reasoning. The site is zoned I-1 Light Industrial, where solar is permitted by right, and one commissioner said the quiet part out loud: "Why in the world would you turn over all your industrial land for solar panels?... that, dollar-for-dollar, brings no revenue to the county at all." The new opposition frame isn't glare or farmland anymore — it's opportunity cost, the argument that a solar array squats on land a county was banking for jobs and tax base. Expect to hear it wherever a project lands on industrial or growth-boundary ground, and get the tax-and-jobs comparison into the record before the other side frames it.
  • The Moratorium Reflex, Six States Deep: the data-center backlash hardened into ordinance this week. DeKalb County, GA extended its moratorium into early 2027 after rejecting zoning in June; Prince George's County, MD passed a two-year hyperscale pause; and Greenwood County, SC voted 4-3 for a one-year freeze and barred data centers from industrial districts — with no application even pending. The pattern is the one that keeps catching our storage and solar work in the crossfire: when a community has no rulebook for a fast-moving use, it reaches for a freeze, and the freeze rarely bothers to tell a battery from a server farm. Plays of the Week
  • NextEra & SRPSubtract to Win: a battery project that died at the Pinal County, AZ board 2-3 in April 2025 came back and cleared it 4-0 on July 1 — with all three supervisors who'd voted no flipping to yes. The tactic was subtraction. NextEra cut the solar entirely, halved the footprint from 160 acres to 68.77, pulled the boundary 500-plus feet off the nearest road, and volunteered a 10-foot opaque wall wrapped with mature trees and oleander screening. Then it changed who benefits: Salt River Project's offtake was extended so Coolidge's own public-power utility (ED2) could buy a share at wholesale, and a $600,000 community fund put city projects first in line. The fire narrative — the same 45-minute fight that helps kill these projects — was met head-on by the regional fire chief's own support letter. Note the caveats, because they're the lesson too: the City of Coolidge and the nearest neighbor stayed opposed ("a farming and family community, not a solar wasteland"), and SRP had to publicly pledge "we will not pick any more projects in this area" to relieve the saturation pressure. When a site is right but the room is hostile, you don't push harder — you take things off the table until the "no" votes run out of reasons.
  • Ute Mountain Ute TribePlay the Long Game, Then Beat the Clock: against a federal permitting freeze that has stalled roughly a third of the nation's planned capacity, a 2,000-member tribe brought 270 MW of solar and 180 MW of storage to groundbreaking on its land in northwest New Mexico. The engagement lesson spans fifteen years, not one hearing. Starting in 2015 with a DOE-facilitated strategic energy plan, the tribe went small first — rooftop, then a 1-MW array powering its casino — building the track record that "had a lot more developers coming to us after that." It sited Foxtail Flats at a transmission crossroads a demolished coal plant left behind, lined up its buyers early (Los Alamos County's utility for half the output; a Meta data center near Albuquerque for the rest), and — the decisive move — locked federal permits "in the final days before US policy changed last summer." The framing to steal: treat a hostile policy season as "wintertime… not a season for building, but a season for research and planning." Relationships and a decade-long plan are what let a small player pour concrete while better-financed projects sit frozen.

When the room says no, take something off the table.
