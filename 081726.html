RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 42 · Monday, August 17, 2026 · Version 2

Happy Monday, all.

The rooms have stopped arguing about whether. They're arguing about the spec sheet. Ohio's largest solar project cleared the state Supreme Court on every objection but one and is still waiting, two years on, because nobody drew a picture of the substations. A Michigan township spent Thursday night going line by line through a 38-page ordinance that sets battery setbacks at 2,000 feet from a school and puts a PFAS monitoring regime on the applicant. A Kalamazoo-area commission heard that an unmitigated battery site would run 63 decibels at a property line against a 55-decibel cap, and the answer on the table is a 14-foot wooden wall three-fifths of a mile long. In Colorado, 1,053 megawatts of finished wind sits idle over 550 feet of easement one landowner won't sell. None of that is ideology. All of it is documentation — renderings, decibels, acreage, bond math, host payments — and every one of those numbers is an exhibit somebody either filed or didn't. Today's Must Read is the clearest version of the lesson we've seen all year.

Must Read

Draw It or Lose It

Oak Run is 800 megawatts of solar and 300 megawatts of storage in Madison County, Ohio — the largest solar project in a state that has spent the past several years perfecting the art of saying no. The Ohio Power Siting Board approved it in March 2024. The county and three townships appealed in October 2024. And on May 26 of this year, the Ohio Supreme Court worked through the objections one by one and rejected all of them but a single item: the board had not adequately addressed the project's visual impacts. Not the water. Not the wildlife. Not the battery safety, which three justices would also have sent back. Six of seven justices agreed the permit failed on one narrow ground — the applicant and the board had never produced photo simulations or sketches showing what the three substations would look like from public vantage points.

Read that again, because it is the entire argument for how our clients should be staffing an application. A project of this size carries thousands of pages of testimony, modeling, and expert reports. It survived every substantive challenge the opposition brought. And it has now spent nearly two years in appellate limbo over a set of renderings that a visualization consultant could have produced in a few weeks for a rounding error against the capital stack. The opposition didn't beat Oak Run on the merits. It found the one exhibit that wasn't in the file and made the court look at the hole.

The board took testimony on the remand July 28. Briefing closed Friday, Aug. 14. Staff filed a brief Aug. 7 recommending the board confirm the permit, which is the likeliest outcome — but the board is under no deadline, and Ohio is the state where roughly 5.3 gigawatts of wind and solar have been blocked in recent years. Two years and counting, on a project that already won.

What travels out of Ohio is not an Ohio rule. It's a method. Opposition counsel in contested siting cases has gotten very good at reading an application the way a defense attorney reads a chain of custody: not looking for the strongest argument, looking for the gap. Visual simulation is the current favorite because it is cheap for the opponent to demand, expensive for the applicant to retrofit under appeal, and intuitive to a judge who has never sited a substation. Next year it will be something else — glare studies, decommissioning bond escalation, groundwater baselines, first-responder training records. The specific exhibit rotates. The vulnerability doesn't.

The Takeaway

Oak Run won on the merits and lost two years anyway. Three instructions. One: run a gap audit before you file, not after you're appealed. Have someone whose job is to be hostile read the application cold and list every question a commissioner could ask that the file cannot answer — then close those gaps while it's still cheap. The visual simulation Oak Run didn't produce would have cost less than a month of appellate briefing. Two: treat visual impact as a first-class exhibit, today. Six of seven Ohio justices found the whole permit deficient on renderings alone. If our clients' filings across the 30 states don't include photo simulations of substations, inverter blocks, and BESS containers from named public vantage points, they are carrying the exact defect that has held up Ohio's biggest project for two years. Three: the appeal is the second hearing, so build for it in the first. A record assembled to persuade a board is not the same as a record assembled to survive a court, and the opposition is now filing with the second audience in mind. So should we.

Also On Our Radar
  • 550 Feet, 1,053 Megawatts: the most expensive easement in America right now, and the cleanest cautionary tale we've ever run. Xcel's Public Service Company of Colorado has asked the Colorado Supreme Court to decide whether a regulated utility can condemn easements for gen-tie lines serving renewable generation, after a district judge in June rejected its taking of roughly 550 feet of easement into the Goose Creek substation across land owned by Dryland Partners. The two projects behind that 550 feet — the 603-MW Singing Grass and the 450-MW Cheyenne Ridge II — are essentially built and cannot connect. PSCo acquired rights across nearly the entire length of a 5.4-mile and a 28.8-mile tie line from more than 100 landowners; one holdout at the interconnection point blocks both. PSCo's warning to the court is that the ruling lets "any landowner controlling the sole access point" dictate terms; the landowner's side counters that the PUC should expect PSCo to come asking ratepayers to cover imprudently incurred carrying costs. The lesson is not about eminent domain law. It's that gen-tie easements are not a real-estate task to be closed in parallel with construction — the last 550 feet is worth the same as the first 34 miles, and the party who figures that out first sets the price.
  • The Receipts on Jobs and Taxes: Illinois just published the audit of a promise our clients make in every host-community meeting, and it does not flatter us. A new report from the University of Illinois' Climate Jobs Institute and Prairie Rivers Network finds the state's Coal to Solar and Energy Storage Initiative — which was supposed to yield at least six significant solar-plus-storage arrays and five stand-alone battery banks on retired coal sites — produced three relatively small solar farms with storage and no stand-alone storage at all. NRG abandoned both of its battery projects; Vistra built three and dropped six, scaling its solar from a proposed 294 MW to 164 MW and installing the bare 2 MW of storage per site needed to claim the credits. The cause was a fixed $30 renewable energy credit price that couldn't move when inflation, borrowing costs, and supply chains did. But the two numbers to carry into the next hearing are the ones about the community: solar and storage arrays "typically offer only some monitoring and maintenance positions" post-construction — "single digits per site," per co-author Roshan Krishnan — and the array at the Baldwin plant is projected to bring in about $6 million in property taxes over its lifetime against a coal plant that paid $3.1 million in tax year 2025 alone. Opponents in Cochise County made almost exactly this argument last week and won unanimously. We should be the ones who bring the honest version of these figures to the room first, with a host agreement built to close the gap, rather than getting handed them across the podium.
  • Floor Price, Ceiling Effect: the module cost line in every county cost-benefit analysis is about to move, and opponents will notice before we do. The administration's Section 232 action on polysilicon and downstream solar pairs a minimum import price regime with a 15% ad valorem tariff plus volumetric duties — baseline MIPs of $21/kg for polysilicon, $100/kg for ingots and wafers, $0.22/W for cells, and $0.38/W for modules, with the twist that a U.S. assembler using imported cells or wafers cannot sell a finished module below that $0.38/W floor either. SEIA puts U.S. module capacity at 65.5 GW, roughly 70% of annual demand, but domestic cell capacity at just 3.2 GW, with more than 90% of cells used in domestic assembly imported. Intertek CEA expects crystalline-silicon module imports to largely stop after the exclusion window. The Commerce Secretary can raise the MIPs unilaterally and they can only ratchet up. For us this is not a trade story — it's a hearing story. "This project only pencils because of subsidies" is about to be joined by "and it's getting more expensive," and the developers who have already run the sensitivity and can answer it in a sentence will fare better than the ones who look surprised.
  • Sampling Rights: FERC just handed PJM's distributed resources a procedural win worth about five gigawatts, and it changes an argument we hear constantly. Ruling for Voltus and the Mission:data Coalition against PJM, the commission ordered the RTO to accept statistical sampling as a valid way to measure the reliability of demand response and virtual power plant programs, rather than requiring smart-meter data from every participating household — a bar most PJM-territory utilities made impossible to clear because they never released the data. FERC found PJM is losing at least 4.9 GW of capacity under the old rule "at a time when PJM is experiencing unprecedented load growth driven in part by hyperscale data center development," and called the status quo unjust and unreasonable. Voltus expects hundreds of megawatts of residential load to come online over the next several years. Note the shape of it: PJM serves 67 million people across 13 states where capacity costs are already a political grenade, and the "we don't need your solar farm, we need efficiency and demand response" line will get louder as VPPs scale. The honest answer is that 4.9 GW of demand response is real and still nowhere near what the load growth requires — but we should have that number ready, not be discovering it at the podium.
  • Project Crystal Sun: the domestic-cell bottleneck may be about to break, and it's a local-benefits story with a Texas zip code. Tesla has filed a Chapter 312 tax abatement application with the Texas Comptroller for a $10.1 billion vertically integrated solar manufacturing campus on a 3,050-acre site near Richmond in Fort Bend County — $1.5 billion in real property and $8.6 billion in manufacturing equipment, with listed equipment spanning the full silicon-to-module chain: ingot growth, wafer slicing, chemical coating, metallization and printing, cell testing, cleanrooms, automated material handling. Nameplate capacity isn't disclosed. PV Tech Research calls it the largest single-site investment ever proposed in U.S. solar manufacturing. Read it against the item above — 3.2 GW of domestic cell capacity against 65.5 GW of module assembly is the gap this is aimed at. And read the filing itself as a template: a company that wanted a local yes led with acreage, capital split, and an abatement request, in public, on the record.
  • The Load That Blinked: the data-center reliability argument just acquired a number, and it cuts against the hyperscalers. PJM said Aug. 11 it is weighing interconnection reliability requirements, including ride-through standards, for computational loads after roughly 3,800 MW of data center load tripped offline in northern Virginia on July 22 — the largest such event in PJM history. A fault on a 230-kV line in Dominion's zone set it off; PJM load dropped 3.8%, from 99,984 MW to 96,205 MW, in two waves, with significant voltage and frequency swings. Operating Committee chair Emanuel Bernabeu was blunt that the fault cleared normally and "these data centers are too sensitive… they are disconnecting too early." Two similar Dominion-zone events moved about 1,500 MW each in 2024 and 2025. NERC must finalize registry criteria and initial standards for computational loads by Dec. 31 under a FERC directive, but won't reach ride-through until next year. Worth having in the pocket: the load that gets cited as the reason the grid needs everything, everywhere, all at once is also the load that walked off the system three times in three years.
Project Updates
  • MIComstock Hears It All and Votes on Nothing: the Kalamazoo County battery hearing went four hours and closed without a recommendation, and the fight is now entirely about two numbers. Copenhagen Infrastructure Partners' two-phase, 400-MW Swan and Cygnet project at the former Hickory Ridge Golf Course went to public hearing Thursday, Aug. 13, and the planning commission closed the hearing without voting. Number one: the township ordinance requires at least 140 contiguous acres, and the project is split into two separately owned parcels — Swan at 16.95 acres and Cygnet at 22.27 — so CIP is seeking a waiver the township's outside consultant has flagged as a threshold question. Number two: that consultant found unmitigated noise at roughly 63 decibels at the property line against a 55-decibel cap, and CIP's answer is a 14-foot-tall wooden sound wall running about 3,370 feet. On the benefits side CIP offers roughly 105 acres of open space potentially deeded to the township, a 20-year decommissioning plan with financial assurance, and $825 per megawatt annually for 20 years. Comstock Public Schools' consultant wants a one-mile buffer; the site is still inside a mile. Commissioners asked the assessor for tax detail before resuming.
  • MIOshtemo Pauses the Panels and Picks Up the Pen: the township ran both plays in one week — stop the clock, then write the rules. On Aug. 11 the board adopted a six-month moratorium on large-scale commercial solar 7-0 on second reading, structured as a police ordinance rather than a zoning amendment, with no public comment offered on the item. Two days later the planning commission began a line-by-line review of a 38-page draft Energy Systems Ordinance covering solar, wind, and storage in a single section — the township's answer to Public Act 233. The draft as written sets BESS setbacks of 2,000 feet from schools and daycares, 1,000 feet from specialty crop farms, 400 feet from occupied community buildings on non-participating property, and 300 feet from roads and non-participating lines, with a 30-decibel property-line cap, automatic fire suppression, early-warning gas detection, emergency plans built with the fire department and reviewed every three years, pre-construction groundwater and soil testing, annual PFAS and lithium monitoring, a 20-year hydrogeological model, and a $5,000-per-megawatt host agreement. A resident adjacent to a proposed site asked for six further changes, including raising the residential setback to 1,000 feet and tying host payments to revenue instead of a flat per-MW rate. NewEdge Renewable Power has a 260-MW BESS proposed on leased farmland at West M Avenue and South Van Kal Street. This is the room where our clients' west-Michigan standards are being written, this month.
  • INCopper Box Gets One More Week: a second three-hour hearing, a second-draft application, and a decision that lands tomorrow. The Montgomery County Advisory Plan Commission voted Aug. 11 to table Copper Box Solar's rezoning petition for one week, and meets at 4 p.m. Aug. 18 to send it on with a favorable, unfavorable, or no recommendation. The petition would rezone 17 parcels across southern Madison and northern Union townships for a roughly 2,620-acre project with up to 825 acres of panels — a revision of a request Copper Box withdrew in March after a 6-2 unfavorable recommendation. Developer Tom Schoder's revisions are worth studying as a resubmission model: bigger residential setbacks for panels, substation, and staging; about two miles of vegetative screening beyond what the ordinance requires; the substation moved farther east; and the estimated lifetime tax benefit raised from roughly $20 million to more than $45 million. County staff still recommends unfavorable, on the grounds that the 2024 comprehensive plan names industrial solar an undesirable land use, and written comment ran 46 opposed to 14 in support. The comprehensive plan is the obstacle a setback can't fix.
  • INParke Writes Its Own Before It Has To: the ordinance goes up for adoption today, and the drafting method is the story. Commissioner Jim Meece told the Parke County Post the county is acting because it has several solar requests in hand and can no longer extend its moratorium, and that he assembled the draft by collecting ordinances from other counties ranging from very restrictive to very permissive and building largely from Tippecanoe County's. The proposal caps solar at 6,000 acres countywide, requires panels 36 inches off the ground to allow sheep grazing, holds height to 16 feet, and scales setbacks from 500 to 1,500 feet depending on how many sides of a property a solar farm touches — "The idea is not to surround my house with solar panels on all sides." The BZA would enforce. Two things to note: a county that can't extend a moratorium will write rules on its own timeline whether or not a developer is at the table, and Tippecanoe's text is now propagating across Indiana by photocopy. If we have an issue with a clause in Tippecanoe's ordinance, we will have it in a dozen counties.
  • VAMount Jackson Wants the Paperwork First: a town council delayed a solar vote for the most instructive reason available — the agreement wasn't public in time to read. On Aug. 11 the council put off a decision on extending Energix's unfinished Turkey Knob project through 2031 because the amended siting agreement governing $1.375 million in payments hadn't been released for residents to review, approving a short-term extension to Sept. 8 by a 5-1 vote and setting a public hearing for that date. Roughly 450 acres and 75 MW remain unbuilt against a 15-MW portion generating since 2021. The payment schedule: $275,000 on approval — owed even if nothing more is built — then $350,000 at site plan, $425,000 at construction start, $325,000 at commercial operation. Resident Alvin Henry did the division on the floor and got about $18,300 per megawatt against a $23,000–$47,000 range he said the town manager had cited. Energix's explanation for the delay is that its software mistook a Shenandoah Valley Electric Cooperative line for Dominion transmission, forcing a PJM interconnection restart. A host payment that a resident can divide by nameplate and find wanting is a host payment that needed a better narrative before it hit the packet.
  • NYTerra-Gen Files the Book: a gigawatt-hour of storage clears a procedural gate in the Hudson Valley. Terra-Gen submitted its Draft Environmental Impact Statement to the Town of Ulster Town Board on Aug. 4 for a 250-MW / 1,000-MWh BESS at 430 Hurley Ave., interconnecting to an adjacent Central Hudson substation. The board is SEQRA lead agency and adopted the final scoping document in April after community input shaped the study areas, following the original site plan and special use permit application in April 2025 — call it sixteen months from filing to DEIS. The design is lithium iron phosphate, built to the 2025 New York State Fire Code and NFPA 855, with layered prevention, detection, monitoring, and emergency response, which is the safety case the opposition will now spend the comment period testing. Terra-Gen is also carrying a labor MOA with the Hudson Valley Building & Construction Trades Council and a PILOT benefiting the county, the town, and the Kingston school district. The DEIS is posted for public comment; this is an advance in a contested review, not an approval.
  • NYSaugerties Village to Saugerties Town, Please Stop: a jurisdictional split worth watching, because the project isn't even in the village. The Saugerties Village Board unanimously passed a resolution requesting a moratorium or denial from the Town of Saugerties for Key Capture Energy's 100-MW KCE NY 34 project, pending a demonstration of public safety and environmental protection — and the town is proceeding with review anyway. The site is 59.8 acres at 7 Tomsons Road, roughly 11 acres of array, with a master plan for 136 battery units each the size of a 40-foot shipping container; the nearest homes sit about 1,200 feet away. The village wants an independent quantitative risk assessment modeling evacuation zones and smoke dispersion under worst-case weather, plus fire department certification that local companies can actually respond. Town Supervisor Fred Costello called the resolution redundant and the moratorium idea "silly," noted the village skipped the town's forums, and put the community at roughly a third for, a third against, a third indifferent. Key Capture's Chris Linsmayer says a commissioned sound study will comply and hopes for a planning board vote in the coming months. When a neighboring jurisdiction with no authority passes a resolution anyway, that's a signal the engagement map was drawn too narrowly.
  • ORTwo Projects, One Gronquist Building: Oregon's siting council reversed its own procedural call and will hear from Gilliam County in person. The Energy Facility Siting Council has set back-to-back public comment meetings in Arlington for Thursday, Aug. 20 at 6 p.m. — one on Avangrid's site certificate amendment request for Oregon Trail Solar, which seeks to split the facility in two after sharing a certificate with Montague Wind and Montague Solar and beginning panel installation last month, and one on EDP Renewables' Notice of Intent for the Big River Energy Facility, a wind-solar-battery complex spanning nearly 30,000 acres that the developer values at roughly $1 billion. The detail to file: ODOE analyst Chase McVeigh-Walker said the agency had initially waived the in-proximity meeting requirement for the Avangrid amendment, then reversed once EDP's NOI arrived and decided in-proximity meetings were warranted given the cluster of projects near Arlington. Density triggers process. When a second application lands in the same county, assume the procedural shortcuts your first one got are gone.
On the Frontlines
  • AZNobody Can Answer That: a unanimous denial built on the two numbers from today's Radar, delivered by commissioners who had clearly rehearsed them. The Cochise County Planning and Zoning Commission voted unanimously Aug. 12 to reject a proposal that would have nearly doubled a solar facility southwest of Whetstone and added two 20-acre battery storage sites. More than 100 people filled the room past capacity the night after a monsoon, and commissioners said that turnout is why they voted rather than tabling the item again. District Two Commissioner John Benedict did the assessment math out loud — state central assessment at 20% of asset value — and landed on "zero tax revenue, zero jobs, and a ton of risk to our community." District One Commissioner Christy Zoumis asked how it benefits the county: "nobody can answer that." Project representatives were largely absent, apparently expecting another tabling. Two failures compound here: nobody was in the room to answer the benefit question, and nobody had prepared an answer that survived a commissioner with a calculator. The company retains appeal rights.
  • AZTwo Weeks' Notice, Eight Square Miles: a textbook example of how a notice schedule creates an opposition group. Hundreds of residents packed a Repsol-hosted community meeting near Robles Junction to oppose a 500-MW solar-plus-battery project that would lease 5,700 acres of Arizona State Trust Land — eight square miles — with panels on roughly 2,000 of them, interconnecting to TEP's Pinal West–South 345-kV line. Landowners got notice letters two weeks before the meeting. Douglas Lowell, a fourth-generation cattle rancher, used the interval to stand up the Open Space Alliance and says the goal is to make a yes vote "unpalatable." Danny Flores wants environmental and water impact fees and is raising grading runoff into Brawley Wash. Repsol's numbers — roughly $200 million in tax revenue, about 600 construction jobs, about a dozen permanent — went into a room that had already decided. Repsol declined an on-camera interview. Construction isn't slated until late 2027 with operations in 2029, which is to say there was time to do this differently and the calendar was used the other way.
  • COThe Conversation That Wasn't: a transmission approval that went through, and a credibility problem that will outlive it. Bent County commissioners adopted Resolution 2026-11 granting a special use permit for a 47.4-mile segment of Grid United's roughly 290-mile Three Corners Connector, a line rated to 3,600 MW running from Pueblo to an Oklahoma substation. Southern Plains Land Trust's conservation director told commissioners the route crosses about 5.8 miles of Heartland Ranch, affecting roughly 492 acres under conservation easement and fragmenting habitat for 12 species of greatest conservation need. But the fight in the room was over a single sentence on page 21 of the application claiming the developer "has been in an ongoing conversation with the Southern Plains Land Trust." A trust board member called it false and read the email chain the trust itself had initiated in July. Grid United's written reply cited 2023–2024 contacts and unreturned courtesy calls a week before filing. The permit passed anyway — and the applicant now owns a documented dispute over whether it told the truth about its own outreach, in a county it has 47 miles of line to build. Never write "ongoing conversation" into a filing unless the other party would use the same phrase.
  • INFull Steam Ahead in St. Bernice: a yes, and a useful picture of what a yes costs. Vermillion County commissioners signed the agreements clearing Apex Clean Energy's Brouilletts Creek Solar Project in St. Bernice Township to proceed. "The commissioners went back and forth with attorneys on this, and we feel comfortable with the agreements we have in place," Commissioner RJ Dunavan said. The economic development agreement runs $13 million — $6.5 million up front and $6.5 million at start of operations — plus an $800,000 Brouilletts Creek Community Fund for St. Bernice residents. The project leases about 1,800 acres, with roughly 1,000 in panels, all from private landowners, many still farming and putting less productive ground into the lease. The part worth underlining: Dunavan posted a video in June asking residents for input, said the majority of responses ran against solar in the county, called himself "not the biggest fan of solar" — and voted yes anyway, on the grounds that Apex had been a good neighbor. Groundbreaking targets spring–summer 2027.
  • MIZeeland Goes Federal: the constitutional challenge to Michigan's state siting law just changed courthouses, and the venue fight is itself the tell. Lawyers for Silver Maple PV LLC and parent RWE filed a notice of removal moving Zeeland Charter Township's lawsuit from Ottawa County Circuit Court to the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Michigan. The township's July 7 suit seeks to block the 200-MW Silver Maple solar-and-storage project and to have 2023 Public Act 233 declared unconstitutional — arguing that moving final approval of large solar, wind, and storage projects from local boards to the Michigan Public Service Commission violates Article 7, Section 29 on municipal consent and reasonable control of rights-of-way. The developer invoked diversity jurisdiction, noting Delaware incorporation and ultimate German ownership by RWE AG. The case had been assigned to an Ottawa County judge before removal; no federal schedule is set. Every developer relying on PA 233 preemption in Michigan should be watching this docket, because the answer to "can the state override the township" is now going to be litigated in front of a judge the township didn't pick.
  • CAFirst One Back After the Fires: the largest battery project in Arevon's portfolio came online in the county that has the most reason to be nervous about batteries. The 300-MW / 1,200-MWh Nighthawk Energy Storage Project held its ribbon-cutting in Poway on Aug. 11 — 329 Tesla Megapacks on lithium iron phosphate, enough for roughly 385,000 homes for four hours, under a long-term agreement with PG&E. It is the first BESS to come online in San Diego County since the run of fires at Valley Center in 2023, Gateway in Otay Mesa in 2024, and SDG&E's Escondido facility later that year, which is exactly why Poway residents fought it. CEO Justin Johnson's mitigation list is the one to copy: LFP chemistry's thermal runaway resistance, a fire hydrant loop, a 12-to-14-foot wall, continuous infrared camera surveillance. Arevon puts lifetime property tax revenue above $30 million and peak construction employment above 130. A commissioned project in a burned county is the most persuasive exhibit our clients can bring to the next fire-risk hearing — and now there is one.
  • PAThe Wild, Wild West: a small approval with an outsized lesson about what happens where there are no rules to comply with. Fairfield Township supervisors in Westmoreland County unanimously approved GreenKey Development's 12-MW solar facility on a 112-acre parcel along Fort Palmer Road on Aug. 13, with panels covering roughly 13 acres across nearly 50 acres of farm, built in four simultaneous phases over about four months. The supervisors picked an alternative layout over the plan GreenKey filed with the county in February and revised in March — one that complies with the township's 2024 solar ordinance setbacks and results in fewer trees cut and less earthmoving. That choice is why this passed 3-0 after a June 30 hearing and a 45-day decision clock. Solicitor Amber Leechalk's aside is the quotable part: the township has no comprehensive zoning ordinance at all, which she called "the wild, wild west." Resident Lynn McDermott's objection was that approval opens the door to more development, which in a township with no zoning is not an unreasonable fear. The application still goes to county planning and the commissioners.
  • CTSixty-One of Sixty-Five: the offshore project that survived two federal stop-work orders is four turbines from done. At Ørsted's quarterly presentation Aug. 13, CEO Rasmus Errboe said Revolution Wind is more than 95% complete with 61 of 65 turbines fully installed, with the last four going in over the coming months and full commercial operation by year end. Peak capacity is 704 MW, roughly 350,000 homes, staged out of the State Pier in New London — the first offshore wind contracted to serve Connecticut customers, though it sits off Rhode Island. Sunrise Wind, the 924-MW Long Island project, passed the halfway mark with 77 of 84 foundations, 20 turbines, and all export cables and the offshore converter station complete, targeting 2027. Both were twice ordered halted by the administration over claimed military radar interference; the developers sued and won both times. Ørsted still took a DKK 1.2 billion (~$126 million) non-cash impairment on U.S. projects on rising interest rates. Two stop-work orders, two lawsuits, and it's getting built — the record held.
  • NCLeasing Is Now Alive and Well: a market that lost its subsidy, found a workaround, and is posting record months — which is the argument-in-a-box for the next hearing where someone says renewables can't stand on their own. North Carolina rooftop installations fell by two-thirds between Q4 of last year and Q1 2026 after the 30% residential credit died, and installers are nonetheless reporting record sales months. The mechanism: a 2017 state law lets third parties lease the equipment even though they can't sell the electrons, and the commercial 30% credit survives through at least the end of next year — so lessors claim it and pass the savings through, letting households go solar with little or nothing down. More than 20 third-party lessors are now registered with state regulators. Batteries sweeten it further through Duke's EnergyWise Home and Power Manager programs, which pay households a monthly incentive — about $50 on the average install — for letting the utility tap the battery 30 to 36 times a year. Duke residential bills are up over 20% in five years with another 10% sought. "It's actually the most cost-effective residential option I've seen yet in North Carolina," said Sugar Hollow Solar's Clary Franko. Bill credits step down again Jan. 1, 2027.

Oak Run beat every objection but one and lost two years to a missing picture. Bent County's applicant got its permit and lost the room's trust over a sentence about outreach it couldn't back up. Cochise County's applicant didn't send anyone to answer the only question the commissioners asked. Different states, same failure mode: the file was thinner than the fight. Audit the gap before someone else finds it.
