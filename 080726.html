Friday morning, team.

Two county rooms this week, forty-eight hours apart, told the same truth from opposite sides. Tuesday in Kansas, the Sedgwick County Commission sat through an eleven-hour meeting and then killed two solar projects 4-1 — the fear that carried the room was the battery attached to one of them. Tuesday in Indiana, the Knox County Area Plan Commission heard the same lithium-ion fire arguments and recommended a battery approval anyway, all but one vote. Same technology, same objections, same week — opposite outcomes. The variable wasn't the panels or the cells. It was who did the work in the room, and that's the whole business we're in.

Hold that local reality against a national tailwind that finally blew our clients' way. Thursday, a federal judge — a Trump appointee, no less — ordered the Pentagon to lift its de facto freeze on onshore-wind reviews, unsticking more than 150 projects across 21 states. Out in Wisconsin, We Energies and Invenergy put shovels in the ground on 335 megawatts of new solar, wind and storage. And in Texas, the PUC blinked on the biggest wire in state history. The macro keeps clearing room; the fights that decide whether anything gets built are still the ones at the folding tables in Wichita and Vincennes. Radar has the roundup, Project Updates has the Kansas denial in full, and the Plays this week are the two safety arguments — one that lost a room, one that won it.

Also On Our Radar
  • Freeze, Thawed: the biggest thing to break our clients' way all summer, and it came from the bench. On Thursday a federal judge in Oregon — Karin Immergut, appointed by President Trump — issued a preliminary injunction ordering the Defense Department to resume its routine military reviews of onshore-wind permits, ending a de facto moratorium that began in August 2025 and hardened into an outright halt this spring. The DoD must now report its progress to the court every 30 days. The stakes are not small: the freeze had stalled more than 150 wind projects across 21 states, according to the industry's own tally, on a national-security rationale the courts have now rejected the same way they rejected the offshore stop-work orders last year. The line to carry into any wind conversation: onshore wind already supplies 10% of U.S. electricity and is, per Lazard, the cheapest new generation to build — the obstacle was never the resource, it was the paperwork, and a court just moved it. One caution for the file: it's a preliminary injunction, not a final win, and the administration has appealed parallel blockades before.
  • Shovels, Not Slides: proof the product still gets built where the process lets it. We Energies and developer Invenergy launched construction this week on three Wisconsin projects that together will power about 100,000 homes: Darien Battery Storage (75 MW, Walworth County, broke ground last week), Dawn Harvest Solar and Battery Park (150 MW of solar plus 50 MW of storage, Rock County), and Badger Hollow Wind (110 MW, Iowa and Grant counties). All three cleared the Public Service Commission of Wisconsin before a single truck rolled — the boring, front-loaded regulatory work that the contested rooms elsewhere never get to. Two details worth stealing for a hostile board: the utility is framing this as an "all of the above" reliability play, not a climate play, and it's leading with "hundreds of good-paying, union-first jobs." When the pitch is reliability-and-jobs and the approvals are already in hand, renewables read as infrastructure, not ideology.
  • Permian Pause, For Real This Time: the update to our standing Texas STEP item — the pressure we flagged Wednesday became an actual vote. The Public Utility Commission of Texas voted to abate its key regulatory decisions on segments of the $33 billion, 765-kV network, holding final approvals until administrative law judges finish reviewing the parallel line segments. What tipped it wasn't only the 40-plus lawmakers led by Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick; it was a structural argument landowners have been making for a year — that a 2023 law compressed the review window from 360 days to 180, leaving affected communities almost no time to challenge routes. State Rep. Brad Buckley's framing is the one to watch spread: "What began as a small regional transmission project to energize the Permian Basin has literally become a statewide transmission plan that was not authorized by the Legislature." When a transmission fight wins on process — not fields, not viewsheds — that argument travels to every gen-tie and interconnection route in the ERCOT queue our clients depend on.

  • KSEleven Hours to No: after a meeting that ran more than eleven hours and drew a full day of testimony, the Sedgwick County Commission rejected two utility-scale solar projects — the Haysville project and the Clearwater project — on 4-1 votes apiece. Read the fault line carefully, because it's ours: neighbors' central objection wasn't the panels but the battery, the BESS planned as part of the Haysville project, with residents raising fire safety, contamination from a potential leak, and tax-revenue questions. The Clearwater project had no storage attached — and still went down 4-1, swept out on the same tide. On the other side stood organized labor: IBEW Local 271's business manager pressed the long-run case ("the sun's going to be up there") and the investment argument, and it wasn't enough. Two lessons for the pipeline. First, in a room primed on battery-fire fear, a co-located BESS can sink the solar it's attached to — the storage question has to be answered on its own terms, early, or it becomes the whole project's liability. Second, a union at the podium is a real asset but not a sufficient one; labor's economic pitch didn't move commissioners who'd spent nine hours listening to their neighbors. Carry both into any Kansas conversation.

Plays of the Week
  • Blue Jeans Energy (IN)Bring the Fire Marshal: the clearest example this week of how you win the battery-safety argument instead of losing it. Facing a Vincennes-area room full of the same lithium-ion fears that sank the Kansas project, the developer of the Ratts 2 Solar battery addition didn't just send its own engineers to the Knox County Area Plan Commission — it brought independent battery-safety and emergency-response specialists who walked the commission through industry safety standards, fire-suppression technology, emergency-response planning, and the training the company would make available to local first responders. Opponents had a genuinely strong hand: residents pointed out that Knox County's original solar ordinance had said batteries would not be permitted, and one neighbor drew applause reminding the board it had promised exactly that. The commission still voted to recommend approval, all but a single dissent. The replicable move is the messenger: when the objection is "these batteries catch fire and you can't put them out," the answer that lands isn't a slide from the developer — it's a fire-safety expert and a first-responder training commitment, in the room, before the vote. The counter-example was 500 miles away in Sedgwick County the same night; the difference in the record was who spoke to the fire question.
  • Pit Stop Solar opponents (SC)Attack the Record, Not the Panels: the opposition tactic every developer in our footprint should be defending against, because it's quiet, procedural, and it works. In Darlington County, South Carolina, opponents of the roughly 75-megawatt Pit Stop Solar project — approved by the Planning Commission back in February — didn't fight the merits. They sued on the record, arguing the commission "failed to issue findings of fact, conclusions of law or otherwise explain the basis" for its approval, which they contend makes meaningful judicial review impossible under South Carolina law, and that the board simply adopted a staff recommendation without independently explaining why the project met the county's standards. That narrow procedural attack has already done its work: this week the County Council took up a nine-month moratorium on all new solar and wind, with a councilman openly saying the pause lets the county "revisit our ordinance" and make its stricter fee-in-lieu terms permanent. Note the sequence — a thin approval record hands opponents a lawsuit, the lawsuit hands the county a reason to freeze everyone, and a project that cleared the commission in February is now the reason the next five can't file. The defensive play writes itself: make the local board build a real, written record — findings of fact, standards met, conditions stated — the day of the vote. The approval you don't document is the approval a neighbor gets to unwind.

Same batteries, same fears, two different rooms — one said no, one said yes, and the only variable was who did the work. Bring the fire marshal; build the record; win the room.
