Volume 25 · Wednesday, July 8, 2026

Good morning, team.

The safe-harbor deadline is behind us, the four-year build clock is running, and the fight has already moved to the least glamorous part of the project: the wire. You can lock the tax credit, sign the PPA, and clear the county planning commission — and still lose the whole thing at the point where the high-voltage line has to cross somebody's back forty. This week made that plain in two states at once.

In Loudoun, the State Corporation Commission handed Dominion a routing win and handed the county a crisis — 200-foot monopoles cleared to march through a subdivision's backyards, and a board of supervisors scrambling inside a two-week window to find another path. In Texas, regulators did the opposite: they pumped the brakes on a $33 billion, 765-kV buildout after 1,300 landowners said they were written into the map without ever being invited to the meeting. Different outcomes, identical lesson — and it's the one this brief keeps landing on.

Today's Deep Dive is below, then a read on the battery backlash that keeps taking projects off the board before they file, and eight from the frontlines.

Deep Dive

The Route Cause

The Virginia State Corporation Commission this week approved Route 3A for Dominion's Golden-to-Mars transmission line — a routing that sends nearly 200-foot monopoles through the backyards of the Loudoun Valley Estates subdivision, including the yard of a resident who has lived there two decades. Hours after the order came down, the Loudoun County Board of Supervisors convened and passed a resolution demanding the county school board hold an emergency meeting before July 20 to authorize an alternate path across school-owned land. A county that spent months fighting the line is now racing the clock to volunteer its own property rather than let the approved route stand.

Eight hundred miles southwest, the mirror image. In mid-June the Public Utility Commission of Texas postponed consideration of the first leg of its Strategic Transmission Expansion Plan — the Bell County East–Big Hill 765-kV segment, the opening move in a buildout whose lifetime cost approaches $100 billion. The pause followed a motion from more than 1,300 landowners who said Oncor and the Lower Colorado River Authority tacked roughly 400 miles of new route segments onto the plan after a June 2025 public meeting, then filed in March without ever notifying the property owners newly written into the footprint. State Rep. Brad Buckley and 42 other lawmakers filed an amicus asking regulators to hold the case until the January 2027 legislative session. A decision that was expected in September is now open-ended.

Notice everything these two cases have in common, because it isn't the sector — solar, wind, and gas all ride these wires — and it isn't the region. It's the failure mode. Neither fight is about whether the electrons are clean or whether the load is real. Both turn on process: which route, disclosed when, to whom, with what paper trail. It is the exact vulnerability that sank the Prince William Digital Gateway last year and the one opposition counsel now drills into first, because it's the cheapest place to win. You don't have to prove a project is bad. You only have to prove somebody wasn't told.

For our clients, this is the whole game now. Generation is increasingly the settled part — the panels get financed, the turbines get ordered — and the project lives or dies on interconnection and the routing of the spine that carries it to load. That spine runs through private yards, school parcels, and cattle pasture, and every one of those parcels is a potential veto. A wind farm stranded behind a line that can't clear its right-of-way is just as dead as one the county denied outright.

The Takeaway

Treat the transmission route as the front line, not the back office. Every route revision is a new notice event — document it, disclose it, and get in front of the affected landowners before the amended map is filed, not after. Loudoun shows what happens when a community is surprised by a monopole; Texas shows what happens when 1,300 of them organize around a single missed meeting. If a project depends on new wire — and nearly all of ours now do — the engagement plan has to start at the corridor, at the very beginning, with clean receipts for every property the line touches.

Also On Our Radar

Charge Withdrawn

The clearest tell in storage right now isn't a denial — it's a withdrawal. The developer of the Seguro battery project near Escondido pulled the application after opponents gathered more than 5,000 signatures and buried the county in letters, citing fire risk on a site ringed by homes. No hearing, no vote — the project simply left the field. It's the pattern more than the outcome: the fire-safety narrative has become organized opposition's master key, portable from town to town and pre-loaded before a project ever files.

The numbers show where it's landing hardest. New York now carries the large majority of the country's active BESS moratoriums — dozens of towns that have hit pause while they figure out how to regulate storage, or whether to allow it at all. In Los Angeles, residents are protesting a facility by tying it to the AI data-center boom, fusing two of the year's most combustible narratives into one. And the codes are catching up: state fire marshals are being directed to rewrite where storage can physically sit, pushing toward dedicated noncombustible buildings and outdoor-only installations.

The Takeaway

The fire conversation happens with or without you — the only question is who narrates it first. On any storage project, get the local fire authority and a mutual-aid/emergency-response plan into the pre-application record early, and put the thermal-runaway facts on the table before the opposition puts fear there. A withdrawn project doesn't show up in the denial statistics, but it's dead all the same.

On the Frontlines
  • VAMonopoles Approved: The SCC cleared Route 3A for Dominion's Golden-to-Mars line; Loudoun's board is racing a July 20 deadline to get its school board to volunteer an alternate corridor before the backyard route becomes final.
  • VABotetourt's Double Bind: Two utility-scale solar farms — the largest ever proposed in the Roanoke Valley — are before the county planning commission, where "industrial solar" opponents and data-center opponents are converging on the same rooms at the same time.
  • OHPublic Interest, Private Veto: The Ohio Power Siting Board rejected a $98M, 726-acre Morrow County solar farm — the seventh large solar project killed in Ohio since 2020 — finding no technical fault, only that local trustees opposed it. In Richland County, voters banned wind and solar in 11 of 18 townships.
  • KSMarion Draws Down: Marion County commissioners barred commercial wind in 15 of 24 townships, citing noise, shadow flicker, and property values — a township-by-township lockout that leaves developers a shrinking map.
  • WATri-Cities at the Bench: The Washington Supreme Court is hearing a landmark challenge to the state's largest proposed wind-and-solar project, near the Tri-Cities — a case that will test how far state siting authority can override local objection in the Northwest.
  • PACorry Says No, Harrisburg Says Maybe: The Corry planning commission declined 3-2 to recommend a 16-acre, 5,000-panel solar array, even as state lawmakers weigh a RESET siting board that would fast-track energy projects over local zoning.
  • CASeguro Surrenders: The Seguro battery project near Escondido was withdrawn after 5,000-plus signatures and fire-risk fears — a storage project pulled before it ever reached a vote.
  • NYClay Weighs the Freeze: The Clay town board is taking up two Nexamp BESS proposals alongside an AI/data-center moratorium — one meeting, both of the year's flashpoints, in a state that already leads the nation in local storage moratoriums.

Start at the corridor, not the courthouse.
