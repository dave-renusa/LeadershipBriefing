RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 68 · Friday, November 6, 2026

Friday morning, team.

The week's biggest result went the developer's way. Peoria County's board approved the Four Creeks wind project 12-5 on Thursday, after its own zoning board had voted 4-2 in October to recommend denial. The approval came with close to forty conditions, including reimbursement of county expenses and a sound study every five years. That is the pattern to watch. Approvals are coming through over local objection, and the fight is moving from whether a project gets built to what it pays and what it must promise. The plays below are about who writes those terms, and they are the ones worth stealing.

Also On Our Radar
  • Over the Zoning Board: Peoria County's board approved the special use permit for Four Creeks Wind 12-5 on Nov. 5, despite a 4-2 zoning board recommendation to deny in October. The project covers up to 113 turbines across four northwest Peoria County townships and is developed by Repsol Renewables through Four Creeks, LLC. Opponents raised property values, noise and wildlife. The land use committee had recommended passage 6-3 before the full vote. We found no reporting of a legal challenge yet.
  • Two Ropes, One Knot: West Virginia residents are organizing a county-by-county coalition against Valley North, a 260-mile 765-kV line, after developers held a second round of open houses in Kanawha County on Nov. 5. Residents said groups in each impacted county are building the coalition.
  • Public Input, Two Days: The Pennsylvania PUC has set in-person public input hearings on NextEra's 500-kV MidAtlantic Resiliency Link. Greene County is set for Tuesday, Nov. 17, and Fayette County for Wednesday, Nov. 18, with afternoon and evening sessions each day. Registering to testify is encouraged but not required.
  • Surveyors at the Gate: Sixty Maryland landowners asked the U.S. Supreme Court to take up their challenge to lower-court rulings that let surveyors onto their property for the Piedmont power line. It is a national bellwether on access rights for transmission routes, and every route team will face the same question at home.
  • Storage Pause, Extended: Oshtemo Township, Michigan, is moving to extend its energy storage moratorium, which is set to expire Nov. 25, 2026.
On the Frontlines
  • IAPage Pivots Its Ordinance: Page County's board gave first approval 2-1 to amendments to its 2024 solar ordinance. The changes are tied to a memorandum of understanding with MidAmerican Energy for the proposed Nodaway Valley Solar Project, about 400 acres north of Clarinda. The amendments are tied to one project, so the vote is also a vote on that project's terms.
  • SCNinety Six Moves Forward: Greenwood County's council advanced a proposed $100 million solar project near Ninety Six and moved Ordinance 2026-30 to bring county solar rules closer to the City of Greenwood's.
  • VAPlanners Say Yes, Supervisors Next: James City County's planning commission recommended Tenaska's proposed large battery storage facility despite resident objections, and the proposal goes to the Board of Supervisors in November. The board takes it up in November.
  • NYTaylor Road Hearing: The Fallsburg planning board heard a battery storage safety briefing ahead of a November public hearing on the Taylor Road site plan and subdivision.
  • MIThumb Approval, Thumb Fight: State regulators approved key agreements for the 185-megawatt Peatville solar project in Sanilac County while opposition across the Thumb grew. Huron County has a temporary moratorium on new utility-scale solar and battery permits running through December.
  • NYFour Thousand Five Hundred Acres of Argument: RWE has leased about 4,500 acres in Cortland County for a wind project the company says could be a $1 billion investment, and local opposition is growing as RWE seeks county land.
  • MDSix Months, Unanimous: Calvert County commissioners unanimously approved a six-month moratorium on new data center applications in August.
  • NCDeadlock, Then Revisit: Davidson County commissioners were split 3-3 on a temporary data-center moratorium and agreed to revisit it.
Plays of the Week
  • Cumberland County Commission, Tenn.Sixteen to Nothing, With a Check Attached: When the county lacked the legal authority to stop a solar project, the commission voted 16-0 for an energy siting agreement that carries $230,000 in community benefit fees and construction requirements. If a board cannot say no, it can still set the terms, and that is the play to copy when the legal position is weak.
  • Ingham County, Mich., and the MPSCHost Money, Written Down: The state Public Service Commission approved a siting settlement on a 90-megawatt solar project that includes host-community payments of at least $2,000 per megawatt, plus money for township legal costs and first-responder training. The county pushed for the payments to flow to the townships that bear the project's burden. The lesson is that the host package is written into the settlement, not offered after the vote.
  • Residents of Mitchell County, Ga.Thirty Percent to Force a Vote: After a contested data-center rezoning, residents launched a recall of three commissioners. Under Georgia law, organizers must first verify 100 signatures, then gather signatures from 30 percent of the district's registered voters to force a special election. Recalls rarely succeed, so the threshold makes this a long campaign.

Build the receipts before the rezoning, and write the terms before the vote.
