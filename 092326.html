RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 53 · Wednesday, September 23, 2026

Good morning, team.

Ohio's biggest solar project won its permit last week, four years after it applied. The only two no votes came from the county and township seats that Ohio law adds to the board for renewables and for nothing else. The rest of the week turned on who carries the burden of proof. Arkansas regulators rejected a 440-megawatt solar deal because the utility could not show the numbers. A Michigan township approved a 1,600-megawatt-hour battery because it felt legally bound to. A Virginia city turned down a solar farm because it did not like how few meetings the developer held. In East Hampton, New York, state regulators traced the chemicals in the town's wells to the fire-suppression gas at a battery site, not to the batteries. Whoever has to prove something tends to lose, so it matters who writes the standard.

Deep Dive

Seven to Two, and the Two Were Local

On Thursday, Sept. 18, the Ohio Power Siting Board voted 7-2 to approve Savion's Oak Run Solar project in Madison County: 800 megawatts of solar and 300 megawatts of battery storage, plus an agrivoltaics plan with about 1,000 sheep grazing between the rows. It is the largest solar-and-storage project in the state. It first applied in 2022 and won a first approval from the board. The trustees of Somerford, Deercreek and Monroe townships and the Madison County commissioners then took it to the Ohio Supreme Court.

The appeal is worth reading closely. On May 26, the court rejected almost every objection the local governments raised, including safety, wildlife and soil harm, and battery fire risk. Justice Patrick Fischer wrote that they had failed to prove they would be harmed "except for concerns about the visual impacts" of the substations and the large poles carrying the lines. The court sent the case back on that one issue. Savion filed renderings and testimony, board staff accepted them, and in its order the board noted that "the only question remaining was the substations' specific visual impact."

The vote is the part to keep. Both no votes came from ad hoc members: one seat for the county and one for the townships. Senate Bill 52, passed in 2021, gives local governments those seats on wind and solar cases and on no other kind of generation. On a nine-member board, two seats could not block a project that had already survived the Supreme Court. They could still put local opposition on the record. The townships and the county can still ask for rehearing and appeal to the Supreme Court again.

Now compare the projects that lost. Since SB 52, the board has repeatedly treated organized local objection as enough reason to deny. The question of whether that is lawful has sat at the Supreme Court since oral argument in the Kingwood Solar case in March 2025. This summer the board asked the court to dismiss Kingwood because the project's interconnection agreement had expired while the court held the case. Oak Run and Kingwood took opposite paths: one kept its local fight on narrow factual issues and won, the other was denied on the general weight of local opposition and is still waiting. That difference, not the size of either project, explains the outcomes.

Oak Run's record shows the court would not treat general local opposition as proof of harm. It wanted specific evidence about specific impacts, and the gap it found was a set of drawings. That standard can be met. It is also the only one of the Madison County objections that anyone had to go back and answer.

The Takeaway

Ohio now has an appellate record saying that fire, soil, wildlife and "we don't want it" do not add up to proof of harm, but a missing substation rendering does. That should change how we build the record on every state-siting file, not just in Ohio. First, the visual package is not a formality. The one objection that held up in Oak Run was about the equipment developers usually draw last. Render every substation, gen-tie pole and battery enclosure from the neighbors' viewpoint before the application is filed. Second, when local governments sit on the deciding board, expect them to vote no, and plan to win the other seats. Our job is to make sure their no vote rests on a record we have already answered. Third, time is part of the risk: Oak Run's permit arrived four years after its application, and Kingwood's interconnection agreement expired while its case waited. Every client should know its queue-expiration date as well as its hearing date.

Also On Our Radar
  • Towers Before Turbines: Steelhead Americas asked the Ringgold County, Iowa, Board of Supervisors for permission to place several temporary meteorological towers to collect weather data for a proposed wind facility. Residents from around the county attended the supervisors' Aug. 24 meeting on the request. According to the Mount Ayr Record-News, the board agreed to keep reviewing the easement requests but set no date for a decision. The paper also reports that a high-voltage transmission line system has been proposed across part of the county.
  • Not Shown to Be Needed: On Sept. 17, the Arkansas Public Service Commission rejected Entergy Arkansas's 20-year power purchase agreement for the 440-megawatt Big Island Solar project, which would cover 3,200 acres in Mississippi County and supply Google's West Memphis data center. The commission found the deal "was not shown to be cost-effective, necessary, or in the public interest." It said Entergy's claimed savings of $29 million to $214 million relied on unsupported assumptions about capacity value and on renewable-energy-credit revenue from Google that is still being negotiated. Entergy can refile. Opponents did not stop this project on land use. The regulator did, on whether ratepayers would come out ahead. When a solar project is tied to a named data center, the ratepayer case needs the same care as the siting case.
  • The Gas, Not the Battery: On Sept. 11, New York's Department of Environmental Conservation issued a preliminary finding that the ultra-short-chain PFAS in two Suffolk County Water Authority wells at the Bridgehampton wellfield most likely came from roughly 4,900 pounds of Novec 1230 fire-suppression gas used during the May 2023 fire at the East Hampton battery site. More than a million gallons of firefighting water likely carried it into the ground. The DEC said "the investigation points to the fire suppression system, not battery storage," as the source. Four days later, East Hampton's supervisor demanded that all Novec 1230 be removed from the facility and taken out of town. Heatmap called the finding relief for the battery industry. It is only partly that. Clean-agent suppression systems are standard in many storage designs, and New York has more BESS moratoriums than any other state. Every client's emergency-response plan should now say which suppression agent is on site and what happens to the water used on a fire.
  • Legally Bound to Say Yes: On Sept. 10, the Comstock Township, Michigan, planning commission approved Copenhagen Infrastructure Partners' Swan and Cygnet storage project, 400 megawatts and 1,600 megawatt-hours. The special land use passed 5-1, and the ordinance-compliance finding and site plan passed 6-0. Commissioners said they felt "legally bound to approve it once the applicant met every requirement in the township's ordinance." The conditions are close to a model set: sound studies before, during and after construction; decommissioning bonds adjusted for inflation every three years; baseline and annual water-quality testing; replacement of any fire department equipment damaged in an emergency; an escrow account to pay for township monitoring; and a binding host-community agreement before building permits are issued. When a code is written clearly enough that the board feels obligated to approve, that is the result we want.
  • The Wire Goes Live: SunZia, the 550-mile line carrying central New Mexico wind to Arizona and California, began commercial operation in June and can now carry 3,000 megawatts of the 3,650-megawatt, 916-turbine wind project. That is according to a Sept. 9 update to Pinal County. SRP is the largest buyer so far, taking 400 MW by this fall and 600 MW by summer 2027, which triples its wind supply. About 2,400 MW is still being negotiated with Arizona utilities. SunZia took close to two decades to permit, and it is now the main example of long-distance transmission for renewables actually getting built. Expect it to be cited in every current 765-kV fight.
  • Next Door to the No: On Sept. 17, the Spotsylvania County Planning Commission voted 4-2 to recommend a special-use permit for Amazon Web Services' Crossroads Technology Campus, 555 acres in the Lee Hill District. Residents criticized commissioners throughout the meeting ("You're selling out your neighbors for projections, not actual numbers"). The applicant brought 60 proffers, including a 300-foot vegetated buffer along homes, a 400-foot building setback from residential zones, 63% of the site kept as open space, and a cut from 15 buildings to seven. The land has been zoned industrial since the 1970s. The Board of Supervisors makes the final decision. Spotsylvania borders Fredericksburg, where the council rejected a data center 7-0 in August. The same regional organizers and arguments are active in both places.
On the Frontlines
  • VANot Enough Meetings: Suffolk City Council denied CEP Solar's 15-megawatt project on Old Myrtle Road. It is the second solar denial on the same rural road since July, after a 3-MW New Leaf Energy proposal. Council members pointed to thin community outreach. LeOtis Williams said: "If there was only one family there, I probably would have had multiple meetings." A family cemetery on the property also came up. The project was denied for lack of engagement, not for any flaw in the project. Under Virginia's new requirement that localities explain every solar denial to the state, "you didn't talk to us enough" is now part of the record.
  • NYFire Rules First: On Sept. 18, the Potsdam town board proposed a six-month moratorium on large commercial battery storage and set a public hearing for Oct. 13. The town's code enforcement officer called the existing law "reasonably complete" but said it needs more fire-emergency safeguards. Permits already granted will be honored. Like East Hampton, this is about fire response, not battery chemistry.
  • VASubstations Too: On Sept. 16, the Loudoun County Board of Supervisors voted 7-1-1 to draft a resolution pausing new legislative applications for data centers and substations for up to 12 months. A final vote is expected in October. Chair Phyllis Randall said it is not a moratorium. The notable detail is that substations are included. Any pause that covers grid equipment also covers the interconnection work that renewable projects depend on.
  • MISecond Pause, Same Township: On Sept. 10, Oshtemo Township's planning commission unanimously sent another solar-application moratorium to the township board while it drafts an energy systems ordinance covering solar, storage and wind. The board had already adopted a six-month pause on large-scale solar 7-0 in August. Oshtemo is next to Comstock in Kalamazoo County, where the same week a clear code produced a battery approval.
  • MIAll Four, Eighteen Months: The Galien planning commission in Berrien County recommended an 18-month moratorium covering wind, solar, battery storage and data centers in a single measure. This is the drafting pattern we have seen all fall: storage and renewables grouped with data centers in the same ordinance, whichever one people were actually worried about.
  • INSolar and Servers, One Ordinance: Switzerland County commissioners adopted Ordinance 2026-8-31-9, a temporary moratorium on applications for commercial and intermediate solar and for data centers, while the county updates its comprehensive plan. The ordinance gives no duration or effective date. An open-ended pause can remain in place for a long time without anyone voting to end it.
  • CAA De Facto Moratorium, Named: San Diego County staff are drafting a battery storage zoning ordinance with tiered permits and 100-foot property-line setbacks. The target is the Planning Commission and Board of Supervisors in spring 2027. Boulevard residents, who lost the Starlight vote last week, want larger setbacks in high-fire zones. Staff have said openly that because most of the unincorporated county is high or very-high fire hazard, banning BESS there "would effectively place a de facto moratorium" on storage. It is rare for a county to put that on the record, and we should keep it.
  • CAThird in One City: Menifee's planning commission will hold a hearing Oct. 28 on the 400-MWh Seaflower battery project, on 3.84 acres tied to Southern California Edison's Valley Substation. It would be the city's third, next to the 680-MW Nova Power Bank and the 500-MW Double Butte project approved in April. At the Double Butte hearing, commissioners focused on fire moving between containers. Where large batteries are already operating, the debate is over how to site them, not whether to allow them.
  • SCWatch the Neighbors First: On Sept. 21, Jasper County Council unanimously approved a 12-month moratorium on new data center and crypto-mining proposals in unincorporated areas, to write operating standards on noise, traffic, power use and environmental impact. The only public comment supported it, arguing the county should watch how neighboring counties that already approved data centers fare first. A county holding off to see how the county next door fares is how ordinance language spreads from one county to the next.

Oak Run won because its opponents' objections were eventually narrowed to one specific, answerable issue. Comstock approved a battery because its code left no room to say no. Suffolk denied a solar farm because nobody held the meetings. Arkansas turned down a deal because the math wasn't shown. Each outcome came down to who had to prove what. This week, for each active file, pick the objection most likely to hold up in an appeal and answer it on the record before anyone asks.
