RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 54 · Friday, September 25, 2026

Friday morning, team.

This week, the written rule decided most of the outcomes. Ringgold County, Iowa turned down a request for temporary weather towers and pointed to the wind ordinance it already had. Oregon's Land Use Board of Appeals overturned a county's denial of a farmland battery because the state's definition of a utility facility already covered it. New York wrote its battery safety code into the contract for 950 megawatts of new storage. A Louisiana parish lifted its solar pause and kept its battery pause because it has a solar ordinance and no battery ordinance yet. Weld County, Colorado approved 805 megawatts of wind and solar on a 3-2 vote over a long list of public objections. Where a code existed, the board applied it. Where none existed, the board paused.

Also On Our Radar
  • No Towers Yet: On Monday, Sept. 21, the Ringgold County, Iowa, Board of Supervisors denied Steelhead's request to put up several meteorological towers, 197 feet tall and temporary, to measure wind speed and direction. According to the Mount Ayr Record-News, supervisors cited the county's existing ordinance guidelines and said they did not want to reverse a position the county had spent nearly a year working out. The ordinance, in place since 2022-2023, sets turbine setback requirements. The paper also reports that Steelhead has continued to ask the county for approvals that would lead to changes in those rules. The request came before the board at an Aug. 24 meeting that drew residents from across the county. Met towers usually get little attention, but a board can act on them well before any turbine application is filed.
  • Three to Two on the High Plains: On Wednesday, Sept. 23, Weld County, Colorado commissioners voted 3-2 to approve the $1.67 billion Chalk Bluffs Energy Project in the north of the county, between U.S. 85 and Colorado 390: 79 wind turbines and more than 2,200 acres of solar, 805 megawatts in all. Commissioners Kevin Ross, Lynette Peppler and Perry Buck voted yes, and Jason Maxey and Scott James voted no, according to the Greeley Tribune. The developer projects $182 million in construction-period economic impact, 1,090 construction jobs and 55 permanent positions. Opponents organized as "Save the Chalk Bluffs" and described 25,800 affected acres as "destroyed wildlife corridors, scarred landscapes, and heavy industrial gear where our wild spaces used to be." Two of five commissioners still voted no on a project with those numbers attached.
  • Contracted to the Code: On Sept. 23, NYSERDA selected eight battery projects totaling 950 megawatts from 46 bids offering about 6 gigawatts, in the first round of its Bulk Energy Storage Program. The winners range from Chautauqua and Allegany counties in western New York to the Bronx and Long Island, with four to eight hours of duration. Every project is contractually bound to the state storage safety codes that took effect Jan. 1, 2026. The state also awarded 13 large-scale renewable projects totaling 719 megawatts, for 1.7 GW overall. The next storage solicitation is due by year-end. New York also leads the country in local battery pauses, most of them over fire safety, and the state is now writing its safety code into the contracts at the same time.
  • Eleven Hundred in the Pines: Georgia Power is adding 1,137 megawatts of solar across seven rural counties (Sumter and Irwin at 200 MW each, Jefferson, Emanuel, Warren, Decatur and Appling), with power flowing as early as 2029, according to a Sept. 14 Hoodline report on the Public Service Commission approvals. The utility's load forecast calls for 8,500 MW of growth over six years, much of it from data centers, including a planned $20 billion OpenAI complex near Savannah. Consumer advocates are pointing to residential bills that rose more than $43 a month from 2023 to 2025. Seven host counties means seven local conversations, and the bill argument will be made in each of them.
  • Everything Stops at the TCEQ: On Sept. 21, Gov. Greg Abbott ordered the Texas Commission on Environmental Quality to stop issuing permits for data center projects until ERCOT finishes auditing every data center in its interconnection queue. This follows his August moratorium on new data center grid approvals. The directive requires TCEQ to report back by Oct. 19, and ERCOT has said the audit could run into December. Troutman Pepper Locke reports that projects will have to meet six conditions before proceeding, including paying for their own electrical infrastructure. For our clients, the question is what happens to generation that was sized for large loads now sitting in an audit.
  • Solar for All, Restored: On Sept. 23, Judge Tanya Chutkan of the U.S. District Court for D.C. held that the EPA unlawfully cancelled Solar for All grants, including Harris County's, which put about $250 million for solar programs across Texas back in play. Harris County Attorney Abbie Kamin said the county will work to protect the money for local projects. This is the week's one federal item. The program funds solar for low-income households, and the ruling restores money that local governments had already planned around.
On the Frontlines
  • ORDenial Prohibited: Oregon's Land Use Board of Appeals reversed Marion County's denial of RWE's 15-acre Remington battery project on exclusive farm use land. The county had ruled that a battery only stores power and so is not a "utility facility." LUBA found that it "furnishes" electricity and makes service "more efficient and effective," and wrote that "denial of the application is prohibited as a matter of law." In a state that restricts farmland use this tightly, battery storage now qualifies under the utility-facility definition.
  • ILOne More Month: The Madison County Board postponed its vote on a special-use permit for Grandview Wittman 2's 41-acre solar facility in Jarvis Township, west of Troy, until Oct. 21. The zoning board of appeals opposed the permit unanimously. A year ago the same county board reversed its own denial of another solar permit. Illinois law limits how strict the county's standards can be, which makes a delay the safer option for the board than a denial.
  • MIFirst Reading Down: On Sept. 22, the Oshtemo Township Board approved the first reading of a moratorium on new solar applications, with adoption set for Oct. 13, while the planning commission writes an energy ordinance covering solar and storage. Next door in Comstock, a clearly written ordinance produced a battery approval this month. Oshtemo has chosen to pause while it writes its own.
  • WANext to the Substation: GridStor has proposed a 100-megawatt battery on seven acres in Custer, north of Ferndale, tied to PSE's Portal Way substation. Whatcom County's 2022 code already allows storage in industrial zones and near substations with a conditional use permit, and bans it on farmland, so the application goes to planning staff and a hearing examiner. Neighboring Skagit County banned storage in agricultural zones in 2024 after a fight near Sedro-Woolley. GridStor said that "having standards in place is helpful because it establishes clear expectations upfront for the community."
  • NYFifty and Counting: Fifty Adirondack towns have now approved a battery moratorium or ban, or adopted BESS-specific rules, according to Adirondack Explorer's tracking. Residents cited fire safety, emergency response, noise and community character. The stated goal is to have zoning in place before a developer arrives. Adirondack Explorer counts more than 100 municipalities across the state with similar delays.
  • WYSmaller, Still Big: Repsol's scaled-down Laramie Range Wind Project, now 139 turbines on about 41,200 acres, down from 170 on 56,000, returns to Laramie County after the state's permit. Public comment closes Sept. 28, the planning commission hearing is Oct. 22, and commissioners decide Nov. 17. Opponents are focusing on 541 acre-feet of construction water and the strain on emergency services in the remote Horse Creek area. The county rejected the earlier site plan, and the company asked a district court to review that decision.
  • MNThe Growth-Corridor Objection: Sauk Rapids is asking the Minnesota PUC to reject NextEra's 100-megawatt Mayhew Lake battery in Benton County. The city's argument is not fire risk. It says the site sits on a development corridor where the county spent $5 million on infrastructure, and that a battery pays far less in taxes than homes or businesses would. The mayor said, "We have concerns with it on that development corridor." The PUC decision is tentatively set for Oct. 22. This lost-development argument is harder to answer with a safety plan than a fire objection is.
  • OHChanged His Mind in the Room: Fayette County commissioners adopted a data center moratorium through June 1, 2027 covering seven unincorporated townships. Commissioner Donnie Fleak said he "changed his stance after hearing public objections." The same day, Warren County commissioners directed their prosecutor to draft a temporary moratorium to study grid, water and zoning impacts.
  • IAWorking From Scratch: Marshall County supervisors voted 3-0 on Sept. 24 for a six-month moratorium on data centers, AI computing, data mining and crypto operations while the zoning commission drafts rules. The zoning administrator said the county is "working from scratch." One resident's observation: "the counties are setting resolutions, and the cities are building data centers."
  • ARRules, Not a Pause: On Sept. 22, Pulaski County's Quorum Court voted 11-4 to adopt a permitting ordinance for "high-intensity digital infrastructure," meaning facilities with 10 MW or more of continuous demand or at least 100,000 square feet of computing space. Applicants must disclose electrical load, water use and emergency plans, and obtain a conditional use permit. The county chose to write rules rather than pause, which is the less common choice this fall.
Plays of the Week
  • Participating ranchers (CO, WY)Let the Lessor Talk: The strongest pro-project voices on the two biggest Western wind projects this month were the ranchers leasing their land. In Weld County, Scott Salo argued that turbines take 18 acres of his ranch while cattle keep grazing, and that "Colorado is the No. 1 state for losing agricultural land to development, and it ain't wind turbines." In Laramie County, Charlie Farthing described wind leases as the way his grandkids keep the ranch. Both men turned the farmland argument around: the threat to agricultural land in their telling is subdivision, not turbines. Weld approved its project 3-2.
  • Entergy LouisianaFull Freight, No Abatement: Washington Parish lifted its solar moratorium for Entergy's 200-MW, $400 million Bogalusa West Solar project over resident objections about drainage, wildlife, property values and battery fire. Entergy committed to paying full taxes with no exemptions or abatements: $82 million in property tax over the project's life plus a $2.9 million permit fee. Councilwoman Kim Wagner said, "Our parish needs the money." By giving up the abatement, Entergy made the vote about revenue the parish would actually collect. The parish's battery storage moratorium is still in effect.
  • DTE EnergyAnswer by the 30th: DTE has given Escanaba Township until Sept. 30 to say whether it will permit a proposed 150-MW solar project locally or whether DTE should go to the Michigan PSC under Public Act 233. The township has no compatible renewable energy ordinance and no moratorium. DTE says it is "simply asking for clarity." The effect is to put a deadline on the township's decision. Board members pushed back ("How many megawatts are you looking at? How much acreage?") and scheduled a public special meeting where DTE will present maps and setbacks. Residents are divided, and some pointed to neighboring Baldwin Township, which negotiated directly with DTE.
  • American Stewards of LibertyIntervene in Everything: The Texas property-rights group intervened in all five segments of the 765-kV buildout so that it would have standing for judicial review in each one. Now that segments are being approved, it is seeking rehearing and planning appeals that run into the next legislative session, with an amicus brief from Attorney General Ken Paxton and a Texas Public Policy Foundation coalition behind it. Intervening in every docket early is what makes appeals possible later. Opposition to long transmission lines in other states is likely to follow the same approach.

Ringgold County denied a request because its ordinance already answered the question. Oregon's appeals board overturned a denial because state law already defined a battery as a utility facility. New York made its safety code part of the contract, and Washington Parish kept its battery pause because it has not written a battery ordinance yet. Wherever a written standard existed this week, the board applied it. Where none existed, the board paused. For every active file, find out which standard the board will apply before the application goes in, and if no standard exists yet, offer to help write one.
