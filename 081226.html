Good morning.

The single most useful number our clients will read this week landed on our desks this morning. The U.S. Energy Information Administration now reports the country is on track to double its battery-storage fleet by the end of 2028 — operators have told the agency they plan to bring another 54 gigawatts online over the next two and a half years, on top of the ~52 GW already standing by mid-2026. Read that as a siting forecast, not a market one. Fifty-four gigawatts is thousands of new cells, containers, and substations that each have to clear a folding-table hearing somewhere — and, per the EIA, the biggest ones sit right next to solar farms. The buildout everyone models as a capacity curve is, for us, a permitting queue.

You didn't have to look far this week to watch that abstraction become an agenda. In one Michigan county, three different local boards took up storage and solar rules in the same five days: Oshtemo Township ran a second reading on a solar moratorium and sends its energy-systems ordinance to the Planning Commission tomorrow, neighboring Comstock Township holds a public hearing on a live BESS project the same night, and the City of Kalamazoo set a data-center-moratorium vote for Monday — with battery storage flagged as the next category in line. Today's Deep Dive is that collision: the macro says build, the local calendar says pause, and the gap between them is the work. Radar has the money still moving (Avantus's $1.05B close, Energy Vault's Texas batteries), and Project Updates has the Kalamazoo County docket in full.

Must Read

Double by Twenty-Eight

Every so often a data release reframes the whole business, and this morning's did. The EIA's latest Preliminary Monthly Electric Generator Inventory shows utility-scale battery storage has grown at an average annual rate of 70% for three straight years, reaching nearly 52 GW of nameplate capacity by mid-2026. The power system carried 43.6 GW at the end of 2025; operators added another 8.3 GW in the first six months of this year alone. And the pipeline is heavier still: developers have told the agency they plan to bring 54 GW more online over the next 30 months — 14 GW in the back half of 2026, 26 GW in 2027, and 14 GW in 2028. If those plans hold, the U.S. battery fleet more than doubles from where it stands today. Wood Mackenzie's Q2 read is even longer: 200 GW / 655 GWh of cumulative storage by 2031, roughly quadrupling current capacity.

Here is the part that should reorganize how we staff and sequence. The EIA is explicit that the largest storage units in the country are the ones bolted onto solar farms. Its examples are our project types: the Bellefield Solar and Energy Storage Farm in Utah (500 MW of PV paired with 500 MW of storage, doubling to a second phase by November), Nevada's Gemini Solar Hybrid (690 MW of solar, 380 MW of storage), Florida's Manatee center (409 MW of battery charged off 75 MW of solar). The economics that drive the coupling are simple and durable — store cheap midday solar, discharge into the evening price spike — which means the storage boom isn't a separate sector bolting onto renewables. It is renewables, wearing a second permit. For a community-engagement shop, that's the whole point: the solar application our clients used to run is now, more often than not, a solar-plus-storage application, and the storage half is the half the room is afraid of.

Because that's the rub the capacity curve hides. Ten gigawatts a year on paper is thousands of discrete land-use decisions in practice, and the objection that has decided battery rooms all summer — lithium-ion fire risk — doesn't scale down as deployment scales up. It scales with it. Every one of those 54 planned gigawatts arrives as a specific container on a specific parcel next to specific neighbors, and the EIA's own numbers guarantee the volume of hearings only rises from here. The developers who treat the storage question as an afterthought bolted to a solar filing will keep losing the rooms where the panels were never the problem. The ones who treat fire safety, first-responder coordination, setbacks, and decommissioning as the lead of the engagement — not the appendix — are the ones who convert a national tailwind into a built project.

And you didn't have to theorize about it this week; you could watch it. In a single county in southwest Michigan, three boards moved on storage and solar rules inside five days. Oshtemo Township — where a greenfield developer has an eye on a substation-adjacent parcel — carried a solar moratorium to second reading Tuesday and sends its solar-wind-and-storage ordinance to the Planning Commission Thursday. Comstock Township holds a public hearing Thursday on an actual BESS proposal from Swan Energy and Cygnet Energy. And the City of Kalamazoo, having heard weeks of public comment, set a moratorium vote for Monday and told staff the zoning pause should reach data centers now and battery storage next. One county. One week. Four rooms. Multiply that by the EIA's 54 gigawatts and you have the shape of the next three years — not a market that clears itself, but a permit that has to be won a room at a time.

The Takeaway

The storage boom isn't coming to the sector — it's coming to the county calendar, and the EIA just put a number on it: 54 new gigawatts in 30 months, doubling the fleet by 2028, almost all of it needing a local yes. Three instructions. One: sell the solar-plus-storage project as a storage project. The EIA is blunt that the biggest units ride on solar farms, which means the battery — not the panels — is the risk the room actually adjudicates; lead the engagement with fire suppression, first-responder training, setbacks, and a decommissioning bond, because that is the half that carries or sinks the whole filing. Two: staff for hearing volume, not headline capacity. Ten gigawatts a year is thousands of discrete land-use fights; a pipeline built on "the macro is with us" will still lose the folding-table rooms one at a time. Three: watch the county that moves on three fronts at once. Kalamazoo County wrote solar, storage, and data-center rules in a single week — when a locality opens all three files together, the smart developer is the one already in the room helping write the standards, not the one reacting to them after they pass.

Also On Our Radar
  • Capital Doesn't Care About the Culture War: proof the money keeps flowing to solar-and-storage even as the local rooms get louder. US independent power producer Avantus (the former 8minutenergy) closed a $1.05 billion corporate credit facility on Aug. 10 to push its pipeline from development into construction across California and the Desert Southwest. The scale is the signal: Avantus is sitting on a 24 GW development pipeline — 13 GW of solar paired with 44 GWh of storage — and a lender roster that reads like a syndication league table (SMBC, ING, HSBC, KKR, Truist, Mizuho, CIBC, and more). It's not abstract, either. In July the company energized Aratina 1 in Kern County (200 MW solar / 500 MWh storage), locked $525M in construction financing for Aratina 2, and signed a 20-year PPA for Rexford 2 (200 MW / 800 MWh), and says it's on track to bring 788 MW into operation with another 800 MW under construction by year-end. When institutional capital this size keeps upsizing solar-plus-storage facilities, the read-across for a hostile board is simple: this product is getting built with or without you — the only variable your county controls is whether it happens on terms you helped write.
  • Batteries as the On-Ramp: the storage-meets-data-center story, and it routes straight around the interconnection queue our clients wait in. Energy Vault signed a strategic agreement, announced Aug. 7, to deploy 1.25 GW of power infrastructure for a hyperscaler AI data-center customer in Texas — batteries, grid-forming power-conversion systems, and control software paired with Caterpillar gensets to run the site as a self-contained plant. The tell for us is in the timeline: initial deployments land in four to twelve months, "operating independently of traditional utility interconnection schedules," letting the customer power up before a permanent grid tie exists, then fold in utility power and renewables later. That's the load side of the same coin the EIA flagged this morning — storage isn't just soaking up solar, it's becoming the tool that lets AI demand skip the queue entirely. For any client whose project is stranded in an interconnection cluster, watch this model closely: the data centers straining the grid may increasingly bring their own batteries rather than wait in the same line, and that reshapes both the load forecasts and the politics of who gets connected first.

  • MIOshtemo Opens All Three Files at Once: the township we've flagged since Vol. 38 is now acting, not previewing. Per this week's public-meetings docket, the Oshtemo Township Board carried a second reading of its solar-development moratorium at Tuesday's meeting, and its Planning Commission begins review of a working-draft Energy Systems ordinance "addressing solar, wind and energy storage" this Thursday, Aug. 13. This sits on top of the one-year BESS moratorium the township already enacted last fall after New York-based greenfield developer NewEdge signaled a battery project near an ITC substation. The pattern to note for the file: Oshtemo is writing all three renewable land uses into one framework simultaneously — the "open every file at once" move from today's Deep Dive, playing out in real time. If there's a west-Michigan parcel in the book, the standards being drafted Thursday are the ones our clients will have to meet.
  • MIComstock Takes a Live Battery to Public Hearing: a rare in-window item with an actual project attached, not just an ordinance. The Comstock Township Planning Commission holds a public hearing Thursday, Aug. 13 on a proposed BESS facility from Swan Energy and Cygnet Energy, following a July 23 informational meeting where the developers laid out the project and their risk-mitigation measures. This is the exact room the Deep Dive is about — a specific battery, a specific parcel, a neighborhood weighing fire and safety concerns — and it's worth watching how Swan and Cygnet handle the fire question in an open hearing, because whichever way Comstock leans becomes a data point for every other Kalamazoo-area board writing rules the same month.
  • MIKalamazoo Sets the Data-Center Vote — With Storage Next in Line: the city moved from talk to a calendar this week. Out of the Aug. 10 Committee of the Whole, commissioners directed staff to draft a moratorium resolution for a vote at Monday's (Aug. 17) regular meeting, and to start the longer zoning-ordinance moratorium — covering data centers, AI data centers, and crypto-mining — through the Planning Commission beginning no earlier than the first week of September. City Attorney Aaron Leal was careful to frame it as a pause, not a ban: Michigan's Zoning Enabling Act bars permanent prohibitions of lawful uses, and courts view moratoriums past a year skeptically. The line that matters for us: commissioners flagged battery storage as "a related but potentially distinct category that could also need attention" — i.e., the storage moratorium conversation in this city hasn't started yet, but it's already on the whiteboard. A locality that pauses data centers this month is a locality worth engaging on storage before it pauses that too.
