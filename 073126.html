Friday morning, team.

We promised Wednesday we'd carry the Henry County outcome, so start there. Tuesday night, more than 100 residents packed the Henry County, Virginia, supervisors' room for the data-center hearing — and got the opposite of what they came for. The board voted unanimously to strip data centers out of the zoning amendments entirely, "going back to the drawing board," in Chairman Jim Adams's words, which leaves the county with no data-center parameters at all for the time being. Residents had asked for stricter rules; they got the rules pulled. But the board approved the rest of the package — and buried in "the rest" was the move we flagged: lowering the solar-acreage cap to 1,754 acres, the exact total the county has already approved. A cap set to the approved total is not a cap. It is a closed door. No new solar gets approved in Henry County.

The mechanics are the lesson. The room's whole emotional charge was aimed at server halls; the solar cap rode through underneath it, in a county that has approved solar at one of Virginia's highest rates and has actually built only a fraction of the acreage it capped against. That's the pattern we keep naming — the county capped against paper, not panels. And it did it in the shadow of Virginia's new "ban the bans" statute, which bars outright prohibitions and routes every denial to Richmond with written justification; Sen. Schuyler VanValkenburg has already promised a "heavier hand" for localities that flout the spirit of it. Henry didn't ban solar. It set the ceiling at the floor and dared the state to notice. Whether Richmond reads a cap-at-the-approved-total as a de facto ban is now the live question, and Henry is the test case.

Also On Our Radar
  • One TWh, Bundled: the offtaker story boards almost never hear. ContourGlobal has signed a long-term corporate PPA with Tesla for Project Sterling in Arizona — 509 MW (dc) / 450 MW (ac) of solar paired with 360 MW / 1.4 GWh of battery storage — under which Tesla buys roughly 1 TWh a year, about 90% of the plant's output, as bundled electricity and RECs to power its California operations. The project sits on the Western Area Power Administration grid but holds firm point-to-point transmission rights into CAISO; on-site construction starts this year, commercial operation targets 2028. Two things to carry into a host-community conversation. First, this is a hyperscaler-adjacent tech giant buying renewables, not a data center demanding grid power — the exact inversion of the story every board assumes. Second, the whole modern project is in one deal: solar, storage, and the transmission rights that make it dispatchable. When a client's project gets framed as "just panels," this is the shape of what they're actually building.
  • Six Times by Thirty: the market-data answer to "is storage even real yet." A new GlobalData analysis, reported by pv magazine July 27, projects global battery storage to grow sixfold between 2025 and 2030 at a 42% compound annual rate, with the U.S. and China holding 74.6% of installed capacity. The domestic numbers are the ones for our decks: the U.S. closed 2025 with a record 57.6 GWh of new storage (137 GWh cumulative), Q1 2026 hit 9.7 GWh — up 32% year over year and the largest first quarter ever, and the EIA expects 24 GW of utility-scale batteries online in 2026, concentrated in Texas (53%), California (14%), and Arizona (13%). Four-hour duration is now the procurement baseline, and developers increasingly favor co-located solar-plus-storage to share a single interconnection. File that last point next to Wednesday's New York piece: co-location isn't just cheaper, it's the pairing that buys ORES jurisdiction over an otherwise freezable standalone battery. The technology a board calls speculative is the fastest-scaling asset on the grid.

  • MIOshtemo Sequences the Pause: the Oshtemo Township board adopted a first reading of a six-month moratorium on principal-use (large-scale) solar July 28, with a second reading set for Aug. 11. Separately, the township's draft energy ordinance — the one that will govern battery storage — goes to the Planning Commission Aug. 13, and Supervisor Cheri Bell said the board expects to recommend extending the existing BESS moratorium when it returns in November. This is Kalamazoo County again, one township over from last week's Kalamazoo Township adoptions, and the tell is the sequencing: pause first, ordinance second, with the study window stretching from summer into November. That window is the drafting table, and right now no developer is sitting at it.
  • ILCatlin Buys Another Month: the Catlin Village Board extended its solar-farm moratorium to Aug. 31 Tuesday night while it keeps rewriting its zoning, as Earthrise Energy's proposed 1,400-acre solar project along Catlin-Tilton Road waits on approvals. "Say No to Solar Farms" signs are up in the Vermilion County village, and neighboring Tilton's own moratorium already lapsed July 1 — a reminder that these pauses expire on different clocks even within a mile of each other. Same structure as Oshtemo: the freeze runs until the ordinance is finished, and the ordinance is where the county's posture on every future project actually gets written.
On the Frontlines

Light by the freshness rule this Friday — most in-geography contested renewable hearings fell outside the seven-day window. The marquee fight that landed is below; the week's biggest signals are carried up in Radar (the Tesla PPA and the U.S. storage build) and in Plays.

  • MIWexford Says No, With an Asterisk: the Wexford Joint Planning Commission on Monday unanimously recommended against a zoning amendment that would have advanced Ranger Power's proposed 1,500-acre solar array on land zoned for agriculture and forestry. About 70 residents turned out, more than 20 spoke against, and State Sen. Michele Hoitenga showed up to back them; a commissioner called the amendment "too broad." Because it's a recommendation, each of the nine townships under the joint commission now votes, simple majority. Here's the asterisk our Midwest clients need to read: the commission's own attorney, from Bauckham Thall, told the room that Michigan's Public Act 233 has "taken away some of your authority," and that "it's not as easy as saying 'I don't want it' anymore." So even a clean opposition win in a PA 233 state is provisional — the state backstop is still standing behind it. Watch which of the nine townships decides to test that line, because one of them will.
Plays of the Week
  • RESET / UW–ExtensionGet It in Writing: the opposition's new coaching program is also the clearest map of our own opening. Jessica Bradish, a University of Wisconsin–Extension renewable-energy specialist working with Renewable Energy Siting and Engagement for Tomorrow (RESET), is running community meetings across rural Wisconsin teaching towns to lock in benefits before construction: decommissioning terms, solar-panel disposal and recycling, road repair, topsoil and erosion controls, funding for specialized firefighter training, and bankruptcy protections. Her line is the whole play: "We want it all in writing … Once a local board approves and builds a solar site, the community loses all its leverage." The lesson for RenUSA cuts both ways. This checklist is being handed to the towns our clients want to build in — so the developer who walks in during the study window already offering the decommissioning bond, the road-repair commitment, and the fire-training fund isn't conceding, they're writing the community-benefit agreement before the extension office teaches the town to demand it. Bring the term sheet before they hand out the checklist.
  • Arena Cavern Solar (IL)Move the Fence: a small project, a clean sequence, worth stealing. Ahead of a July 28 Quincy Plan Commission vote on two modest permits (3.0 and 5.0 MWac on land above old mining caves), developer Arena Cavern had held a winter open house, listened, and then moved its panels from about 150 feet off the nearest homes to about 350 feet, adding perimeter tree screening and pollinator vegetation. The commission recommended approval, and the city's planning director explicitly credited the setback change as the adaptation that made the project "a better product." The replicable part is the order of operations: open house first, visible concession second, favorable recommendation third. But note the ceiling on the play — neighbors still spoke against it, and the testimony that landed was emotional ("the farmland where I grew up playing"), the kind of grief no setback fixes. Geometry buys the board; it doesn't buy the grandmother in tears at the podium. Pair the setback with the relationship, or you win the vote and lose the town.

Capped against paper, not panels — and the drafting window is the whole game. Be in the room before the ordinance is written.
