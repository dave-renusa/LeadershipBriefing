RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 46 · Friday, August 28, 2026 · Version 2

Friday morning, team.

This was a week decided by paperwork, and the paperwork was almost entirely one-sided. In Coweta County, Georgia, a project that was already zoned industrial, asked for no variances, conformed to the county's own data-center ordinance and offered to build two road extensions was denied unanimously, with no commissioner discussion at all — because the file contained 121 letters of opposition from 112 people and not one letter in support. In Wheeling, opponents denied a hearing by city council rented a hall and held their own, with a sign-in sheet at the door and the developer's lawyer on the panel. In Lubbock, a citizens group skipped persuasion entirely and used the city charter to petition an 18-month moratorium onto the council's calendar, converting a discretionary ask into a mandatory vote. Three Virginia counties each put $250,000 behind intervening against a 765-kV line, and a Kansas landowner couple sued a county commission for approving a solar permit on an incomplete record. The through-line is uncomfortable and useful: entitlement quality is no longer what decides these votes. The record is. Whoever fills the file wins the room, and right now the people filling it are not us.

Also On Our Radar
  • Boring Is the Product: ERCOT discharged a record 13,256 MW of batteries at 7:45 p.m. on Aug. 23 and issued zero conservation appeals all summer — against six appeals in the last week of August 2023 alone. The price data is the argument. At 4 p.m. on Aug. 24, with load above 90 GW, real-time prices ran about $46/MWh with gas at 42% and solar at 36% of the mix; the same hour in 2023, with wind and solar around 20% combined, cleared at $85/MWh. At 7:50 p.m. batteries put out 11,573 MW at roughly $125/MWh while 2023 was bouncing near the $5,000 cap. "We're really seeing peak demand divorced from peak prices," said UT research scientist Joshua Rhodes; Aurora's Olivier Beaufils was blunter — "We've added so much battery capacity that they've cannibalized, they've eaten their own lunch." Texas also set a 91,308 MW load record and a 53,000 MW renewables record this summer. When a county asks whether storage actually does anything, this is the cleanest answer in the country.
  • Ninety Percent, Still: EIA Form 860M data shows 90% of U.S. capacity built in the first half of 2026 was solar, storage or wind, with solar and batteries alone accounting for more than two-thirds of everything added January through June. Texas led overall; New Mexico came second almost entirely because the 3.65-GW SunZia project — the largest wind build in America — finished in June despite a federal posture openly hostile to wind construction; Arizona's solar-plus-storage run put it third. Tax-credit phase-outs, a permitting freeze and new solar tariffs have raised costs without yet changing the construction mix. Keep this chart handy: the "renewables are collapsing" frame is now empirically wrong, and it shows up in hearing rooms constantly.
  • Maine Finally Buys the Wire: the Maine PUC on Tuesday picked Clearway Energy to build 800 MW of onshore wind in Aroostook County plus a 1.2-GW Avangrid transmission line, with the wind award contingent on the line getting built — the structural fix that killed the last attempt in 2023. "I think we now have a pair of projects that are incredibly likely to be built," said PUC Chair Philip Bartlett. Commissioners put net consumer benefits near $400 million and more than 2,500 construction jobs, and Commissioner Carolyn Gilbert estimated the wind farm could cut winter oil burn at New England power plants by 10%. The genuinely novel part is the cost-sharing: Connecticut, Massachusetts, Rhode Island and Vermont agreed to split the transmission bill, leaving Maine customers paying under 11%. Commissioner Patrick Scully: "I've not seen anything like this in the United States." Renew Northeast's Francis Pullaro warned that the last line through Maine drew an outcry that added years and more than $500 million — the engagement work has not started.
  • Capital Says Yes Anyway: two independent power producers closed more than $1 billion in a single week. Swift Current Energy landed a $750 million three-year corporate credit facility led by Crédit Agricole CIB, ING Capital and Truist Securities, with a $250 million accordion. ING Capital's Sven Wellock framed it as a maturity signal: the deal "reflects the continued maturation of the renewable energy sector, where scaled platforms increasingly require flexible corporate capital alongside project-level financing." Separately, KKR-backed Avantus closed $300 million in Truist tax equity for the 150-MW/452-MWh Aratina 2 solar-plus-storage project in Kern County, California, which carries a 15-year PPA with Southern California Edison on top of roughly $525 million in BBVA-led construction debt. Completed, Aratina reaches 350 MW of solar and 952 MWh of storage. Lenders are still underwriting this asset class at scale; the binding constraint is local.
  • Nine Percent and Climbing: PJM's independent market monitor put data-center load at 9% of the wholesale power price through July — $10.48 of every $116.53/MWh — all of it flowing through the capacity market, and that figure excludes energy and transmission effects. Across the last four capacity auctions, data-center load growth drove a combined $29.4 billion increase in capacity revenues, and the monitor wrote that the total "will continue to grow until the issues associated with the addition of large data center loads are addressed." Total first-half wholesale cost rose 46% year over year to $56.7 billion. Buried in the same report: wind supplied 4.5% of PJM electricity year to date and solar 3.5%, up from 3.7% and 2.9%. That is a number worth quoting in an affordability fight, because it separates the cost driver from the thing being blamed for it.
  • Virtual Power, Real Deadline: two California bills from Sen. Josh Becker cleared a key committee hurdle ahead of Aug. 31 floor votes. SB 913 gives the CPUC until mid-2028 to write rules letting virtual power plants count toward resource adequacy; SB 905 requires grid-utilization metrics from PG&E, SCE and SDG&E. Newsom vetoed a similar slate in 2025 and has until the end of September to decide. A Brattle analysis for GridLab found VPPs could cover more than 15% of California peak demand and save $550 million a year by 2035; Becker's frame is affordability — Californians pay roughly $1 billion annually to keep fossil peakers on standby, and "we can get rid of those if we take better advantage of what's already in people's homes." California now has 2.5 GW of residential batteries. PG&E and SCE dropped their opposition after June amendments.
  • Emergency, Declared: the president signed an executive order Wednesday declaring a national emergency and preparing to block foreign-made transformers, batteries, inverters and associated software from the bulk power system, with DOE required to write implementing rules within 120 days. The retroactive clause is the one to read twice: it lets the Energy Secretary "impose conditions on the continued use, operation, maintenance, servicing, or updating" of covered equipment already installed. NEMA's Bridget Bartol called it "kind of an update and expansion" of 2020's EO 13920 but said software raises "a lot of questions around what does it mean to be designed and developed by a covered foreign entity," adding that "there's a need for a lot more clarity." China accounts for more than 80% of world production of some grid equipment, including lithium-ion cells. Equipment provenance is now a second federal category opponents can raise, three weeks after the FCC opened the first.
Project Updates
  • VAThree-Quarters of a Million to Say No: Buckingham, Louisa and Goochland counties have each allocated up to $250,000 to fight Valley Link's Joshua Falls-to-Yeat 765-kV line, a roughly 115-mile route from Campbell to Culpeper County developed by a Dominion, FirstEnergy Transmission and Transource joint venture, with towers reaching 165 feet. Frederick County added its own $250,000 in July, and four West Virginia county commissions — Barbour, Upshur, Lewis and Preston — have formally opposed the companion 260-mile Valley North segment. Louisa has retained Dale Mullen of Whiteford, Taylor & Preston as coordinating counsel for a nine-county coalition. Board Chair Duane Adams supplied the frame: "Who in Louisa County wants an interstate highway system running through the middle of our county, whether it's for cars or electricity?" Dominion expects to file with the SCC in September and says the opposition does not change its timeline. SCC spokesman Greg Weatherford noted intervenor status lets counties "cross examine witnesses, call their own witnesses, and enter things into evidence" — which is what the money actually buys.
  • MIGustin Says No to the Project It Already Approved: Gustin Township denied a special use permit on Aug. 6 for Consumers Energy's 3,985.6-acre, 335-MW Gustin Solar project in Alcona County, and the developer has not yet said what it will do. The history is the lesson: Gustin approved a permit in 2024 when this was Sapling Solar under Ranger Power; Consumers acquired and substantially expanded it in September 2025, and the township that had said yes to the small version said no to the big one. Landowners hold 30-year leases across a footprint that would put solar on 2,684 acres and clear 35 acres of trees. Harrisville Township's planning commission votes Oct. 7. Zoning administrator Adam Brege says the township is "waiting to hear the response from Consumers." State Rep. Cam Cavitt, R-Cheboygan, is already framing it against PA 233, which "stripped local governments of much of their authority" — meaning a state-preemption appeal here lands in a district whose representative has pre-committed to opposing it.
  • KSThe Neighbors Who Weren't Offered an NDA: Keith and Jody Pfannenstiel sued the Ellis County Commission, the county clerk and developer ibV Energy Partners over the June 2 conditional-use permit for the roughly 1,800-acre, 175-MW, $275 million Tallgrass solar project west of Hays. The suit calls the approval "arbitrary" for deferring drainage, stormwater, road, emergency-response and decommissioning studies to later approvals. The detail that should stop everyone: plaintiffs' attorney John Bird says ibV offered compensation to other neighbors under nondisclosure agreements but not to his clients — "They're not opposed to solar, but they don't believe the county followed all the rules." The suit also alleges the county quoted $2,046.42 to fill a Kansas Open Records Act request. County Administrator Darin Myers denies wrongdoing and ibV says the case has not delayed construction. Selective, confidential neighbor payments are the single most reliable way to convert an indifferent landowner into a plaintiff.
  • NMFifty Feet to Two Hundred: PNM and Pattern Energy held a two-hour virtual open house Aug. 24 on the Mid-State Transmission Project, a 345-kV line running more than 100 miles through Torrance, Socorro and Valencia counties from the Western Spirit Switchyard to the Hidden Mountain Substation near Los Lunas. Roughly 1,200 postcards went out; attendance peaked at 41. Loma Parda residents objected to widening an existing 50-foot right-of-way to 200 feet, and Manzano Land Grant Association president Lenora Lovato Romero put it in the chat: "I say NO to the PNM Project." Structures would be 80- to 125-foot steel H-frames. A PNM representative conceded on the record that the company would use eminent domain where no agreement and no feasible alternate route exist, and could not say whether the line is meant in part to serve data-center load. Three applications go to the PRC between mid-September and early October. A 3% turnout on a virtual open house is not consultation; it is a compliance artifact, and the record will read that way at the PRC.
  • LAWashington Parish Holds the Pause: a packed council chamber on Aug. 24 rejected Resolution No. 26-1526, which would have lifted the parish's utility-scale solar moratorium while extending a separate 90-day pause on battery storage. The moratorium — triggered when a BESS component surfaced inside a proposed solar development tied to the Bogalusa West project with Entergy Louisiana — has about 45 days left to run. Tim Tackett, who told the council he spent 42 years in commercial power generation, argued the existing ordinance is deficient on interconnection standards, fire protection, construction requirements, decommissioning and cybersecurity. The revealing testimony came from Sheriff Jason Smith, who said he personally opposes solar and urged lifting the pause anyway: "We have the opportunity to increase our budget by a million dollars a year." The issue returns Sept. 14. Second Louisiana parish in two weeks to hold a project on the ordinance rather than the merits.
  • KSDickinson Buys Three Years: the county commission passed a three-year moratorium covering data centers, battery storage and renewable energy — structured to study one sector per year, with each sector's pause expiring after its study year. Chairman Kenny Roelofsen worked hard on the framing: "This isn't a prohibition on these different industries. This is an exploratory moratorium so that we can better define and better regulate these types of industries. I think a lot of people think of a moratorium as a ban, and that's not what it is." He conceded the county has not started on storage — "When you get into battery, I'll be honest. We haven't talked about it yet" — and that no permit request has been filed. Planning and zoning director Tim Hamilton is already collecting comment and a town hall is planned. A pre-emptive pause with no application pending is the cheapest possible win for opponents and the most expensive possible loss for a developer who shows up in year two.
  • INDearborn Slows It Down, Section by Section: the county adopted a one-year moratorium in February on commercial solar and battery applications, stood up a Citizens Advisory Committee to draft amendments, and this week the Plan Commission walked the resulting draft solar ordinance section by section, taking public comment after each one. A special discussion-only meeting is set for Sept. 9, and the earliest possible vote is Sept. 28 — and even that requires a separate legal advertisement. The draft is posted publicly and the meeting video is on YouTube. Read it as a technique, not a delay: serializing a single adversarial hearing into a dozen small negotiations makes the process almost impossible to attack as closed, while running the clock past two construction seasons.
On the Frontlines
  • SCA Factory Is Not a Permitted Use: Circuit Judge William McKinnon took about 24 hours to reject Silfab Solar's motion to reconsider, leaving intact his ruling that the York County Board of Zoning Appeals was right in 2024 to find that solar cell manufacturing is not a permitted use in light-industrial zoning. Zoning board attorney Ross Appel at the Aug. 26 rehearing: "We had an extensive hearing, and he considered everything. Nothing raised in Silfab's motion to reconsider is anything new." The opposition group Move Silfab called it "a huge victory for our community." York County says the ruling applies to future permit applications but "does not automatically cancel uses, permits, determinations, or approvals" issued before it, leaving retroactivity to the courts; Silfab's Fort Mill plant, opened early this year, continues to operate under its existing certificate of occupancy. The domestic-manufacturing buildout everyone in this industry has been asking for is now hitting the same zoning-definition wall the projects hit.
  • DEOne Resident, Still Standing: the Delaware Environmental Appeals Board unanimously denied DNREC's motion to dismiss Sussex County resident Edward Bintz's 2024 appeal of two conditional federal-consistency letters the department issued for US Wind's Maryland offshore project, clearing it for a full hearing as soon as September. The project would land export cables at 3R's Beach and connect near the Indian River Power Plant. DNREC argued its role is finished and the matter now sits with the federal government, leaving no state remedy — the board did not buy it. Bintz's attorney Jane Brady framed it as a Coastal Zone Management Act compliance question against a state actor and argued nobody has heard the merits: "There's no risk to the state, there's no risk to the environment for this case to be heard." A separate beach-construction permit challenge goes to Superior Court next month. Offshore wind keeps losing time in state venues nobody modeled.
  • NDSix Counties, Two Rooms: the North Dakota PSC set two September public hearings on the North Plains Connector, covering a 240-plus-mile stretch through Golden Valley, Slope, Hettinger, Oliver, Morton and Grant counties. The full line runs about 422 miles from near Colstrip, Montana, to endpoints near Center and St. Anthony, at $6 billion total with $3.2 billion in North Dakota; Grid United and Allete are joint developers, with Montana-Dakota Utilities and Minnkota Power also taking capacity. Hearings are 8:30 a.m. Sept. 2 at Mandan City Hall and 9:30 a.m. MT Sept. 3 in Regent, with a technical hearing Sept. 1 at the Capitol. The procedural trap worth flagging: comments only enter the PSC record if delivered in person. Written submissions and email do not count, which quietly hands the record to whoever can drive there on a Wednesday morning.
  • ILFirst Battery in St. Clair, Unanimous: RWE's 200-MW lithium-ion BESS on roughly 20 acres near an Ameren substation in Fayetteville Township cleared the county zoning board on a unanimous recommendation after a long hearing that was almost entirely about fire; the full County Board votes Aug. 30. RWE lead developer Nicholas Brown used portfolio experience as the trust argument: "I know that this is St. Clair County's first battery, but this is not RWE's. We've operated them, built them. We know how to do it safely." Engineer Pedro Duarte walked through automatic hydrogen and carbon monoxide venting, 24/7 third-party monitoring with alarms routed to both RWE and local responders, firefighter training, and a site-specific emergency plan requiring local fire sign-off. Kimley-Horn's Liam Sawyer put decommissioning at an estimated $14 million within 18 months, re-estimated every five years. Board member Ed Cockrell: "I fully support the project. I think the safeguards are in place." Note what won it — a named dollar figure on decommissioning and a fire plan the chief signs.
  • TXNo Zoning, So Buy a Negotiator: Colorado County commissioners approved an agreement retaining Capex Resources Group to write tax-abatement guidelines and negotiate agreements with the developers of Paloma BESS and Firefly Field Energy's solar farm, both already under construction and due to finish in December. County Judge Ty Prause named the structural reality Texas counties live with: without zoning, abatement agreements are one of the only levers to negotiate project conditions — "the business part of it is simply how do we protect our citizens as far as their tax rate and tax structure." Fees are capped near $30,000 and paid by the developers, not taxpayers. The part to watch: the county cancelled its scheduled Aug. 24 public hearings on all three projects and Prause says there will be no further hearings before the abatement vote, citing "good dialogue" already had. Colorado County denied a BESS abatement in June 2025 over first-responder communications, and its attorneys are now reviewing neighboring Austin County's moratorium.
  • NJFive Thousand Down, Twenty-Five Interest-Free: PSE&G launched the GridSmart Battery Program, a residential virtual-power-plant pilot pairing a $5,000 incentive toward a qualifying home battery with up to $25,000 in interest-free, on-bill repayment for the balance. Enrolled batteries provide outage backup and may be dispatched during noticed "GridSmart Battery Events" to cut grid draw at peak. The utility's own framing is the interesting part — the program is pitched as "potentially deferring or avoiding transmission or distribution upgrades," which is a utility conceding in writing that customer-sited storage substitutes for wires. It follows the BPU's July launch of a statewide VPP framework process. In a state where 38 municipalities have now banned data centers, the pitch that a battery in your basement defers a substation upgrade is the most portable pro-storage message available.
  • GAFifty Million for the Back End: Princeton NuEnergy was selected for a $50 million DOE grant toward a $110 million closed-loop battery-material recycling plant in Commerce, Georgia, with a $60 million company cost share. The facility will process 3,000 tonnes a year of nickel-based lithium-ion manufacturing scrap using low-temperature plasma-assisted separation, returning rejuvenated cathode material to manufacturers in about seven days at roughly 45% below virgin cost, with 70 permanent and about 100 construction jobs. CEO Chao Yan and chief commercial officer Joe Fisher framed it as supply-chain security. Keep the number: the most common end-of-life objection in a BESS hearing is that nothing gets recycled, and a domestic 3,000-tonne closed-loop plant with a federal cost share is a concrete counter.
  • NCDurham Draws the Triangle Line: county commissioners voted 4-1 for a nine-month data-center moratorium running to May 2027 to align with the city's existing pause, exempting facilities under 100,000 square feet that use closed-loop cooling and no diesel backup. Commissioner Nida Allam cast the lone no vote against the carve-out: "A delay for a company of nine months is monetary. But the impact on folks' health and wellbeing, and financial stability, is going to last a lifetime." Commissioner Larissa Williamson named the real driver, which is enforcement, not siting: "I don't know how we would enforce that, particularly since we have no enforcement powers over the electrical usage." Orange, Chatham and Harnett counties plus Apex and Wendell all have pauses; Lee County called a moratorium a "legal quagmire" and rewrote its rules instead. Both approaches are now live in one metro, which makes the Triangle the cleanest natural experiment in the country on pause-versus-ordinance.
  • NJThirty-Eight and Counting: Jackson Township council voted 4-1 to ban data centers outright, the 38th New Jersey municipality to do so and the second in Ocean County that week after Stafford, with Manchester introducing a zone-withdrawal ordinance a day earlier. The procedure is instructive: introduction passed only 3-2, then the planning board found the ordinance inconsistent with the master plan, which raised the adoption threshold to four votes — and Council President Mordechai Burnstein flipped to yes, carrying it anyway. Sponsor Chris Pollak argued the ban does real work beyond the master plan: "By specifically prohibiting data centers this ordinance would make it more difficult for an applicant to gain approval. It puts a higher hurdle in front of anyone who wants to build a data center in Jackson and that's the idea." The scaling mechanism is the story — Climate Revolution Action Network New Jersey is training residents statewide to run these bans, and executive director Ben Dziobek says "every week we're seeing more and more towns stand up." Nobody has proposed a data center in Jackson.
  • NYOrangetown Pauses Everything but the One: the town board adopted a six-month moratorium on new data center and battery storage applications on Aug. 25 — and carved out DataBank's pending second building, already before the planning board with a full environmental impact study underway. Residents were not satisfied. Matt Heinlen, whose New Jersey property abuts the site: "I'm not against data centers, but a site immediately adjacent to a drinking water reservoir, so close to the residents, directly on top of class one and class two wetlands, and dependent on significant power from the existing grid, just simply may not be the right place." The draft EIS puts wetland removal at 0.1 acres and forest at 2 acres, with the second building raising campus power demand 50%. Supervisor Teresa Kenny defended the scope: "This moratorium is not just about DataBank. The infrastructure for these facilities is there already. That's why they're all coming here." The lesson for anyone with an application in flight: being grandfathered protects the permit and costs you the room.
  • INElkhart Buys Sixteen Months: the city council passed a moratorium on data centers and battery storage at a special hearing Thursday night, suspending new applications through Dec. 31, 2027. First District councilman Aaron Mishler framed it as escalation rather than prohibition: "A moratorium is the next level to protect our community while we continue to investigate data centers to determine if they have a fit ever in our community." The pause applies to the city only, not Elkhart County — which means the county line is now the siting map. Note the coupling, again: battery storage was swept in alongside data centers without a separate record on storage. That bundling is the fastest-spreading structural risk to BESS siting in the Midwest, and it happens in rooms where nobody showed up to argue the two are different.
Plays of the Week
  • Coweta County (GA) OppositionWin the Filing Cabinet: Project Oak was, on paper, an unloseable application — the site had been zoned industrial since April 2024, the developer asked for no variances, conformed to the county's data-center ordinance, cut the footprint to 255 acres, and offered to fund extensions of Coweta Industrial Blvd. and Amlajack Blvd. It was denied unanimously with no commissioner discussion whatsoever. Opponents ran two moves. First, the paper trail: zoning coordinator Nicole Blackwell reported 121 letters of opposition from 112 individuals and not one letter in support — a record so lopsided that a no vote required no justification. Second, the room: rather than repetitive three-minute comments, they divided a coordinated presentation among speakers and deliberately saved two students for last, a high school sophomore and a seventh grader arguing that their generation inherits the decision. The applicant must wait six months to refile. The transferable lesson is brutal and simple — an empty support column loses a technically perfect application, and the support column is the one thing a developer can actually control.
  • Save Lubbock (TX)Skip the Persuasion, Use the Charter: Citizens of Lubbock Against Data Centers never had a council majority, so they stopped asking for one. They gathered signatures under the city charter's direct-initiative provision, got them certified by the city secretary, and forced council to formally accept an ordinance imposing an 18-month moratorium on high-water, high-energy data centers. Acceptance on Aug. 25 was a legal formality — which is exactly the value: it converts a discretionary policy ask into a mandatory calendar item with a public hearing Sept. 8 and a 30-day decision clock. The play wins even in defeat. If council rejects, petitioners can call an election, and because the November deadline has passed it lands next May, giving incumbents a full campaign season of exposure. It is already moving the council: Councilman Tim Collins argued the 18-month term violates state law but volunteered to draft a compliant version — "For the most part I'm on your side. We will be coming back with that" — and Mayor Mark McBrayer conceded, "We will be voting on ordinances that will restrict them." Check whether the charters in our host communities carry an initiative provision, because the other side is checking.
  • Upper OV United (Wheeling, WV)If They Won't Hold the Hearing, Hold It Yourself: refused a public hearing by Wheeling City Council on Silicon Foundation's GPU data center at the former Centre Foundry site, opponents rented Waterfront Hall and staged "A Very Public Data Center Hearing" — with the promotional copy doing double duty as an attendance test: "The city won't give us a public hearing, so we'll do it ourselves. We'll see who among our elected officials makes it to the event." About 100 people came. Organizers ran a sign-in sheet, handed out "No Data Centers In Our City" yard signs and shirts at the door, crowdsourced questions online in advance, and — the sophisticated part — invited both sides onto the panel, including a councilman, the vice mayor and the developer's own counsel. Inviting the opposition removed the "one-sided rally" critique and put the company's lawyer on unfamiliar ground; he complained that "it's unfortunate that the environment is as hostile as it is," which is the sound of a format working. The emcee is simultaneously a plaintiff in county circuit court, so the evening also generated earned media for the litigation. Counter-move for developers: never let the other side own the only venue where both sides speak. If the jurisdiction won't hold a hearing, we should offer to host one.

A fully conforming project died on 121 letters to zero. A township that approved a project in 2024 denied the bigger version of it in 2026. A landowner who wasn't offered the confidential deal his neighbors got became the plaintiff. A county paused batteries it has never once discussed. None of that is a siting problem or a technology problem. It is a record problem, and the record gets built months before anyone votes — in mailboxes, at kitchen tables, and in the letters people bother to write. Fill the file. Nobody else is going to fill it for us.
