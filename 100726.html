RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 58 · Wednesday, October 7, 2026

Midweek morning, team.

Kenosha County, Wisconsin passed a 12-month battery moratorium 22-0 in May. On Sept. 30, Robin Energy Storage filed its 200-megawatt Wheatland project with the state Public Service Commission, after withdrawing its local applications. The same move is playing out in other states. In Michigan, RWE refiled Silver Maple Solar with state regulators while Zeeland Township sues to have the enabling law thrown out. Howard County, Maryland voted 4-1 against a solar pause and formed a task force instead. And Texas regulators sent a 765-kV line back to the judges. In each case the question is which room decides.

Deep Dive

Over the County's Head

On Sept. 30, Robin Energy Storage LLC filed its application with the Wisconsin Public Service Commission to build a 200-megawatt battery energy storage system in the Town of Wheatland, Kenosha County. It is roughly 200 battery containers on about 60 acres north of Highway 50 near New Munster. Robin had already withdrawn its local applications, and the Wheatland Plan Commission had unanimously recommended denial before it did.

The county had moved first. On May 19, Kenosha County voted 22-0 for a one-year moratorium on battery storage approvals. The Wheatland Town Board then adopted a resolution asking the state for restrictions on battery storage. Robin did not wait out the pause. Its engineering plan went to the PSC on June 18, and the full application followed on Sept. 30, which opens the state's formal review. If approved, construction could begin as early as late 2027. The case is under docket 9842-CE-100.

County Executive Samantha Kerkman, a Republican, said she was "disappointed that Robin Energy has decided to sidestep our countywide moratorium," and called the filing a "direct circumvention of local control." She is lobbying the Legislature for state battery rules, which Wisconsin does not have. County Board Chairman Mark Nordigian and Supervisor Erin Decker cited fire safety, emergency response, environmental impacts and farmland. Asked whether the moratorium has any standing, the PSC said any conflict between its authority and a local ordinance "would be up for the courts to resolve."

Opponents are already working on the next room. Organizer Jenny Morehouse told West of the I that the group needs to prepare for the PSC comment periods. No hearing dates have been announced. Kathy Denko, who lives within the evacuation zone, told TMJ4 it is "very disappointing to know they had no respect for our one-year moratorium."

Michigan shows how the next stage can go. After RWE withdrew and refiled Silver Maple Solar with the Michigan Public Service Commission, Ottawa County was added as an affected local government, and Zeeland Township became entitled to $50,000 in intervenor funds. Zeeland also sued, arguing Public Act 233 is unconstitutional. And a month ago we reported that a Virginia judge overrode a 5-1 denial and a wind developer walked past a county ban to the state siting council. Wisconsin is the same pattern with a battery.

The Takeaway

A county moratorium stops local approvals, but if a state agency has jurisdiction it does not stop the project. Opposition then moves to the commission docket, and that is where it learns to organize: intervenor status, comment periods, and a 22-0 vote and a unanimous plan commission recommendation entered as evidence. For each client project, we should know which state body could take the application if the county says no, what its comment and hearing process looks like, and whether the host community has an answer ready before the filing happens. The county executive is now lobbying for state battery rules that did not exist when Robin filed. When a state pathway is open, the community-engagement work has to start before the county vote, not after it.

Also On Our Radar
  • Zeeland Fights the Statute: RWE Americas' $300 million, 200-megawatt Silver Maple Solar would cover about 1,900 acres across Zeeland and Jamestown townships in Ottawa County, Michigan. The case is now in two lawsuits. Zeeland Township argues Public Act 233 is unconstitutional, RWE moved that case to federal court, and the township wants it sent back. Separately, RWE sued the Smallegan family for allegedly blocking access to land that is about 30% of the site, and a bench trial is set for Oct. 8. Township and county officials also wrote to the Trump administration asking for support, and Rep. Bill Huizenga replied on Facebook to "keep up the fight."
  • Four to One for a Task Force: Howard County, Maryland Councilman David Yungmann's Bill 59-2026 would have halted approvals of solar facilities over one megawatt for a year and required a public meeting within 30 days and a report in 120. It followed the state's Renewable Energy Certainty Act, which moved projects of two megawatts and up to the Public Service Commission. The council voted 4-1 against the pause and created a solar task force. Solar United Neighbors says its supporters testified and called members to beat the bill. The bill's origin was a Woodbine resident who said she learned of a project near her home from a posted sign.
  • Back to the Judges: On Sept. 9, Texas PUC Chairman Thomas Gleeson recommended remanding the Bell County East to Big Hill 765-kilovolt line to the administrative law judges, saying they had not answered whether the facilities meet reliability standards or what congestion-cost savings customers would see. The judges had recommended on Aug. 20 that the PUC deny both Bell County and Sand Lake because ERCOT's Permian Basin Reliability Plan does not support the need for Import Path 2. Texas Scorecard reports the commission remanded the segment, so no final decision or oral argument comes until the judges respond.
  • Paused From the Bench: On April 23, North Carolina Utilities Commissioner Brawley issued an order pausing Duke Energy's 2026 solar and battery storage RFP, which the commission's 2024 Carbon Plan required. SACE, Sierra Club, Vote Solar and EJCAN sued on June 18, and the question is whether the order was procedural or substantive and needed notice and a hearing. The case was argued Aug. 3 and its schedule runs to Oct. 5 while the parties wait on the commission. Duke's cluster study has to start in December to meet its regulatory obligations.
  • Forty-Five Months to Connect: A Berkeley Lab analysis of 2.7 million interconnection applications at 188 utilities in 24 states found median waits of 2 months for systems under 30 kW and 45 months for 1-5 megawatt projects (2020-2025). Of projects above 100 kW, 76% are still without permission to operate, against 8% of projects under 100 kW.
  • Hill Country Pushback: In Gillespie County, Texas, residents and county officials are united against Marshall Springs, a roughly 1,000-acre solar and battery storage project in wine country. County officials unanimously oppose it, though the state gives counties little regulatory power over projects like battery storage.
On the Frontlines
  • NYIslip Closes the Door: The Islip Town Board voted 5-0 to ban new battery storage facilities above 600 kilowatts, which blocks a proposed Rabro Drive project in Hauppauge. Fire Commissioner Scott Munro warned that "there's nothing much that the fire department can do," while a Key Capture Energy developer said New York's updated 2025 fire code has addressed the safety concerns.
  • ILFour to One in Danville: The Danville Area Planning and Zoning Commission recommended 4-1 that Pivot Energy's community solar farm off West Newell Road be approved. It would serve Ameren customers with bill discounts. The City Council takes final action Oct. 20.
  • ILFour Creeks, No Vote Yet: The Peoria County Zoning Board of Appeals is hearing Repsol's Four Creeks Wind, up to 113 turbines as tall as 657 feet across about 27,000 acres. Testimony on Sept. 30 included a projection of $307 million in local tax revenue over 40 years, and the board took no vote that day. Its recommendation goes to the County Board's Land Use Committee and then the full County Board.
  • IAHalf a Mile to 500 Feet: Page County supervisors voted 2-1 last month for an agreement with MidAmerican Energy on the Nodaway Valley Solar Project near Clarinda that keeps regulatory control with the county rather than the Iowa Utilities Commission. The ordinance changes that come with it would cut the setback from a half-mile to 300-500 feet. Hearings are Oct. 27, Nov. 5 and Nov. 10, and MidAmerican extended the deadline to Nov. 10.
  • OKOffice Closed in Lincoln County: Apex Clean Energy's 35,000-acre Sandstone Hills wind project is on hold, and the developer closed its local office, according to Heatmap's reporting. A decision on whether to abandon it is expected by the first quarter of 2027.
  • INZionsville Pauses Everything: The Zionsville Town Council on Sept. 21 approved a moratorium covering data centers, solar farms, wind farms and battery storage for uses that lack a permitted category in the zoning code, and directed staff to update the code.
  • CTPermits by Default: Connecticut's HB 5340, signed May 20, took effect Oct. 1 and creates a streamlined online automatic permitting process for residential solar and battery systems.
  • INFort Wayne Waits: The city council's vote on a one-year pause on data center actions and a nine-member study commission was pushed from Sept. 23 to Oct. 13 while amendments are worked out. The existing Google data center is not affected.

Kenosha County voted 22-0 for a pause and the developer went to the state anyway. For each client project this week, name the state body that could take the file if the county votes no, and find out what that body's comment process asks of the community.
