Good morning, team.

The boards came back off summer recess and handed us a thesis on the first swing: delay is the denial now. In Ohio, state regulators told the Supreme Court not to bother ruling on a 175-MW solar permit fight — because the four years the fight consumed outlasted the project's interconnection agreement, and a project without a queue slot is, in their telling, no longer a live controversy. Nobody had to win that argument on the merits. The calendar won it. That's our Must Read, and it is the sharpest illustration yet of something we have been saying to clients all year: schedule risk and opposition risk are the same risk, priced differently.

New York moved in the opposite direction and moved fast. Gov. Hochul signed Executive Order 62 on July 14, pausing state environmental permits for hyperscale data centers — anything at 50 MW or above — for up to a year. That resolves a standing item we have carried for weeks, and it is a genuinely big deal, but the part worth your attention isn't the freeze. It's the Community Investment Framework the order directs Empire State Development to publish within 60 days: a state-authored template telling localities how to negotiate community benefits from large-load development. Our job description, about to be issued as guidance. Read that section of Radar closely.

Standing items, so we don't re-litigate them. Loudoun's Golden-to-Mars monopoles: the school board's deadline to volunteer an alternate corridor is today — nothing filed as of this writing. Texas's $33B STEP 765-kV pause: no update. North Carolina's NCUC solar freeze: SELC's reconsideration petition still pending. Valley North's 765-kV open houses continue — Harrison and Preston tomorrow, Gilmer Wednesday — with no route chosen. The WV PSC's Mon Power / Potomac Edison docket at Maidsville closed its evidentiary hearings Friday with all 32 public commenters opposed; the $182M of solar riding along in that filing (arrays in Tucker, Preston and Hancock counties) now waits on a gas-plant ruling expected within the year. East Point's Mill Stone battery goes to the Chesapeake city council tomorrow, July 21 — the over-built-setback play we ran Friday gets its verdict. Starlight Solar heads to San Diego's Board of Supervisors September 16; Comstock Township's storage information session is Thursday. New York's data-center moratorium is no longer standing — it's in Radar as a full item.

Must Read

Denial by Calendar

The Ohio Power Siting Board denied Vesper Energy's 175-MW Kingwood Solar project in December 2022. Vesper appealed to the Ohio Supreme Court. Nearly four years later, the court still hasn't ruled — and on July 1 the OPSB filed a motion asking it to dismiss the case as moot, on the grounds that Kingwood's PJM interconnection agreement has since expired. No queue slot, no project. No project, no controversy. Nothing to decide.

Sit with the mechanics for a second, because they are worse than they first look. The OPSB's original denial rested on a single finding: every local government with standing — Greene County and all three host townships — had passed resolutions opposing the project, and the board held that unanimous local opposition was "controlling" on the statutory public-interest test. Every other permit criterion was met. The project was, by the state's own accounting, technically and environmentally sound. It lost on sentiment, formally expressed. Vesper appealed precisely that holding — the question of how much weight a siting board may give a township resolution — and it is exactly the question the industry needs answered. Now the agency that made the finding is arguing the court should never reach it, because the delay in reaching it destroyed the thing under review.

The people on both sides of this can see what happened. "Getting a resolution and clarity from the court on this issue remains a matter of great public interest," said Karin Nordstrom of the Ohio Environmental Council. Doug Herling, a VP at Open Road Renewables, put the same point less diplomatically, aimed at the court: "Why did you run out the clock?" And the pattern is not a one-off — a parallel appeal over NextEra's Circleville solar farm, filed in March 2025, is also still sitting undecided. Ohio has now built a permitting environment in which the doctrine that decides your project is unreviewable in practice, because review takes longer than your interconnection agreement lives.

Vesper's response to the mootness motion was due July 13. Whatever it argued, the strategic reality for anyone developing in Ohio is already fixed: the township resolution is the permit. A county board that passes a one-page resolution of opposition — no zoning authority required, no findings required, no hearing record required — has functionally exercised a veto that the state will treat as controlling and that no court will get around to reviewing. That is a remarkably cheap weapon to hand the other side, and it is currently sitting on the table in thirty-seven Ohio counties that have already drawn solar exclusion zones in at least one township.

The Takeaway

Two things follow, and both are about time. First: your interconnection agreement is a perishable asset, and opposition knows it. Every month of appeal, every continuance, every "let's table this until the fall" is not a neutral procedural event — it is a partial win for the other side, and increasingly it is the whole win. Build the schedule risk into how you brief a client on a contested site, and stop describing litigation as the backstop. In Ohio it is the trapdoor. Second: fight the resolution, not the permit. Kingwood was decided the day three townships and a county passed pieces of paper that cost them nothing. Those resolutions are drafted in public, weeks in advance, usually by two or three people, almost always without anyone from the project in the room. That meeting — the boring one, before the hearing, before the record exists — is the entire ballgame. We should be selling that meeting.

Also On Our Radar
  • Empire State of Pause: New York became the first state to freeze an entire class of development statewide. Gov. Hochul signed Executive Order 62 on July 14, directing DEC to withhold discretionary permits for hyperscale data centers — 50 MW and up — for up to a year while DPS develops a Generic Environmental Impact Statement covering energy demand, water use and air quality. "These hyperscale AI data centers consume enormous amounts of power, truly threatening to outpace our grid's capacity," Hochul said. "I refuse to let those costs get passed down to New Yorkers." But skip past the freeze, because three things buried further down the order matter more to our clients than the pause does. One: Empire State Development must publish a Community Investment Framework within 60 days — formal state guidance on how localities should negotiate benefits from large-load projects, including infrastructure, child care, direct payments, prevailing wage, PLAs, local hiring, and "a formula to help communities assess where to begin investment negotiations." A published starting number changes every host-community conversation in the state, ours included, and the public comment window on it is open now. Two: DPS is directed to consider requiring data centers to fund new dedicated clean generation — explicitly including distributed energy resources and battery storage. Three: a proposed Grid Acceleration Fund would make large loads pay into grid upgrades and into an insurance pool against speculative interconnection requests. Read together, that is a state deciding that load pays for the renewables and the wires it consumes. If that framework holds, it is the most consequential thing to happen to storage economics in New York this year, and it arrived inside a document everyone is reading as a data-center story.
  • The Cap Fits: PJM's 2028-29 capacity auction cleared at the $325/MW-day cap across the entire footprint, and the number under the number is the alarming one: the auction fell roughly 6.8 GW short of PJM's reliability target, up from 6.5 GW last time, while drawing only about 525 MW of new resources — 208 MW of which was just uprates on existing plant. Without the price collar it would have cleared near $555/MW-day footprint-wide and $777 in ComEd, at $29.7B instead of $16.4B. COO Stu Bresler attributed roughly 2 GW of the demand increase to data centers. FERC Chairman Laura Swett was blunt: the results "compound the alarm bells for a call to action in PJM," and, at a briefing, "Am I surprised that PJM failed to deliver? No, I am not." She has set a technical conference for July 23 on PJM governance (Docket AD26-7-000), calling the stakeholder process "slow where it must be fast, opaque where it must be transparent, and vulnerable to vetoes and agenda control." Aurora's Julia Hoos warned of "an intervention doom loop." The read for us: scarcity this severe is the strongest argument our clients have ever had in a hearing room, and almost nobody is using it. A township weighing a solar moratorium is making a capacity decision whether it knows it or not, and $325/MW-day is what that decision costs. Put the number in the record.
  • Cheap, But Less So: Lazard released its 2026 Levelized Cost of Energy report on July 13, and the headline holds — utility-scale solar at $40–$98/MWh and onshore wind at $37–$99 still beat combined-cycle gas at $51–$129, peakers at $144–$276, and nuclear at $175–$255. With the PTC, solar's low end drops to $16/MWh. But the trend line turned: costs rose across the board on "higher capital costs, sustained interest rates, tariff pass-through and supply chain repricing," and the levelized cost of standalone utility-scale storage increased, reversing last year's declines, as lithium-ion import tariffs cut off access to cheap cells. Offshore wind sits at $105–$167. Keep the top-line comparison in the talking points, because it is still the single most persuasive slide in any host-community deck — but stop quoting last year's storage numbers, and expect the "it's getting cheaper every year" line to draw a challenge from a prepared opponent for the first time in a decade.
  • Steel River Runs Deep: Google and Cypress Creek Energy broke ground July 14 on Steel River Energy Center in Mississippi County, Arkansas — 2.5 GW and 2.9 GWh of solar-plus-storage, expected to be the largest solar facility in the country, with Google as anchor investor and offtaker on the first two phases and full operation targeted for 2029. Out of geography for us, but the community-engagement construction is worth stealing wholesale. The benefits were announced at groundbreaking, itemized and specific: $5 million from Google for local energy affordability, $3 million more from Cypress Creek, the first tranche being $400,000 for a school-district playground, plus 700 construction jobs per phase and roughly $300 million in local tax revenue over the project's life. And the supply-chain framing was aimed squarely at the room — U.S.-made panels, nearly all structural steel from Mississippi County itself. "Some people still question whether a domestic solar supply chain is possible," CEO Kevin Smith said. "This project is proof." Compare that to Starlight, where the benefit package is still being negotiated in public two months after the vote. Same instrument, opposite sequencing: define the number first and it reads as investment; let the room define it and it reads as a settlement.
Project Updates

Editor's note: the boards are back. After four thin recess weeks, this section returns to full strength — nine verified in-window proceedings below, all traced to a dated page inside our seven-day window. The through-line: the moratorium wave has stopped distinguishing between a battery and a server hall, and several of these ordinances are still in draft, which means still negotiable.

  • NYSchroeppel Stacks the Pauses: the Oswego County town adopted a 12-month data-center moratorium on July 14 with no application pending — the same day the governor did it statewide. That town is now running three freezes at once: commercial solar since February, data centers as of last week, and a proposed 12-month BESS pause on the August 11 agenda. Also on that agenda: a Nexamp waiver request for site approval on Bankrupt Road, with the company's Phoenix-Oxbow solar project already carrying a negative environmental declaration from December. A developer with an approved SEQRA finding, walking into a room that has now paused every category it could think of. August 11 is a date to have someone in.
  • NYHudson Falls Skips the Moratorium Entirely: the village isn't pausing storage, it's proposing to ban it outright. Local Law No. 5 of 2026 would prohibit the siting, construction, installation, expansion, operation and maintenance of battery energy storage systems anywhere in Hudson Falls, permanently. The findings language is the tell, and it is escalated well past the usual: thermal runaway producing "intense fires, explosions, toxic gases, and prolonged combustion lasting for days or weeks," releases of hydrogen fluoride and heavy metals, and a flat declaration that local emergency response "may be insufficient." Public hearing August 10 at 4 p.m., Village Hall. Nearby Ticonderoga passed a six-month lithium moratorium two months ago. Note the drafting escalation across those two towns — a six-month pause upstream becomes a permanent ban downstream in about eight weeks, because the second village copies the first village's findings and then hardens them.
  • MIKalamazoo Township Doubles Up: trustees gave first readings on July 13 to Ordinance 645 and Ordinance 647 — one-year moratoriums on battery storage and on data centers respectively — both on voice votes with no substantive discussion. The stated rationale for the BESS freeze is the part to underline: "public sentiment in other Michigan municipalities." Not a local application, not a local incident, not a local study. Sentiment somewhere else. Supervisor David Combs noted the township doesn't currently permit data centers at all. The formal adoption vote is July 27 — one week out, and the last point at which the storage ordinance and the server-hall ordinance can still be pried apart from each other.
  • MIOshtemo Plays Both Boards: the township is drafting BESS rules and funding a lawsuit against the state's power to override them at the same time. Its draft ordinance is with the fire department for review, after which a contracted industry expert will brief the planning commission on safety — a genuinely constructive process. In the same July 14 meeting the board readopted its resolution backing the municipal coalition's PA 233 appeal, now pending before the Michigan Supreme Court after a split Court of Appeals ruling in May. Residents used public comment against a specific proposed site, including a physician testifying on local hydrogeology. Worth watching precisely because it is the sophisticated version: a township building a real rulebook while litigating to keep the authority to enforce it.
  • ILTazewell Writes the Rules and Rejects the Project: the county Land Use Committee voted 6-2 to recommend denial of a 5-MW solar farm from Schlappi Solar 1 near Mackinaw, while separately advancing the county's first-ever BESS standards. Chairman Russ Crawford was careful to frame the denial as siting, not sector — proximity to a residential area and two small businesses, and property-value concerns that a seller "would have to say 'Yes, there is a large solar farm right there.'" The new storage standards carry expanded insurance, financial assurance and decommissioning requirements, which Crawford tied to storm debris: "if a tornado comes… those winds will take that debris over a wide geographic area." Full county board votes the last Wednesday of July; a data-center ordinance follows in August or September. A 5-MW project is small enough that most developers wouldn't staff the hearing. This is what that costs.
  • NCWarren County Reaches for the Pause: the planning board voted to recommend a one-year data-center moratorium to county commissioners, who will likely take it up at their August 19 work session. The county has no regulations addressing data centers at all; Assistant County Manager Matt Garner said that absent a moratorium, staff would have to apply whatever "closest existing category" the zoning ordinance offers to any application that arrives — and framed the pause as "a pause, not a stop sign." Same board rejected Tyr Energy / TED Renewables' solar text amendments in May. A jurisdiction with no rulebook and a recent habit of saying no to text amendments is a jurisdiction where the ordinance drafting is the project.
  • PAPenn Township Borrows the Language: the Butler County supervisors voted July 14 to advertise a public hearing on a draft data-center ordinance, expected in August. No developer has proposed anything there. What's instructive is the sourcing: residents showed up carrying paper copies of other Pennsylvania townships' ordinances as drafting references. That is how a clause travels. Resident Barbara Chapman also named the demand we keep seeing attached to these: "I understand that you can't just say 'no,' but you could make ordinances and zoning to the point where (data center developers) are not going to even try to be here. And I would really appreciate transparency … like no nondisclosure agreements between the township and whatever companies, because that would be a deal-breaker." The NDA objection is spreading faster than any technical argument, and it lands on renewable developers exactly as hard.
  • TXAustin County Listens, Says Nothing: residents filled the Expo Center for a two-hour hearing July 13 on a proposed temporary moratorium covering both battery storage and data centers. Commissioners took no action — County Judge Tim Lapham said the court was listening only, with formal action expected at a later meeting. Two disclosures make this one worth tracking: Lapham put roughly $30,000 in legal fees in June alone on the record, and said he expects a legal challenge if the moratorium passes. Residents near the existing BESS on Substation Road described a continuous low-frequency hum, alongside the usual groundwater, light and notification complaints. Industry reps countered that Texas has had no battery storage fire. The hum complaint is the one to take seriously — it is specific, verifiable, and the only objection in that list that a developer can actually fix.
  • TXAngelina County Asks Austin for a Zoning Code: commissioners voted unanimously July 14 to adopt Resolution 010-26, formally asking the Texas Legislature for limited siting authority over data centers and battery storage — buffer zones, setbacks, noise attenuation, light pollution standards, certified water impact statements, environmental safety plans, and mandatory public hearings above a size threshold. County Judge Keith Wright was candid about the motive: "If we can't get an abatement, if there's not going to be an abatement, then there are zero control that we have over these data centers." He has pitched the incoming state senator and state rep, and concedes he doesn't expect to get the full list. Session opens January 12, 2027. Texas counties asking to be given zoning power over storage is a genuinely new development in a state whose entire value proposition to our clients has been that they don't have it.
On the Frontlines
  • VASuffolk Says No to Three Megawatts: the city council voted 8-0 on July 15 to deny New Leaf Energy's conditional use permit for a 3-MW solar farm on 25-plus acres of previously logged agricultural land, following a 7-0 denial recommendation from the planning commission. The project would have fed Dominion's Shared Solar Program. New Leaf did the homework — inverters centered on the parcel, a noise study showing 15 decibels under the limit, mature-tree buffers, added screening pledged — and it did not matter. Dozens of neighbors signed a petition; adjoining owner Jimmy Smith argued Old Myrtle Road couldn't carry construction traffic: "The sides of the roads are collapsing." Councilmember Timothy Johnson gave the denial its epitaph, citing proximity to a church built in 1779: "The project is not a bad project, but the project is out of place." Councilmember Ebony Wright noted "there was not a single comment in favor of this project." Zero. On a 25-acre shared-solar project with a clean technical record. Somebody should have been building a supportive record for six months, and nobody was.
  • VAHalifax Heads to Appeal: a resident is filing an appeal of the Board of Supervisors' approval of Boxwood Solar — 69 MW on 954 acres — after the board overruled its own planning commission's denial recommendation in June. Jeremy Barksdale's complaint is procedural as much as substantive: a petition with more than 100 signatures, he says, never came up during the vote. "I feel like a hundred voices speaks louder than a couple emails sent to the board." The board's case was revenue, made explicitly — CEP Solar's Rob Propes said the project generates "38 times more revenue than its current use," and Supervisor Dennis Witt tied it to unmet public-safety needs and the alternative of a tax increase. Volunteer Fire Chief Terry Hudson supplied the counter-line that will follow this project through appeal: "It seems as though money blinds everybody." Note the mirror image with Suffolk above — same state, same week, same sector, opposite outcome, and the difference was a board willing to overrule its commission on fiscal grounds. That is the Virginia split to brief clients on right now.
  • NJBeach Haven Powers Up Anyway: Atlantic City Electric fully activated its lithium-ion storage system on July 1, on a former substation site on Long Beach Island, over a request from Beach Haven's own leaders to delay the launch — on a project approved more than four years ago. Resident Jim Ciulla made the barrier-island argument that is very hard to answer: "We're on a barrier island — one way in, one way out." But the utility has the fact that matters most in a fight like this: LBI electricity demand hit a ten-year high over the Fourth of July weekend, and the battery helped keep the power on. Region President Andrew Hendry framed it as peak-demand support. This is the rarest item in the brief — a contested battery that went live and immediately produced its own proof of value in the first month. Get the load data from that weekend. It is the best available answer to "we don't need this here," and it has a shelf life.
  • INCrosstrack Clears, Coalition Counts the Rest: the Indiana Utility Regulatory Commission approved Invenergy's 130-MW Crosstrack Energy Storage project in Pike County — one of three AES Indiana builds tied to Google's Monrovia data center. Citizens Action Coalition's Ben Inskeep immediately reframed the win as an opening move, noting the approved infrastructure serves only the 390-MW first phase while AES filings point to an eventual 1,200 MW: "That's creating a real crisis on the grid, where they're driving prices much higher at the wholesale level." He also pre-loaded the transmission fight, since AES hasn't finalized routes: "Eminent domain is supposed to be for projects that benefit the public interest. They're not supposed to be for projects that are benefiting the private interest of a $1 trillion company at the expense of the general public." That framing is durable and it will be waiting for the route announcement. A 130-MW storage approval bought AES roughly zero goodwill, because the opposition is arguing the program, not the project.
  • MIWexford Weighs Whether to Keep the Decision: Ranger Power wants roughly 1,500 acres of Wexford Township farmland for solar, and the joint planning commission is weighing a zoning amendment while the state-permitting alternative sits in the background — ten projects are now pending before the MPSC under PA 233. The most useful quote in the piece is from a resident, not an official: "I by no means want this project to go through, but if it does with the state, it's something that I can live with," said Jake Kerby. That is the entire PA 233 dynamic in one sentence — local opposition that would rather lose to Lansing than approve locally. Nearby townships are splitting on the response: Green Lake is drafting a compatible ordinance to keep decisions local, while East Bay declined, with planning director Claire Karner saying "it would probably make the most sense to defer to the state." Ranger's Drew Vielbig says the company prefers local permitting, which is the right public position and also the harder road.
  • TXCedar Creek Signs Up: more than 2,500 people have signed a "Save Cedar Creek Lake" petition against a Diode Ventures data center on 759 acres near Trinidad, with organizers planning to carry it to a July 27 interim hearing in Austin. The organizing hook is water, and it's concrete in a way that fire risk never quite is: the reservoir is already three feet down, residents fear up to 5 million gallons a day during a Stage 3 drought, and Stacy Farmer's line — "A lot of people on the Cedar Creek Reservoir cannot get their boats out" — does more work than any consumption statistic. The second hook is process: a public information request showed talks ran more than a year before homeowners heard about the project. Two thousand five hundred signatures in a county with no zoning authority, assembled around a visible, daily, physical grievance. File this next to Eastland: the Texas opposition playbook is now petition first, resolution second, legislature third.

Delay is a decision. Someone is always making it.
