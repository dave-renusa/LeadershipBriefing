Happy Monday, all.

The cliff came and went on Saturday. Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, a wind or solar project had to commence construction by July 4 to keep the federal tax credits our clients’ economics ride on — and by every industry account the sprint largely worked: hundreds of gigawatts of solar and wind were safe-harbored before the holiday. But the deadline didn’t end the pressure; it moved it. A safe-harbored project now has to plug into the grid within four calendar years or forfeit the credit anyway, which means the whole industry just traded a finance deadline for a build deadline — and the build deadline runs straight through the county boards, the interconnection queues, and the hostile hearing rooms that are our whole job. The clock didn’t stop at the cliff. It reset, and it’s pointed at us.

Two counter-facts frame the week. First, batteries were never on the cliff — OBBBA left storage’s credit intact — which is exactly why the storage side of the ledger kept clearing approvals over the holiday even as solar caught its breath. Second, the fight over who pays for data-center load moved onto federal ground: FERC put all six grid operators on notice at once. Both threads run in the same direction for our clients — the megawatts are wanted, the money is there, and the binding constraint is the permit and the wire, not the appetite.

North Carolina’s NCUC solar freeze: still no ruling on the reconsideration petition. Ohio’s Oak Run Solar: still in OPSB remand. Sangamon County’s buffer-rezoning gambit: still no dated, in-window outcome, so we keep holding it. Virginia’s HB711 solar-preemption law and its new agrivoltaics definition: in effect, still waiting on the first denial that has to file its reasons with the state. If any breaks, you’ll get a full item.

Must Read

Over the Cliff, Onto the Clock

The safe-harbor deadline that hit Saturday was never really about July 4. It was about the four years that start counting the day after. To lock in the 30% Investment Tax Credit, a developer had to establish the beginning of construction before the cutoff — and, as module maker Heliene laid out in an industry note the week before the deadline, the questions its customers were asking had already moved past “did we make it?” to “can we prove it, and can we finish?” The firms that safe-harbored were relying on the 5% method — incurring at least 5% of project cost before the date, inside a tight 105-day delivery-presumption window — and pairing high-domestic-content modules with domestic racking and inverters to clear the tax-equity bar. The unglamorous work of buying transformers and locking supply is done. What’s left is the part that eats projects: getting built.

Read the four questions Heliene says it keeps hearing and you can see the next two years of risk. Is it too late to safe-harbor? Barely not, but the window shut. How do you hit domestic-content targets without breaking the math? By blending — configurable bills of materials that clear the exact subsidy threshold rather than paying a premium for a 100% domestic module, because the 10% domestic-content bonus isn’t worth overpaying for. Can suppliers actually prove Foreign-Entity-of-Concern compliance? Only with third-party audits and legally vetted traceability, because the penalties are severe and the credit can be clawed back years later. And what’s the plan if a project missed? Mitigate tariff exposure, optimize what incentives remain, and accept the blunt truth the note ends on: after the deadline, the most economical project is simply the one that actually reaches commercial operation. Price stopped being the whole game. Delivery is the game.

That is the sentence to carry into every host-community conversation this quarter. When the binding question becomes “will this thing get built and energized before the credit runs out,” a contested site stops being a political problem and becomes a financial one — a delivery risk that buyers and lenders now price. The developer who arrived early, solved siting, closed the community-benefit agreement, and can show a clean path through the local board is no longer just the good neighbor; it’s the bankable one. The projects pouring concrete this month are the ones that did the engagement work before the sprint, not during it. For RenUSA, the deadline didn’t shrink our value — it repriced it. A trusted relationship with the town is now a line item on the pro forma, because a stalled project on a hostile site is exactly the four-year risk everyone upstream is trying to underwrite out.

The Takeaway

Saturday didn’t settle anything; it started a four-year build clock. Safe-harbored solar and wind now have to reach the grid before the credit lapses, which turns every contested site into a delivery risk that lenders and offtakers price directly. Our job — get the town to yes early, in writing, with a benefit package it will defend — just became the difference between a bankable megawatt and a stranded one. Sell the certainty, not just the goodwill.

Also On Our Radar
  • FERC Puts the Whole Grid on Notice: in orders backed by all five commissioners, FERC issued Section 206 “show cause” orders to all six regional grid operators — PJM, MISO, SPP, CAISO, ISO-NE, and NYISO — demanding they justify or rewrite how they connect large loads like data centers, with responses due inside 60 days. Strip away the procedure and it’s a flexibility order: the reform categories reward co-location, behind-the-meter generation, and resources that shrink a big load’s peak draw on the system. As Aurora’s Oliver Kerr put it, “batteries are one of the few technologies that can make large loads connect faster, easier to manage, less expensive for the rest of the system.” The read for our storage and solar clients: the federal regulator just told every ISO to build rules that pay renewables and storage for the flexibility they provide — and, critically, left states’ siting and permitting authority untouched. The value case we make in a hostile room now has a tailwind in the tariff.
  • Pinal Says Yes to the Battery: the Pinal County, Arizona, Board of Supervisors approved Plus Power’s 250-MW / 2,000-MWh Copper Basin storage project on July 1, clearing three linked land-use cases — a comprehensive-plan change, an industrial rezone, and a planned-area overlay carrying 18 conditions — each on a voice vote with no supervisor opposed. The engagement tape is the lesson. The site sits wrapped on three sides by Salt River Project energy land, next to an existing 270-acre solar field and the substation it will plug into. The town’s planning committee and council both recommended approval unanimously; the county received seven letters of support and zero of opposition; and when supervisors asked the fire-and-safety question, the applicant answered it with a retired Phoenix firefighter walking the board through post-McMicken testing standards. Sited next to the grid you’re serving, backed by the town before the hearing, with the fire narrative met head-on — that is what a “yes” looks like when the homework is done, and it’s the mirror image of the freezes we keep logging elsewhere.
  • A Third of Indiana Reaches for the Pause: nearly a third of Indiana’s counties have now moved to restrict data centers, Indiana University’s Environmental Resilience Institute counts — 11 with ordinances, at least 17 with temporary moratoriums, and two (Marshall and Cass) with outright bans — even as the state courts hyperscalers like Meta’s $10 billion Lebanon campus. On July 1, Indianapolis’s Metropolitan Development Commission voted 5-3 to advance new data-center zoning rules to the City-County Council over residents asking it to pause approvals entirely. Watch the shape, because it’s the one that keeps catching our storage and solar work in the crossfire: when a community has no rulebook for a fast-moving use, it reaches for a freeze, and the freeze rarely bothers to distinguish a battery from a server farm. The developer who shows up holding a model ordinance is the one who avoids the year on ice.
  • Pennsylvania’s Smokestack Problem: a new report finds that seven gas-fired power plants planned to feed Pennsylvania data centers would meaningfully worsen the state’s emissions — the industry’s answer to soaring load is, in several cases, a new fossil plant on the footprint of a retired coal one. For our clients this is less a data-center story than a contrast to bank: when the load fight’s default answer is a gas plant with a visible plume and a real air-quality argument attached, the solar-and-storage alternative gets to be the clean, quiet, water-light neighbor in the same room. The opposition’s sharpest line — “this project will make our air worse” — is one renewables can turn around, but only if we’re the ones framing the comparison first.
