Must Read

Seven to Nothing, With Strings Attached

The San Diego County Planning Commission approved the Starlight Solar project on July 10 by a unanimous 7-0 vote — and on paper, a 7-0 for a nearly 600-acre solar-and-battery project in a rural, organized community looks like a rout. Read the conditions attached to that "yes," though, and it's the opposite of a rout. It's a template for how a hostile room extracts its price even when it loses the vote, and a map of exactly where the fight goes next.

Start with the ground it's on. Starlight would sit in Boulevard, in San Diego's back-country between Old Highway 80 and the Mexico border, on land owned by the Haagen Company — and squarely inside a CAL Fire High Fire Hazard Severity Zone, in a region already scarred by battery-storage fires in Otay Mesa and Escondido. So the opposition didn't argue aesthetics. It argued fire, and it argued it technically: residents attacked the developer's plume study directly, saying the wind conditions modeled didn't match Boulevard's, and that the community hadn't been given time to review the excerpts before the meeting. That's the mature version of NIMBY — not "we don't want it," but "your risk model is wrong, and here's why." And it worked. The commissioners didn't wave the project through; they ordered another review of the plume study and told the developer to move the second-phase batteries as far from homes as possible.

Then look at the rest of the conditions the room pried loose on the way to approval: bigger setbacks from roads and existing homes, a required water-retention basin, dust-control measures with independent monitoring during construction, and more money into the community in phase one. This is the tell for our work. A unanimous approval is not the same as an uncontested one. The vote was 7-0; the project that got approved is materially different — smaller footprint near neighbors, a re-opened fire study, a bigger community package — than the one the developer walked in with. The land-use consultant called it a "win-win" because both sides claimed happiness, and that's real. But the mechanism that produced it was concession under pressure, applied condition by condition.

And the last front is still open. Community and developer remain apart on what the reinvestment actually looks like: residents want a $7 million fund they control at their own discretion; the developer has countered with a refurbished local resource center and electricity-bill reimbursements for qualifying residents. That gap — discretionary cash versus in-kind benefits the developer defines — is the single most negotiable, most winnable thing on the table, and it heads to the County Board of Supervisors on September 16 unresolved. One retiree at the meeting said the money didn't matter to him at all; he wants it stopped for the environment's sake. But he's now the minority voice, because the residents who came in worried left "more positive" — not because the project shrank, but because "the commissioners have heard from us." That sentence is the whole game.

The Takeaway

On a contested renewable site, the conditions are the currency, and you should be the one spending them. Two moves fall straight out of Starlight. First, own the fire model before the hearing: on any BESS in a high-fire zone, get the plume study — and the community's chance to actually read it — out early, because "your wind assumptions are wrong" is the argument that reopens your permit and relocates your batteries. Second, define the community-benefit package yourself, in writing, before the room defines it for you: a vague "we'll invest in phase one" invites a $7M discretionary-fund counter you'll be negotiating in public up to the final vote. A 7-0 is winnable even in a hostile room — but the version that gets approved is the one whose conditions you wrote first.

Also On Our Radar
  • Banned by Arithmetic: Humboldt County, Iowa didn't say no to wind — it just did the math. After 40-plus minutes of testimony, the Board of Supervisors on July 13 unanimously adopted Ordinance No. 77 governing commercial wind, and developer RWE told the board flatly that the setbacks make it "an effective prohibition" — "unworkable," in the words of its attorney, forcing any future project off the county track and onto the Iowa Utilities Commission's state-siting process instead. The wrinkle worth stealing for our side of the table: neighboring landowners can waive most of the setbacks, so participation — signed waivers gathered before the ordinance fight, not after — is the difference between a buildable parcel and a dead one. Note too what else cleared that same meeting: BESS, data-center, pipeline, and utility-scale solar ordinances, all in one night. When a rural county finally writes its renewable rulebook, it tends to write the whole thing at once, and the setback table is where the real policy hides. This is the Midwest local-control pattern arriving in a new county, and it argues for getting in before the ordinance is drafted, when the numbers are still soft.
  • The Health Board Opens a Front: in Panama, New York, the push toward a storage moratorium isn't coming from the zoning board — it's coming from the county's Board of Health. A letter from the Chautauqua County Board of Health, read into the village's July meeting, urged municipalities to "maximize setbacks and minimize decibel levels," cataloged resident complaints about noise, shadow flicker, fire safety, air quality, and emergency response, and offered to help localities weigh the risks — and it was enough to move the mayor to start drafting moratorium language with his code officer, with two solar facilities (one on Blockville-Watts Flats Road, one proposed on Swede Road) already in the village's sightlines. The health board has no zoning authority and says so plainly, but it doesn't need any: a "public health" letter is a permission structure, a way for a moratorium to arrive wearing a lab coat instead of a NIMBY sign. Watch for this vector to spread. When opposition can't win on land use, it reframes as health — and a county health department's letterhead is a lot harder for a village board to wave off than a petition.

Frontlines returns to full strength as the boards come back.

  • INIndianapolis Reads the Room, and So Does the Developer: a City-County Council committee voted 10-3 on July 13 to recommend a data-center moratorium through Dec. 31, 2027, after months of protests; it heads to the full council Aug. 10, with the mayor and the city's own planning staff on board. The context is a statewide wave — at least 17 Indiana counties have enacted moratoriums, and Marshall and Cass have banned data centers outright. But the sharper item for us is the developer response in the same story: days before today's (July 15) Metropolitan Development Commission vote on its east-side campus, DC Blox shrank the project — fewer buildings and generators, a bigger buffer. That's the Starlight move again: concede on footprint and setbacks before the vote, not after the denial.
  • KSLawrence Doubles the Pause: the Lawrence City Commission voted unanimously July 14 to adopt a two-year data-center moratorium — doubling the one-year version staff had proposed — with more than 75 residents packing City Hall. The fight in the room wasn't over whether to pause; the mayor said commissioners were already sold. It was over an exception clause that would let applications proceed case-by-case, which residents attacked as a "loophole" letting "favored projects skip the line." The city attorney says the carve-out exists to preserve due process and limit liability. The read for any land-use pause we're navigating: the exception language is now where the actual negotiation happens, and opponents have learned to hunt for it.

Write the conditions before the room does.
