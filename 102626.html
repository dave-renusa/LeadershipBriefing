RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 64 · Monday, October 26, 2026

Happy Monday, all.

New York wants 6 gigawatts of battery storage by 2030 and had about 576 megawatts at the end of August. Meanwhile one in five Adirondack Park towns and villages has paused batteries this year, and three have banned them. The state already rewrote its fire code to answer the fear driving those pauses. The pauses kept coming anyway, because a code in Albany does not reach a town board on its own. Someone has to bring it into the room. The same pattern runs through the rest of this issue. Kentucky's Daviess County capped solar at 1,200 acres countywide after residents asked for a ban the law would not allow. Wisconsin residents took a 765-kV fight to 49 legislative offices in one day. The local rules are being written now, and the side that shows up is writing them.

Must Read

Twenty-One Towns Hit Pause

Twenty-one of the Adirondack Park's 101 towns and villages have filed battery storage moratoriums since Jan. 1, the Adirondack Explorer reports. The pauses run six months to a year. Fort Ann in Washington County, Gloversville in Fulton County and Forestport in Oneida County have gone further and banned battery systems outright. North Elba and Lake Placid added theirs this month, and Ticonderoga and Hadley are working on renewals.

St. Armand in Essex County shows how fast a single proposal can turn into a ban. Carson Power proposed a 20-megawatt facility near the Mount Pisgah ski area. The town passed a one-year moratorium in March and a ban in June, without finishing its state environmental review. Supervisor Davina Thurston says the town is now running that review and will pass the ban again, and that the ban would block the project. NYSERDA's director of clean energy siting visited the town on Sept. 1 to explain the state's safeguards. Carson Power declined an invitation to that meeting.

An approval is not the end of it either. The Adirondack Park Agency permitted the park's first utility-scale battery, a 10-megawatt, four-hour Carson Power system in Northampton. A resident then sued the town, the agency, the developer and the landowner, and a Fulton County judge issued a temporary restraining order that is holding the project up. No ruling on the merits has been issued.

Dannemora in Clinton County is going the other way. Supervisor Joey Varin has declined calls from outside the town to pass a moratorium. He has attended battery and fire-safety education sessions while Brookfield Renewable Partners studies a site on the old Lyon Mountain mine tailings, and he plans a public meeting early next year. New York has already changed its fire code so that developers must pay for a peer review, inspect with the local permitting authority, train local fire departments, and get a battery fire expert on site within four hours of any fire. An industry consortium has testified that more than 100 New York municipalities have passed moratoriums, stalling more than a gigawatt of projects.

The Takeaway

New York has already written the fire safeguards these towns are asking for, and the pauses keep spreading anyway. The difference is the conversation in each town. Dannemora's supervisor went to the safety sessions and set his own timeline. St. Armand heard from the state only after its ban was on the books, and the developer did not show up. For any client with a battery in its plan, the fire chief briefing and the code walkthrough have to happen before the first moratorium is drafted, in person, and with the developer in the room.

Also On Our Radar
  • Contested, Officially: Wisconsin's Public Service Commission on Oct. 15 sent Midcontinent Grid Solutions' application to become a Wisconsin utility to a contested-case hearing. Requests to become a party are due Oct. 29. The company is developing BECI, a roughly 190-mile 765-kV line across southern Wisconsin. The day before, residents from 10 counties took the fight to the Capitol, where Rep. Derrick Van Orden said he would oppose the lines in the Driftless Region. Eighty-nine local governments have passed resolutions on the lines. Organizers met with 49 legislative offices and are publishing each office's answer, which turns a lobby day into a scorecard.
  • Pahrump Says Yes, 4-1: Nye County commissioners granted a special use permit for Copper Rays Solar, up to 700 megawatts of solar and 700 megawatts of four-hour storage on 4,414 acres of federal land southeast of Pahrump, Nevada. Residents raised the valley's water supply and the cumulative footprint of six planned projects covering nearly 20,000 acres. Federal review is still listed as under protest.
  • Twenty Percent for the Oaks: The developer of Coyote Creek Solar near Folsom, California settled a lawsuit by cutting the project's footprint about 20 percent, saving roughly 3,300 blue oaks and putting a conservation easement on the land. The project, 200 megawatts of solar with a 100-megawatt battery, was approved 5-0 by Sacramento County supervisors last November. It still needs a power buyer after SMUD canceled its contract in January.
  • No Ban, So a Cap: Daviess County, Kentucky's Fiscal Court limited solar to 300 acres per project and 1,200 acres of farmland countywide, with projects at least three miles apart and 1,000-foot setbacks from homes, schools and roads. The judge-executive said residents asked for an outright ban, which state law does not allow. The zoning change passed 3-1.
  • Permitted, Not Settled: Dozens of residents packed a Hounsfield, New York meeting over AES's 140-megawatt Greens Corners Solar, which already holds its state siting permit. Residents raised farmland, safety and property values, and some want legal action to revoke it. A town board member said the board backs farmers earning solar income but opposes putting prime farmland at the center of the project. Construction is expected next year.
On the Frontlines
  • COMirasol Gets the Land: Colorado's State Land Board approved its largest solar lease on state trust land: 1,140 acres for NextEra's Mirasol Solar about five miles south of Avondale in Pueblo County. The lease covers 120 megawatts. County filings allow up to 200 megawatts of solar plus 200 megawatts of storage.
  • LAVernon Tables It Twice: The Vernon Parish Police Jury postponed tighter solar rules for a second straight month at the district attorney's request. The draft would double road buffers to 300 feet, require 500-foot setbacks from neighbors' property lines and cap noise at 10 decibels above ambient. A moratorium has been in place since Aug. 10, and the parish president says the jury faces state and federal pressure to lift it.
  • SCTax Deals and a Pause, Same Night: Marlboro County Council on Oct. 13 passed second reading of fee-in-lieu-of-taxes agreements for Strata Energy's 442-acre Violet Solar and a 100-acre Pivot Energy project, and approved a four-month moratorium on new solar applications while it reviews its solar and wind rules.
  • NCDenton Goes Four Years: The Denton Town Council in Davidson County voted 4-0 on Oct. 6 for a four-year moratorium on solar farms in town limits and its extraterritorial jurisdiction.
  • INKnox Adds Distance: Knox County commissioners passed first reading 2-1 of larger solar setbacks: an extra 100 feet from homes bordered on one side and 500 feet from homes bordered on more than one, plus soil and well-water monitoring. A 750-foot proposal died for lack of a second. The ordinance now goes to the Area Plan Commission.
  • VAWashington County Draws the Lines: Washington County supervisors adopted a battery storage ordinance on Oct. 13 that restricts where larger systems, those over 600 kilowatt-hours, can be placed.
  • TXSolstice Is Next: Texas regulators extended their rehearing window on Oncor's Dinosaur-to-Drill Hole 765-kV line to Dec. 6, and vote Nov. 5 on a similar extension for the Howard-to-Solstice line approved Sept. 11.
  • ILMadison County Asks Its Voters: Madison County's Nov. 3 ballot carries a question on wind and solar farm development. The county board denied a solar permit 16-7 in March despite a lawsuit threat.
  • MALeominster, 8-0: The Leominster City Council approved one-year moratoriums on both battery storage and data centers, effective until July 31, 2027, citing water, power and fire risk.
  • CAForty-Five Days, Six-Month Study: San Joaquin County supervisors voted 5-0 for a 45-day freeze on data center approvals in unincorporated areas. The county's own power study needs about six months, so an extension is likely. San Francisco's mayor signed a similar 45-day pause on Oct. 16.

The towns that paused batteries are not waiting for a better fire code, because New York already wrote one. They are waiting for someone to explain it to them in person. Get to the fire chief and the town board before the moratorium draft does.
