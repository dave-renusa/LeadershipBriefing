RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 47 · Monday, August 31, 2026

Happy Monday, all.

Three decisions landed in the last week and none of them were really about solar, wind, or servers — they were about who gets the last word, and every party involved picked a different room to get it in. In Franklin County, Virginia, a circuit judge approved a solar project the board of supervisors had voted down 5-1, on the narrow ground that the land was unzoned and the county's own comprehensive plan set aside 1,500 acres for exactly this — meaning the board's only job was to check conformity, and it never articulated a reason the project failed. In Whitman County, Washington, a developer facing an ordinance that functionally bans locally permitted wind stopped arguing with the county and filed with the state siting council instead. And in Fredericksburg, a council that had watched two planning-commission denials take the question back and answered it itself, 7-0, at 12:46 in the morning. The pattern to carry into this week: the merits are increasingly decided by which body is holding the gavel, and the side that thinks hardest about venue — comprehensive plan, state preemption, circuit court, or a four-hour hearing — is the side that wins. An Oklahoma judge hears one of these venue fights this morning.

Must Read

The Plan Was the Permit

A Franklin County Circuit Court judge on Friday cleared the Constitution Solar project in the county's Henry community to proceed, overriding a board of supervisors that had rejected the more-than-35-acre development 5-1 in December. The ruling did not turn on need, or jobs, or climate. It turned on a document the county wrote itself.

Franklin County's land is unzoned. Where there is no zoning, there is no discretionary zoning standard to apply — the only legal test a project must pass is conformity with the county's comprehensive plan, and that plan designates 1,500 acres for solar use. Union Hall District Supervisor Dan Quinn, the board's lone yes vote, framed the question exactly as the court eventually did: "Did this project conform with the comprehensive plan?" His account of the December meeting is the part worth reading twice — he said none of the other supervisors "had any rationale for citing a reason that it did not conform with the comprehensive plan." Five votes, no articulated ground. That is not a denial a court can defend.

The objections raised in the room were real, but they were the wrong objections for the forum. Blue Ridge District Supervisor Tim Tatum pressed on end-of-life cleanup, and specifically on whether a posted decommissioning bond would still cover the cost after twenty-plus years of inflation. That is a legitimate and serious concern — and it is a condition to negotiate, an escalator clause to write into a bond, a five-year re-estimate requirement. It is not a finding of nonconformity with a comprehensive plan. A board that converts a conditions problem into a denial hands the applicant the courthouse.

Franklin County now joins a run of the same outcome from opposite directions. Illinois boards have been ordered to approve projects they denied. A Louisiana appeals court sent a permit denial back over procedural failures. Ohio's Supreme Court remanded the state's largest solar project on the narrowest of grounds — the visual impact of substations — rather than on the merits everyone spent two years arguing. The common thread is not that courts favor solar. It is that boards keep losing on the record: they vote the room, then discover that the room is not a legal standard.

For our side of the table, the operational read is the inverse and it is uncomfortable. If a county's own comprehensive plan is the whole case, then the comprehensive plan is where the fight actually happens, and it happens years before an application exists — in plan updates, land-use element rewrites, and the quiet resolutions that add or subtract a designated acreage figure. Franklin County's 1,500 acres were the entire ballgame, and they were adopted long before anyone had heard of Constitution Solar. Wherever we are working, the question to ask this week is not what the ordinance says. It is what the comprehensive plan says, who is currently proposing to amend it, and whether anyone on our side is in that room.

The Takeaway

Boards are losing these cases because they vote on sentiment and then cannot name a legal ground. That cuts both ways: it means a comprehensive plan is a durable asset when it names solar acreage, and a permanent liability when opponents amend it while nobody is watching. Audit the comprehensive plan in every county we work — and treat a proposed plan amendment as a higher-priority engagement event than any single hearing.

Also On Our Radar
  • No Byte at 12:46 a.m.: Fredericksburg City Council voted 7-0 to deny the rezoning for the 1500 Gateway data center, then 7-0 again to deny the special use permit that would have raised the two buildings from 50 to 75 feet, after a public hearing that ran more than four hours and adjourned around 12:46 a.m. Wednesday. The application, filed by 1500 Gateway LLC, covered roughly 83 to 84 acres of the Hylton tract along I-95 and about a million square feet of building; the city planning commission had recommended denial twice. Council members cited the site's position outside the Technology Overlay District the city mapped in 2025, transmission routing that would run near residential Cowan Boulevard, low-frequency sound, and the public trust built during the overlay process. The parcel remains zoned Planned Development–Medical Center, and the city's own mapped data-center location near Celebrate Virginia South is described as power-constrained. Read the sequence, not just the tally: an overlay district adopted a year earlier did the analytical work, and by the time the hearing started every council member had a map to point at.
  • Around the County, Not Through It: after Whitman County commissioners adopted a commercial wind ordinance in March on a 2-1 vote — Tom Handy and Chad Whetzel yes, Art Swannack no — with buffers running seven miles from Kamiak Butte and other landmark sites, Steelhead Americas stopped litigating the county and filed a pre-application with the Washington Energy Facility Site Evaluation Council for the 200-MW Harvest Hills project — 45 turbines up to nearly 700 feet on private land between Colfax and Palouse. The county code, as reported at adoption, functionally prohibits locally permitted wind; Save the Palouse backs the ordinance and Nimiipuu Protecting the Environment has endorsed the project. Meanwhile the county's separate draft solar and battery-storage chapters are still in workshop, and a siting feasibility study floated a 1,500-foot setback that critics say would make solar and BESS construction close to impossible. Two lessons in one county: a local ban does not end a project, it relocates the venue — and the ordinance written for wind becomes the template for the storage ordinance nobody has voted on yet.
  • Confirm It or Redraw It: the record is finally closed on Oak Run, and the Ohio Power Siting Board's own staff filed an Aug. 7 brief asking the full board to confirm the permit for the state's largest proposed solar and storage project — 800 MW of solar and 300 MW of storage. The board is not bound by its staff and has no deadline to act. The two-year detour came from an appeal by Madison County and three of its townships, which produced a state Supreme Court remand instructing regulators to address the project's visual impacts more thoroughly, specifically through photo simulations or sketches showing how the substations would look from public vantage points. A gigawatt-class project has now spent two years on rendering quality. Every visual-impact exhibit we produce should be built to survive an appellate reading, not a hearing-room glance.
  • Pause, Then Appeal: the North Carolina Utilities Commission chair's April order directing Duke Energy to stop processing new solar requests for 2026 is now being fought on two tracks — a motion from the Southern Environmental Law Center on behalf of the Southern Alliance for Clean Energy, Vote Solar and the Sierra Club asking the panel to reconsider, and an industry appeal. The order does not cancel anything already approved; it simply declines to add, which delays roughly 770 MW and pushes the uncertainty into future procurement years. This is the cleanest example of the year of a decision that is invisible at the county level and decisive at the portfolio level. When a state pause has no hearing room attached to it, there is no room for us to work — which is exactly why the intervention has to be at the commission.
  • Second Swing at the Surplus: PJM is trying again to build a workable surplus-interconnection pathway, letting new generation use spare capacity at existing plant interconnection points after an earlier attempt at the same reform went essentially unused. The context makes it matter: the reopened standard queue drew 811 requests totaling about 220 GW, including 67.5 GW of storage and 14.8 GW of solar plus 8.8 GW of hybrid — against 106 GW of gas. Surplus interconnection is the only near-term route that skips the study queue entirely, and it puts projects on land that already hosts generation, which is also the land where the siting argument is easiest to win. Worth knowing which of our host communities sit next to an existing interconnection point.
  • Sunniest State, Roughest Year: the Arizona Corporation Commission approved line-siting for 12 solar and battery storage projects between 2025 and the first eight months of 2026 — and in the same stretch, state lawmakers and regulators moved to block utility-scale solar on state land, repeal rooftop tax incentives, and raise fees on solar adopters, with the commission cutting APS and UNS Electric residential export rates by 10% on Aug. 12. Both things are true at once, and that is the useful part: transmission-connected utility-scale keeps clearing while the distributed-solar constituency gets squeezed. The political coalition that shows up for us in a county hearing is often built out of rooftop owners, and it is being actively demobilized at the state level.
On the Frontlines
  • OKArgued This Morning: Wagoner County District Judge Douglas Kirkley hears Persica Solar's appeal at 9 a.m. today after a full briefing schedule — plaintiff's brief in chief in June, county response in July, reply in late July. The NextEra subsidiary is asking the court either to issue the conditional use permit outright or to order the commissioners to issue it, for a $336 million, nearly 5,000-acre project outside Porter that county commissioners denied 2-1. The 164-page brief leans heavily on the meeting minutes: the permit request was delayed four times before the fifth meeting produced the no vote. Commissioners maintain they properly exercised police power and that the denial was not arbitrary or capricious. Serial continuances are becoming a documented liability, not a neutral scheduling choice.
  • WAStill Waiting on Nine Justices: the Washington Supreme Court heard argument June 11 on the challenges to Scout Clean Energy's 1,150-MW Horse Heaven Hills complex south of the Tri-Cities, and the ruling has not landed. Tri-Cities CARES, Benton County and the Yakama Nation filed separate petitions to overturn the October 2024 site certification, arguing the state siting council and the governor moved too fast on environmental, community and tribal cultural impacts. The project spans roughly 72,500 acres along I-82. Whatever the court says will set the terms for every EFSEC-route project in the state — including the one Whitman County just pushed there.
  • WABuffer Math in Pomeroy: Garfield County commissioners are weighing a wind-tower buffer zone, revisiting turbine siting distances in a county that let its wind moratorium lapse before the planning commission finished rewriting the ordinance — leaving applicants filed in the interim subject to the old code, with Cloudwalker Wind and solar development near Pomeroy among the projects that followed. Buffer distances are the quietest possible way to reduce a buildable envelope, and they are usually set in a room with no developer in it.
  • NYThe Village Votes on the Town's Project: the Saugerties Village Board unanimously passed Resolution #5 on Aug. 3, asking the Town of Saugerties for a moratorium or outright denial of Key Capture Energy's 100-MW KCE NY 34 battery project pending demonstrated public safety and environmental protection. The town is proceeding with its review anyway. The site is 59.8 acres at 7 Tomsons Road, with roughly eleven acres of battery array and the nearest homes about 1,200 feet away. The village's ask is specific and worth copying down, because it is becoming the standard demand list: an independent quantitative risk assessment, modeled evacuation zones, smoke-dispersion modeling under worst-case weather, and written fire-department certification that local companies can actually respond. A jurisdiction with no vote can still write the conditions everyone ends up arguing about.
  • NYTwo Adirondack Towns, Two Speeds: Tupper Lake's town board introduced moratoriums on battery storage and data centers, drawing no public comment at all at the Aug. 24 meeting and pausing any pending BESS application before the joint planning board; North Elba tabled its own BESS moratorium while the supervisor asked the town attorney to draft one, likely six months with an extension option. Note the coupling again — storage swept in alongside data centers with no separate record on storage — and note the attendance. An empty room is not consent; it is a moratorium passing at zero cost.
  • OHThirty-Four Comments From Nobody: at least 34 comments opposing the Crossroads Solar project in Morrow County appear to have been fabricated, submitted in the names of people who did not authorize them or who do not exist — and the Ohio Power Siting Board has not referred a single fake comment for prosecution in the last ten years. Regulators blocked the project regardless. Clean-energy advocates argue that misleading public officials through knowingly false testimony is already illegal and should be charged. The practical exposure is ours as much as anyone's: a docket that cannot distinguish real comments from manufactured ones devalues every genuine letter of support we help a resident write. Verified, attributable, named support is now a differentiator — insist on it.
  • INKnox Recommends the Battery Anyway: the Knox County Area Plan Commission voted to recommend approval of a battery storage system attached to an already-approved commercial solar project in Harrison Township, over strong opposition from area residents. The structural advantage is worth naming: the storage was proposed as an addition to a project that had already cleared its entitlement, which meant the hearing was about one component rather than about whether the site should exist at all. Where storage can be phased onto an approved solar footprint rather than sited cold, it faces a materially different room.
  • NMTwo Approvals, One Appeal Threat: the Santa Fe County Planning Commission approved Linea Energy's 350-MW Globemallow project on roughly 2,000 acres in the southeastern part of the county, less than a year after commissioners approved AES's Rancho Viejo Solar 4-1 — 96 MW of solar with 48 MW of storage — over opponents who said they would appeal to state District Court. Santa Fe County has now said yes twice in a jurisdiction where saying yes was supposed to be impossible; the appeal risk moved to the courthouse, not the calendar.
  • KSSpecial Use, Granted: Finney County commissioners approved special use permits for Triple Oak's renewable projects, including Sherlock Solar's roughly 400-MW facility across about 6,150 acres and the 330-MW Lone Bison project on roughly 3,850 acres in western Finney County, with construction windows running into 2027. The projects sit inside the broader Home Range program spanning parts of Finney, Kearny and Wichita counties. Southwest Kansas keeps clearing utility-scale acreage at a scale that would be unthinkable two states east.
  • COSchool Money Moves the Room: the Colorado State Land Board approved the lease for the Mirasol Solar project on about 1,140 acres of state trust land in Pueblo County — a $500 million private investment sized to power roughly 36,000 homes, projected to generate more than $26 million for Colorado's Public School Trust over the lease term, which feeds the state's Building Excellent Schools Today grant program. Siting on state trust land routes the benefit argument straight into school construction budgets, which is a constituency that shows up. Where trust or institutional land is available, the host-benefit case writes itself.
  • ILSmall Project, Full Ordinance: the Tazewell County Board denied a special use permit for a 5-MW commercial solar farm near Mackinaw at the intersection of Lowery and Gresham roads, with members citing location and nearby-resident concerns — and in the same action moved to regulate future renewable projects countywide. That second half is the one that matters. A denial affects one applicant; an ordinance adopted in the same motion affects every applicant for years, and it gets written while everyone is still arguing about the five megawatts.
  • UTFrom Ban to Rulebook: Emery County's conversation about utility-scale solar shifted from an outright prohibition to writing detailed ordinances setting development requirements instead. This is the outcome we should be engineering everywhere, and it almost never happens by itself — a county talks itself out of a ban when someone credible offers it a rulebook it can defend. Somebody handed Emery County a third option. That is the job.

A board that votes the room and cannot name the standard loses in circuit court. A county that bans local permitting loses the permit to the state. A council that spends a year mapping an overlay district can deny a project 7-0 at a quarter to one in the morning and make it stick. The difference between those outcomes is not sentiment, and it is not the merits — it is whether somebody did the paperwork years earlier. Go find out what your county's comprehensive plan says this week.
