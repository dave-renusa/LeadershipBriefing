Wednesday morning, team.

Two weeks ago we handed the team a play. In Friday's Vol. 29 we singled out East Point Energy's 150-MW Mill Stone battery in Chesapeake as a model of how to lose the room and keep the project: over-build the setback nearly five times over, document everything, and go over the planning commission's head to the elected body that actually decides. The council vote was set for Tuesday, July 21. On Tuesday morning, hours before the public hearing, East Point withdrew the application. That's our Must Read, and it is the most instructive thing to happen to a storage client all month — because a bulletproof record still couldn't manufacture a "yes" out of an elected board, and the developer read that in time to reset the clock rather than take a recorded no.

Hold that next to a mirror-image story out of Illinois. Same fight — developer versus a local board that keeps saying no — opposite resolution. A McHenry County judge just ordered the county board to approve six solar projects it had voted down, on pain of contempt, because the state siting law leaves the board no discretion to deny once the criteria are met. One developer retreated in front of the elected no; another went and got a court to override it. The through-line for our clients is the one we keep selling: when your record is clean, the fight stops being about the merits and becomes entirely about venue and timing — which room, which clock, which sovereign.

Must Read

Withdrawal Symptoms

East Point Energy's Mill Stone Energy Center was, on paper, the best-prepared contested battery in our coverage. A 150-MW lithium-iron-phosphate storage facility on about 31 acres of a larger parcel at 1912 Centerville Turnpike South in Chesapeake's Hickory area, it walked into its process with the kind of record we tell clients to build: a setback of 1,431 feet against a required 300, a fence and sound barrier, roughly 80 acres left permanently undeveloped, a written prohibition on any data center on the property, more than 3,000 feet of separation from Hickory High School, and a tax-revenue figure north of $25 million over the project's life. It still drew a 7-1 planning-commission recommendation to deny on June 10, driven by the affluent Woodard's Mill neighborhood raising fire, wells, and "character." The elected City Council was scheduled to make the real call on July 21.

It never did. On the morning of the hearing, an agent for the applicant filed a request to withdraw. No decision was announced on whether Mill Stone will be re-filed, and under Virginia law there is no waiting period — the company could refile as soon as the next day. So this is not a denial. Nothing is on the record. The project is not dead; it is paused by its own hand, on the developer's timetable rather than the board's. But make no mistake about what a voluntary withdrawal on the morning of the vote signals: East Point's own head count told it the council was going to say no, and it chose an unrecorded retreat over a recorded loss.

That distinction is worth more than it looks, because Virginia just changed the cost of a recorded loss. The 2026 General Assembly's new solar-and-storage laws (effective July 1) require a locality to report every denial of a solar or storage project to the State Corporation Commission within 60 days, and Chesapeake's own council voted 8-0 on June 23 to start rewriting Article 13 of its zoning ordinance to match those laws. A denial today would have been a data point the city had to justify to Richmond, and it would have frozen Mill Stone under the old rules. A withdrawal keeps the slate clean and lets East Point refile into the new rulebook once the city finishes writing it — potentially a friendlier frame, since the state now treats storage as a favored use. The retreat isn't surrender. It's a bet that the ground will be better in six months than it was on Tuesday.

The Takeaway

Two lessons, and they cut against each other in a useful way. First: a perfect record neutralizes "it doesn't meet our standards," but it does not manufacture political will. Mill Stone cleared every ordinance by a mile and still couldn't move an elected body that had decided. Over-compliance wins the technical argument and loses the room anyway when the room is already gone — so stop treating the documented record as the whole game and start counting votes on the dais weeks out. Second: know the difference between a withdrawal and a denial, and use it. In a state that now makes localities answer to regulators for every no, a recorded denial is expensive for the town and a hard public wall for you; a clean withdrawal costs the town nothing to accept and costs you only time. When your head count is short and the rulebook is mid-rewrite, pulling the application is not losing — it's refusing to hand the other side a precedent. The play we praised in Vol. 29 didn't fail. It just found its own exit.

Also On Our Radar
  • Contempt of Cornfield: the opposite of the Chesapeake retreat played out in northern Illinois this week, and it is the strongest local-control story of the cycle. A McHenry County circuit judge, Joel Berg, ordered the county board to approve a slate of solar projects it had rejected — issuing a writ of mandamus and warning the board, as a body, that it faces contempt if it refuses. The board had voted 1-16 to deny Pebble Solar and 4-13 to deny Water Locust, even though the zoning board acknowledged both met every siting criterion; the only stated reason was that members "did not feel sufficient efforts were made to minimize impact to woodlands." The judge found that Illinois's 2023 siting statute flatly bars a county from denying on grounds stricter than state law allows, so the denials were "not supported by the record" and the county had a "clear, mandatory and non-discretionary duty to approve." Six consent-order cases — Pebble, Water Locust, Crystal Lake, Arena Topaz, Railway, plus a Pivot Energy zoning fix — landed on Tuesday night's agenda, their passage court-mandated. Board member Matt Kunkle's response is the quote of the week: "I will be a 'No' vote. If they put me in jail, I could use the vacation." The read for us: where a state has a real preemption statute with teeth, the local no is theater, and the smart developer litigates rather than negotiates. Illinois just showed what teeth look like.
  • Two Blues, No Blues: while the fights grab the headlines, the build quietly continued. Heelstone Renewable Energy, a Qualitas Energy company, reached financial close on July 20 and started construction on three Illinois solar projects — the 40-MW Two Blues and 28-MW Snapdragon in Perry County and the 18-MW Pike in Fayette County, 86 MW combined — all backed by long-term corporate PPAs with Meta for the full environmental-attribute output, with commercial operation targeted by the end of 2026. Stonehenge Capital took the tax equity; ING led the debt. The detail worth stealing for a host-community deck: this is a hyperscaler paying for renewables it will consume, sited in-state, financed and under construction now, on a timeline measured in months rather than the interconnection-purgatory years our clients keep quoting. When a township asks "why here, why now," the answer in Perry County is that the load showed up with a checkbook and the panels are already going in the ground. Different framing than "trust us, the grid needs it."
  • Big Towers, Big Fight: the transmission spine that all of this renewable buildout eventually rides on drew its own crowd in West Virginia. Valley Link — the 260-mile, PJM-identified line that would cross Tucker, Barbour, Lewis and neighboring north-central counties on its way to Maryland and Virginia — held back-to-back open houses this week, in Barbour County on July 16 and Lewis County on July 17, and landowners left unhappy on both nights. Farmers complained the poster-board format "won't even give you answers," and the "Big Towers, Big Mistake. Stop MARL & Valley Link" red shirts that have trailed the project since June turned out again. State filings aren't expected until mid-2027, with a 2031 in-service target, which means the corridor is still soft and the engagement is still all upside for whoever does it well. It also means the opposition has eighteen months to organize before a single regulator sees a route. We flag it because the wires are the renewables story most developers forget to staff: no line, no interconnection, no project — and the fight over the line starts years before the fight over the panels.
Project Updates

Editor's note: under our seven-day freshness rule, an item runs only when the proceeding traces to a dated, in-window page. Mid-summer recess kept the in-geography local calendar thin again this week — several of the week's sharpest fights are carried up in Must Read and Radar, where they belong. Three verified proceedings below, each traced to a July 20–21 page. The through-line: the moratorium is now the default first move, and it keeps failing to distinguish a battery from a server hall.

  • KSMontgomery County Can't Find a Second: a proposed six-month moratorium on wind, solar, battery storage and data centers never even came to a vote at the July 20 commission meeting — the motion died for lack of a second. The county administrator had walked commissioners through a benefits-and-risks report covering community investment alongside zoning and water concerns; supporters pitched jobs and infrastructure, opponents raised property taxes, health and water use, with one resident invoking the specter of "a Pitcher, Oklahoma." The instructive part is the failure itself: even in a county primed for a pause, a blanket freeze that sweeps renewables in with data centers couldn't muster two votes. A moratorium is only the easy default until someone on the dais has to own lumping a solar lease in with a hyperscaler. That's the seam to work.
  • SCSumter Says the Quiet Part: the county council approved second reading of three moratorium ordinances on July 21 — separate 180-day pauses on new solar, battery-storage and data-center applications — after months of opposition to a 1,700-acre solar farm the council ultimately rejected. Chairman James McCain didn't hedge: "I think the county council is at a point where we're not going to accept any solar farms in Sumter County." Out of our core geography, but worth tracking because state Sen. Jeff Zell framed Sumter as "leading on this statewide issue," and a chair openly declaring the county closed to solar is the kind of on-the-record hostility that migrates. One more reading before the pauses take effect. When a jurisdiction stops pretending the moratorium is temporary, the engagement window has already closed — you have to be upstream of the 1,700-acre proposal that triggered it.
  • MIFabius Fires a Flare at Lansing: a rural St. Joseph County township of 3,300 did the sophisticated thing this week, and it's aimed at us as much as at the state. With a roughly 178-MW solar array paired with a 122-MW battery system — EDP Renewables' Three Lakes Solar and Battery Storage — already moving forward on local farmland under PA 233 over township objections, the Fabius Township board unanimously passed resolutions on July 21 demanding Lansing return siting control to localities and banning township officials from ever signing NDAs. Supervisor Dan Wilkins — "I'm just fed up" — has sent the resolutions to the Speaker, the Senate Majority Leader and both local lawmakers, one of whom backs a full PA 233 repeal. It landed the same week Gov. Whitmer rolled out a voluntary data-center pledge on July 15. The NDA ban is the tell, and it's the demand we keep seeing attach to renewable and data projects alike: transparency-as-precondition. A developer who still expects to run a confidential host-community negotiation in southwest Michigan is drafting from a playbook the towns have already publicly torn up.

  • WVMaidsville Waits, the Wires Move: the transmission fight we've tracked in Radar has a human face on the ground in north-central West Virginia this week, where Valley Link's open-house tour hit Lewis County on July 17 after Barbour the night before. Residents packed Robert L. Bland Middle School and left saying the format gave them "no answers," while the "Stop MARL & Valley Link" signs multiplied outside. PJM says the 260-mile line is needed to keep the lights on across the region; landowners say the towers benefit distant load and stick them with the corridor. No route is chosen, no filing is in — which is exactly why this is a Frontlines item and not a docket one. The organizing is happening in the eighteen-month gap before regulators ever see a map, and the coalition forming in these gymnasiums is the same one that will show up at the SCC and the PSC in 2027. Whoever wants that line built should be in Lewis County now, not in the filing.

A withdrawal is not a denial. Know which one you're taking.
