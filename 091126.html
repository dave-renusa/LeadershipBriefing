RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 50 · Friday, September 11, 2026

Friday morning, all.

Nobody argued about whether the power was needed this week. They argued about notice, schedule and standing — and that is where every case moved. In Texas, the PUC chairman told his four colleagues on Wednesday that two of three 765-kV proposals should go back to the administrative judges because the proposal for decision was incomplete on federal reliability standards and silent on consumer savings, after hundreds of landowners filed challenges saying they never got the advance notice the law requires. In Wisconsin, the Dane County Board voted unanimously to demand an independent necessity study and a ratepayer cost-benefit analysis on a 190-mile 765-kV project that has not filed an application — objecting specifically to one joint open house for two counties and a September 7 comment deadline. In West Virginia, the PSC denied its own staff's request for more time and kept the October hearing dates on a 500-kV case. And in Ohio, one resident sued Wilmington over a data-center rezoning passed as an "emergency" ordinance — a designation that under state law takes effect immediately and cannot be referended — and had a standstill agreement inside of a day. Four states, four fights about process, zero about need.

Also On Our Radar
  • Back to the Judges: the Public Utility Commission of Texas took up certificates from Oncor, AEP Texas and CPS Energy for three 765-kV lines covering nearly 800 miles through at least 40 counties, and Chairman Thomas Gleeson wrote the other four commissioners Wednesday recommending two of the three be remanded to the State Office of Administrative Hearings — his stated reason being that SOAH's proposal for decision failed to address federal reliability standards and omitted consumer savings figures. Hundreds of landowners have filed letters and challenges arguing they never received legally required advance notice and that the commission's 180-day processing window is too short to answer them. Sen. Charles Schwertner, who chairs Senate Business & Commerce, has called on the PUC to reject all three after a 15-hour July hearing; Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick and Rep. Ken King have echoed him. The same commission on Friday approved the roughly 370-mile Howard Solstice line across 14 counties including Uvalde, Kerr, Bexar and Medina — conditioned on the transmission companies working with landowners on further route modifications to cut construction through grassland. Read the two together: the commission is not hostile to the buildout, it is hostile to a record it cannot defend, and the thing that put two cases in jeopardy was notice.
  • Split Decision: Illinois' Third and Fourth District appellate courts have now landed on opposite answers to how much authority counties retain over utility-scale solar, and the question is heading toward the Illinois Supreme Court on four active cases. The Fourth District upheld Winnebago County's block of a project; the Third District forced the Will County Board in April to approve six projects it had previously rejected, and Grundy County has appealed. Shorewood and Channahon are suing over forced approvals on their borders, and attorney Steven Becker has filed a due-process suit for Will County residents against Earthrise/Lincoln Energy's 6,100-acre Pride of the Prairie project across Green Garden, Manhattan and Wilton townships. Joliet attorney James Murphy is carrying Winnebago, Grundy, Shorewood and Channahon — one lawyer, four jurisdictions, which is how a county-level grievance becomes a statewide precedent.
  • Eleven Point Four: the U.S. installed 11.4 GWdc of solar in the second quarter, up 45% year over year, with utility-scale volume up 61% while residential and community solar fell, per SEIA and Wood Mackenzie. Texas led at 3.44 GW, Arizona 1.76 GW, Florida 1.61 GW. The strength is safe-harbored pipeline built ahead of the July 4 begin-construction deadline, and the analysts are blunt that what happens next is a permitting and interconnection story rather than a demand story — they project roughly 44 GWdc a year through 2030, essentially flat. Solar's share of total installed capacity actually fell, from 51% in 2025 to 45% in the first half of 2026.
  • Record Quarter, Shrinking Pipeline: ACP's Q2 market report counts 164 projects and 17.1 GW online in the quarter, cumulative clean power capacity near 388 GW, and land-based wind's strongest second quarter on record at nearly 5 GW — driven largely by Pattern's 3.65 GW SunZia commissioning, which put New Mexico at the top of the state rankings and ended a two-and-a-half-year Texas streak. The number to carry is the other one: the development pipeline hit a record 205 GW overall, but the land-based wind pipeline shrank 4% quarter over quarter, with more than 11.6 GW of expected capacity delayed by federal permitting and review backlogs. Wind is posting its best installation quarter and its worst origination quarter simultaneously, which is what a permitting bottleneck looks like on a two-year lag.
  • Emergency Powers, Ambiguous Scope: the Aug. 26 executive order declaring a national security emergency over foreign-made grid equipment bans acquisition, import, transfer or installation of foreign-produced bulk-power system equipment for transactions initiated on or after that date — and gives agencies authority to force owners to isolate, disconnect, replace or remove equipment already installed. Norton Rose Fulbright's Keith Martin says the order does not clarify whether it reaches only software and controls or the cells and modules themselves, and China supplies roughly three-fifths of the lithium-ion batteries in U.S. grid installations. DOE has until Dec. 24 to issue clarifying rules. BloombergNEF's Isshu Kikuma puts more than 23 GW of large-scale storage already at financial close or under construction in the exposed set. Every storage project we are engaging on now has a financing question attached to it that no amount of community goodwill answers.
  • Eleven Hundred Thirty-Seven: the Georgia PSC approved seven Georgia Power solar PPAs totaling 1,137 MW under the CARES 2023 and CARES 2025 programs — Appling 78 MW, Decatur 130, Jefferson 194, Sumter 200, Warren 150, Emanuel 185 and Irwin 200, with commercial operation as early as 2029. Georgia Power calls the CARES 2023 tranche its largest single solar procurement ever, on top of 1,068 MW cleared a year ago, and has opened enrollment on a customer-identified program approved for up to 3 GW more. The context that makes those seven counties worth mapping now: the utility says 32 large-load customers have committed to roughly 15,600 MW of service. The procurement is real, the counties are named, and the siting conversations have not started.
  • Six Gigawatts, Fifteen Years: PJM's one-time Reliability Backstop Procurement auction runs Sept. 30 to Oct. 21, procuring roughly 6 GW of new capacity on 15-year contracts open to gas, nuclear, clean energy, storage and uprates. The auction exists because the December capacity auction cleared at the FERC-approved cap of $333.44/MW-day for a third straight year — $16.4 billion total, of which data centers accounted for $6.5 billion, and $6.2 billion of that tied to facilities that do not exist yet. Storage is expected to have the edge on the one variable that matters in a 15-year backstop: construction time. That is the most favorable procurement environment BESS has had in this RTO, and it lands in the same month as a federal equipment order nobody can scope.
  • Twelve Days in January: Eversource, National Grid and Unitil are suing Hydro-Québec for $40 million over 12 days in January and February when the company largely halted exports on the New England Clean Energy Connect line, which had begun operating Jan. 16. The utilities say they had to backfill with dirtier, costlier power and buy replacement renewable energy credits, and that sub-zero Québec temperatures were "entirely foreseeable." Hydro-Québec says its provincial grid operator ordered the curtailment, that compensation is owed only on a voluntary halt, and that the contract permits make-up delivery later — and is countersuing for $50 million for power it did deliver and was not paid for. A transmission line that took a decade of permitting to build is now generating litigation in year one over what the contract actually promised. Worth a look at how our clients' delivery commitments read.
Project Updates
  • KSFour to Two in St. John: after a two-hour public hearing at the Annex Building in St. John, the Stafford County Zoning Board voted 4-2 to grant a conditional use permit to Lightsource bp for Zion Valley Solar and Storage — 342 MW of solar plus storage across roughly 2,400 acres, about 1,800 of them fenced, and around $300 million in capital investment. Members Jenna Zeman and Darryl Lucas voted no. Notice went to the applicant and 29 affected property owners; about 30 residents attended. A 342-MW approval decided in front of thirty people is the normal case, not the exception, and it is why the notice list is the most important document in the file.
  • MIBond It, Don't Letter It: the Comstock Township Planning Commission voted 5-1 on Sept. 10 to approve the special land use for Copenhagen Infrastructure Partners' 400-MW, 1,600-MWh Swan and Cygnet battery project on the former Hickory Ridge Golf Course in Kalamazoo County, followed by 6-0 votes that it meets the township BESS ordinance and on the site plan. Board liaison Amber Lawrence cast the lone no on the health-and-safety finding. The conditions are the story: a decommissioning bond held in trust with the township as beneficiary and re-indexed for inflation every three years, with letters of credit expressly not accepted; sound studies before, during and after construction; baseline and annual water testing; and replacement of any fire department equipment contaminated in a response. Commissioners voted down an emergency siren requirement, and a binding host agreement must close before any building permit issues. This is the most sophisticated BESS condition set we have seen a township write, and it will be copied.
  • AZFive Thousand Acres, Six to One: the Goodyear City Council approved a special use permit for AES Clean Energy's Desert Rainbow solar and storage project on roughly 5,050 acres south of Queen Creek Road and east of Waterman Wash, on a 6-0 planning and zoning recommendation. Councilmember Benita Beckles was the lone no. The package includes PV, a battery system, collector substation and at least one O&M building.
  • CAAppeal on the Fifteenth: Aypa Power takes its 400-MW Dirac BESS to the Chino City Council on Sept. 15 after the planning commission denied the conditional use permit and site approval 4-3 in July and recommended against the development agreement. The site is 13.89 acres north of a Southern California Edison substation, roughly 900 feet from Ayala Park and homes on Edison Avenue, and would be the city's first major battery facility. Chino has no BESS ordinance — the city attorney confirmed setbacks, permitted chemistries, enclosure standards and fire mitigation were all handled inside the development agreement. When the code is empty, the development agreement is the code, and a 4-3 denial means three commissioners already read it that way.
  • VAFive Hundred Seventy-One, Eight Seventy-Five: the King William County Board of Supervisors approved a 5-MW shared solar facility on about 30 acres of a 93-acre site off West Rose Garden Road for King William Solar 2 / New Energy Equity, together with a financial agreement: $50,000 up front, annual payments starting at $10,000 and escalating 10% every five years, projected at $571,875 across the initial 40-year schedule, plus $15,000 in escrow for third-party inspections. The planning commission had already found it met the county's small-scale solar standards. Note the escalator — a fixed annual payment loses to inflation and gets relitigated in year twelve; a stepped one does not.
  • INContinued, Not Decided: Jupiter Power is seeking rezonings at two Madison County sites for battery storage, and the county spent hours on fire safety before continuing the request rather than voting. County Surveyor Ken Kocinski pressed long-term lithium fire risk; Jupiter's Nat Drucker answered with the installed base — "hundreds of full-scale projects already in operation." A continuance is not a loss, but it is an invitation: the record now has an open safety question with a county official's name on it, and whoever fills that gap first owns the answer.
  • IASeven Hundred Twenty-Four Acres, Unanimous No: the Hancock County Planning & Zoning Commission voted unanimously on Sept. 9 to deny MCNI LLC's request to rezone roughly 724 acres of Concord Township from agricultural to industrial for a data center near Garner, after a nearly three-hour meeting before a standing-room crowd with about three dozen speakers, most opposed. A company representative said MCNI would likely appeal to the county Board of Adjustment.
  • NCNothing Filed, Nothing Allowed: the Nash County Board of Commissioners adopted a temporary development moratorium on data centers, data processing facilities and cryptocurrency mining in unincorporated areas, effective immediately and running to June 30, 2027 or until new zoning regulations are adopted, whichever comes first.
  • PAPostpone, Then Padlock: the Springhill Township supervisors in Fayette County tabled two ordinances regulating data centers and fossil-fuel power plants to meet state notice-posting requirements, rescheduling the votes for Sept. 17 — and in the interim passed a resolution barring anyone from applying for a data center or power plant in the township, plus a noise-and-dust ordinance. Solicitor James Tomasy said drafting started after independent power producer Nadara entered a right-to-purchase agreement with the Fay-Penn Economic Council for 800 acres near Gans. The interim bar is the move worth noting: a township that has to delay its own vote can still close the window while it waits.
  • ILAnnexed, Rezoned, Permitted: the Paxton City Council voted unanimously on Sept. 8 to approve an annexation agreement with Lightstar Renewables' Illinois subsidiary and landowners Josh and Mark Garrell for a 4.95-MW community solar project on farmland between the Colmac Coil plant and I-57 in Ford County, then voted 7-0 to rezone the 45-acre parcel to M-3 manufacturing and issue a special-use permit. On the city attorney's advice, the annexation ordinance itself was pushed to Oct. 13 to satisfy notification timelines — the same procedural care that is costing projects months elsewhere, spent up front.
On the Frontlines
  • WIOne Open House for Two Counties: the Dane County Board unanimously adopted a resolution Thursday night urging Midcontinent Grid Solutions and the Wisconsin PSC to delay consideration of the roughly 190-mile BECI 765-kV project until an independent necessity analysis, a ratepayer cost-benefit study, a comparative environmental assessment of routes, an alternatives evaluation and a prime-farmland impact analysis per route are complete. The county's specific procedural objection: a single joint open house covering both Dane and Dodge counties, with a Sept. 7 feedback deadline. The Town of Bristol passed its own routing opposition last month. Two days earlier, Rep. Derrick Van Orden published a Sept. 7 letter to MGS declaring "strong opposition" to the current routes, citing groundwater impacts in the Driftless Area and telling the company his constituents "feel like they are being pushed around." A shared open house across two counties saves a day and costs a congressman.
  • WVNo, You May Not Have More Time: the West Virginia PSC denied its own legal staff's request to toll the procedural schedule in NextEra's MidAtlantic Resiliency Link case, holding that "the circumstances presented do not justify extending the existing procedural schedule." Evidentiary hearings stay set for Oct. 26 through Nov. 2. Staff had wanted time to address route concerns, questions about need, and the effect of FERC's June large-load interconnection orders; NextEra agreed to the delay and said it might not be necessary. MARL is a 107.5-mile, 500-kV line PJM assigned in 2023, about 60 miles of it in West Virginia, with filings pending in four states and the route still not final. A commission that denies its own staff's unopposed continuance is telling intervenors exactly how much slack the calendar has.
  • TXInjunction on the Twenty-Ninth: Gillespie County and AMPYR Energy USA are back in state district court in Fredericksburg on Sept. 29 on the county's request for a temporary injunction halting the 1,100-acre Marshall Springs solar and battery project while its suit proceeds. Judge Pat Patillo already denied AMPYR's motion to dismiss in July, rejecting the argument that the county's fire and environmental claims were premature. The Fredericksburg City Council has passed a resolution against the project and the county economic development commission passed one opposing all utility-scale solar and wind. SEIA Texas director Daniel Giese is defending on HB 3824's statewide fire-safety and emergency-plan requirements — which is the right argument and is currently losing to a local ordinance fight the state law was written to preempt.
  • NYForty-Five Versus Forty: Liberty Renewables drew more than 100 people to a three-hour pre-application open house Wednesday in Olean for Hemlock Hollow Wind — 19 turbines, 95 MW, 650 feet to blade tip, across Allegany, Olean and Portville in Cattaraugus County, targeting 2028-29 construction. Attorney Jessica Klami confirmed the project is still pre-application at ORES with non-participating setbacks of twice turbine height and a 45 dBA two-hour average noise limit, looser than the 40 dBA nighttime cap the neighboring Alle-Catt project carries under Article 10 — a comparison the opposition made immediately. One resident climbed a picnic bench to demand a health survey and threatened court action; the county IDA sent its executive director to assess a possible PILOT. The Allegany portion mirrors a plan the town board rejected in 2014. When a newer siting law produces a looser standard than the project next door, that delta becomes the entire argument.
  • ILFive Minutes, Thirty Total, No Yielding: the Peoria County Zoning Board of Appeals opens its contested hearing on Repsol's Four Creeks Wind application Sept. 14, with a retired judge facilitating, sessions daily through Sept. 18 and reserve dates Sept. 28 through Oct. 1. The project spans more than 27,000 acres across Brimfield, Millbrook, Princeville and Jubilee townships at 500-plus MW. The procedure is the thing to copy or counter: five minutes per speaker, first-come sign-up, 30 minutes of public comment total per day, one comment per person, no yielding time, no cross-examination of commenters, livestreamed, and social media comments excluded from the record. That design caps the theater and forces the opposition into the written record, where it is answerable.
  • MIWrite the Rule, Pause the Queue: the Oshtemo Township Planning Commission voted unanimously to send a moratorium on new solar applications to the township board while it finishes an energy systems ordinance covering solar, storage and wind — and no member of the public spoke at the hearing. The draft storage provisions are unusually prescriptive: multi-gas detection able to identify thermal-runaway off-gases including hydrogen and carbon monoxide before smoke or flame, automatic suppression that operates on battery power during an outage, network segmentation and firewalls, hydrological analysis with annual monitoring for metals and PFAS, and an emergency response plan built with the township fire department. The commission also reinstated an alternative-site analysis requirement for projects on undeveloped land. A pause with a finished ordinance at the end of it is a better outcome than an open queue with no code, and an empty hearing room is how a township gets to write whatever it wants.
  • COFifty Megawatts at the Airport: Black Hills Energy is starting construction on a 50-MW, 200-MWh battery at its Pueblo Airport Generating Station, approved by the Colorado PUC in 2025 under the utility's 2022 Clean Energy Plan and expected to serve southern Colorado customers in late 2027. Cloudbreak Energy developed it and oversees construction; Linxon delivers balance of plant for 50 Tesla Megapack enclosures. The utility estimates roughly $30.1 million in economic benefit and files its 2026 Clean Energy Plan this fall — which is the window where the next round of sites gets named.
  • INTwenty Years, No Effect: Ball State's Center for Business and Economic Research analyzed more than 230,000 Indiana home sales from 2004 to 2024 within five miles of wind projects and four miles of solar projects and found no statistically significant negative effect on sale prices. Models that produced negative point estimates — homes within half a mile of solar at 0.9% to 1.4% lower, homes within a mile of East Central Indiana wind at 11.5% below comparables three to five miles out — did not hold up to significance testing. Researchers controlled for project size, rural versus urban siting, brownfield proximity and provider type. CBER research director Dagney Faulk built it explicitly for local officials in zoning fights, and she and Paul Niekamp present it in a Purdue Extension webinar Sept. 16. Put this one in the binder: it is Indiana-specific, it is twenty years deep, and it is the answer to the single most common objection in the room.
  • CAThirty-Two Percent of the Evening: a Kevala study commissioned by the Coalition for Community Solar Access finds California's three big IOU distribution systems could host 3,112 five-megawatt front-of-meter solar-and-storage installations serving 15,560 MW of local load, with sub-5-MW systems adding 1,976 MW — roughly 17.5 GW, or about 32% of load between 4 p.m. and 9 p.m. from June through September in 2032. SCE carries the largest absolute impact at 1,657 installations and 9,188 MW, or 37% of its expected summer peak; SDG&E is highest proportionally at 39%; PG&E at 26%. PG&E's spokesperson said the findings align with the utility's own planning work. Distribution-scale siting is a different community conversation than utility-scale — smaller footprints, more of them, closer in.
  • NATIONALNinety Days at the Same Substation: comments filed at FERC Sept. 8 show Google, Xcel, AES Indiana, Ameren, Dairyland, Advanced Energy United and SEIA backing MISO's Zero Injection Generator Interconnection Agreement, which would give 90-day reviews to zero-injection resources serving large loads at the same substation and voltage level, capped at the nameplate of the load they serve. The Sierra Club, NRDC and allies want FERC to send it back, warning it risks "operationalizing ongoing cost shifts" onto existing customers and gives ZGIA resources a permanent advantage over projects grinding through the Definitive Planning Phase. Google wants the concept extended to two-substation energy-park configurations. The filing partly answers FERC's June show-cause orders; other RTOs respond by mid-November. A 90-day interconnection path is the most valuable thing on this list — and the fight over who qualifies is only starting.
  • PAWho Gets Curtailed First: the Pennsylvania PUC voted 5-0 on Sept. 10 to adopt Vice Chair Kimberly Barrow's motion directing staff to rewrite the state's emergency electric load control regulations — the order, criteria and circumstances under which customers may be curtailed in pre-emergency and emergency conditions — and to convene a fall technical conference on cost responsibility for large computational loads. A tentative order is due at the Oct. 1 public meeting, with a final order targeted for Jan. 28, 2027, under docket M-2026-3064961. It follows Gov. Shapiro's Aug. 18 executive order and PJM's July auction identifying a 6,831-MW reliability shortfall for 2028-29. Curtailment order is about to become a negotiated term in Pennsylvania, and generation sited near large load is on both sides of that question.
  • OHCounting the Load Twice: Ohio has about 100 operating data centers and nearly 150 more announced, and AEP Ohio's February load forecast reflected signed contracts for 17.8 GW of data-center demand through 2035 — down from its 2025 estimate. The Ohio Manufacturers' Association has accused the utility of double-counting projected growth, citing a March PUCO staff filing; PJM capacity charges have risen more than tenfold since May 2025; and Consumers' Counsel Maureen Willis says nobody independently examines the prudence of state-jurisdictional supplemental transmission projects, which is why she asked FERC last month to accelerate a complaint pending since 2023. A new PUCO rule limiting cross-examination time on customer charges will make those challenges harder. PJM's Asim Haque pushed back directly, calling the claim that load growth is "not real" both naive and irresponsible. Between 2025 and 2027, added fossil generation in PJM will run 2.5 times added solar and wind — the forecast fight is the interconnection fight with a different caption.
Plays of the Week
  • Recurrent Energy (OH)Pick the Ground Nobody Defends: the Ohio Power Siting Board unanimously approved 149 MW of solar plus 149 MW of storage for Recurrent's Hamden Energy project in Vinton County — and not one party opposed it, in the state that has rejected eight utility-scale solar projects since 2020 largely on local-government objection. The play was site selection: the shuttered Sands Hill strip mine, reclaimed coal-mine land. Three moves made it hold. First, the framing put the project inside the county's own history rather than against it — developer Ali Trunzo pitched it as a chance "to harvest another one of the area's abundant resources" and support a legacy energy community. Second, when roughly 25 commenters and a June 10 local hearing surfaced one dominant concern — whether reclamation would finish before construction started — Recurrent answered it by committing in the docket to begin construction only after reclamation is complete, converting the objection into an enforceable condition instead of a reassurance. Third, the Vinton County commissioners simply declined to use their SB 52 veto, because nobody had given them a reason to. Ohio's HB 15 now makes former mine land and brownfields eligible as priority investment areas, and RMI counts more than 70,000 eligible acres. On a portfolio where three sites pencil equally, the one on disturbed ground is worth two years of calendar.
  • Clearway + Microsoft (WV)Twenty Years, Not One Check: at the West Virginia Chamber of Commerce summit, Gov. Patrick Morrisey announced a Microsoft-Clearway $13 million community commitment that recurs annually for 20 years, with $4.5 million already allocated. Three structural choices are worth copying. The money is tied to the long-term PPA under which Microsoft buys power from Clearway's Mount Storm wind farm in Grant County, so it is a contract obligation rather than a discretionary line item that a change in project ownership can quietly end. It is administered by an independent entity, the Sustain Our Future Foundation, through a named Community Energy Fund — which means the recipients deal with a grantmaker, not with a developer's community relations budget, and the announcements keep coming without the company having to generate them. And the first allocations went to workforce institutions with constituencies of their own: Eastern West Virginia Community and Technical College, Mont West CTC, West Virginia State, Bluefield State, the WV Community Development Hub, the Eastern WV Community Action Agency and the South Branch Career and Technical School serving Hardy, Grant and Pendleton counties. A one-time capital gift buys a ribbon cutting. A twenty-year instrument with a third-party administrator buys seven organizations that will show up at the next hearing without being asked.

The Texas chairman moved to remand two cases over an incomplete record and defective notice. Dane County demanded an independent study of a line that has not been filed. West Virginia's commission refused to give its own staff two more weeks. A Wilmington resident stopped a rezoning by attacking a single word in the ordinance. None of that is about whether these projects are needed, and all of it decides whether they get built. So the ask for next week is narrow: on every active file, pull the notice list, the comment deadline and the hearing procedure, and check them the way an opposing intervenor would — because on this week's evidence, that is the document that loses the case.
