RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 44 · Monday, August 24, 2026 · Version 2

Happy Monday, all.

The decisive documents this week were not permits. They were mailing lists, court filings and land-use classifications. In Texas, three administrative law judges recommended that regulators deny two segments of a $33 billion transmission plan in part because the utilities rerouted hundreds of miles of line and left roughly 1,400 newly affected landowners with no public meeting — a notice failure, not an engineering one. In Indiana and Michigan, two solar developers who lost local votes went straight to court, one of them alleging a zoning board member had been funding the opposition's yard signs. In Massachusetts, a town of 1,900 that banned batteries years ago is now spending $250,000 to appeal the state board that overrode it. The through-line for our work is uncomfortable and useful: the local vote is increasingly just the opening move, and what determines the outcome afterward is whether the record we built is clean. Notice lists, hearing transcripts, who said what and when — that is the material every one of these fights is now being decided on. Ohio, meanwhile, offered the week's one cheerful data point, and it was about dirt: the same siting board that has rejected eight solar projects on farmland since 2020 approved 280 megawatts on a shuttered strip mine without much drama at all.

Must Read

The $33 Billion Mailing List

The largest transmission buildout in Texas history is in serious trouble, and the reason should be pinned above every project manager's desk in this company. On Aug. 21, State Office of Administrative Hearings judges Linda Brite, Linda Burgess and Dee Marlo Chico issued a proposal for decision recommending that the Public Utility Commission of Texas deny Oncor's and LCRA Transmission Services' certificate applications outright for two segments of "Import Path 2" — the Bell County East-to-Big Hill run of more than 200 miles, and the Big Hill-to-Sand Lake segment from San Angelo to Pecos. Both are pieces of the Permian Basin Reliability Plan, a roughly $33 billion program of 765-kilovolt lines meant to move power across the state.

The judges found the utilities had not proven need, faulting reliance on a 2022 S&P Global oil-and-gas electrification forecast and pointing to 37 planned or announced gas generation projects totaling about 39.5 GW in the Permian, West Texas and the Panhandle that could serve the same load without the lines. That much is a conventional evidentiary loss. The part that matters to us is the second finding. Oncor and LCRA sent 2,809 invitations to public meetings held in June 2025 — and then added roughly 400 miles of route links afterward, leaving about 1,400 landowners newly in the path of a 765-kV line who were never invited to anything. The judges concluded that denial was "the only meaningful remedy remaining."

This is not an isolated stumble. Three days earlier, in an Aug. 14 filing made public that week, SOAH judges Pratibha Shenoy and Brent McCabe rejected Oncor's objections to a parallel finding on the Dinosaur Switch-to-Longshore Switch line, a 235-to-270-mile 765-kV segment running from Glen Rose west toward Forsan. Same pattern: about 2,533 public-meeting notices went out for June 2025 meetings, then roughly 1,650 additional owners within 520 feet of added or modified route links surfaced who had never received meeting notice. More than 4,200 later application notices did not cure the defect, the judges held, invoking the Fifteenth Court of Appeals' ruling in PUC v. City of Denton and noting the commission retains discretion to deny the application rather than simply extend the clock. Landowner counsel from American Stewards of Liberty argued that the customary day-for-day extension "is no longer available" as a fix.

Then the politics arrived. Less than an hour before the PUC's Aug. 21 open meeting, 31 Texas House members filed a letter asking regulators to deny every certificate application tied to the plan until the 90th Legislature can rewrite the process, arguing that HB 5066 "did not contemplate 765-kilovolt lines" and that its 180-day clock "was never intended for projects running transmission lines all across the state." The commission took no action on the Dinosaur-to-Longshore application that day, saying it would deliberate and return to it. The remaining two projects — including the Howard-Solstice line running from San Antonio through the Hill Country to Fort Stockton — are on the following Friday's agenda. Oncor says it strongly disagrees with the judges.

Read this as the transmission story of the year so far, because renewables cannot move without these corridors and because the failure mode here is entirely inside our discipline. Nobody beat this plan on cost-benefit modeling. A rural landowner coalition and a set of legislators beat it on a distribution list, and they did it by noticing that the route on the map at the June meeting was not the route in the application. Once that gap existed, the merits stopped mattering — and a bipartisan bloc of thirty-one legislators found a low-risk way to side with angry constituents without ever having to argue the engineering.

⚡ The Takeaway

Every route change after a public meeting creates a new class of people who were promised process and did not get it — and that class is now a legal strategy, not a grievance. Two practical moves for our clients. First, treat the notice list as a living document owned by someone senior: if the centerline moves, the list gets rebuilt and those landowners get a meeting, not a certified letter. The marginal cost of one extra open house is trivial against a proposal for decision recommending denial. Second, get ahead of the "we were never told" narrative publicly, because it is the single most sympathetic thing an opponent can say and it requires no technical expertise to say it. On big linear projects, the record of who was invited is now as load-bearing as the need case.

Also On Our Radar
  • Strip Mine, Clean Slate: the clearest natural experiment in solar siting we have seen all year, and it ran in a single meeting. The Ohio Power Siting Board permitted Recurrent Energy to build a 280-megawatt, 314,000-panel array on top of the shuttered Sands Hill Coal Mine in Hamden, Vinton County — the state's least populous — projecting 300 construction jobs and 11 permanent ones. At the same session the board upheld its rejection of Sloopy Solar in Clark County, setting up a likely Ohio Supreme Court appeal, and denied a complaint from Citizens Against Fountain Point against an already-permitted project. Signal Cleveland's tally is the number to carry into every Ohio conversation: the board has rejected eight industrial-scale solar projects since 2020, and every single one was on agricultural land, each time citing formal township opposition. The variable is not megawatts, developer or technology. It is what the ground used to be. A reclaimed mine has no farm-preservation constituency, no viewshed lobby and a county that already thinks of the parcel as industrial. Where a brownfield, mine scar or capped landfill is anywhere near a client's interconnection point, that alternative deserves a real engineering look before we spend two years fighting a township over cornfields.
  • See You in Court: two solar developers lost local votes this month and neither one went home. In Indiana, Hoosier Solar sued in La Porte Superior Court after the county Board of Zoning Appeals denied a special exception 3-2 for a roughly 300-acre project, alleging the board never stated its reasons and that the process was biased — specifically that a BZA member funded and distributed "No Industrial Solar Plants on Farmland" signage and still had one at his home in late April, and that a commissioner called the company "outside carpet baggers." In Michigan, Ranger Power sued Fayette Township in Hillsdale County Circuit Court after its planning commission unanimously denied a special land use permit for Heartwood Solar II, a 140-megawatt project on about 1,350 acres near Jonesville, arguing the denial was arbitrary and ignored the township's own newly adopted renewable energy ordinance. Both complaints turn on the same defect: a board that voted no without building a defensible record. That is worth understanding precisely, because it cuts both ways. It is the opening our clients' counsel will use — and it is also the thing a well-advised opposition now coaches boards to avoid, by papering the denial with findings. Expect the next generation of local no votes to come with a written rationale attached.
  • Eighteen and a Half: the largest single renewables number filed anywhere in the country this week came out of a utility resource plan, not a project. Duke Energy filed its 2026 Carolinas Resource Plan on Aug. 17 recommending 18.5 GW of solar and 13 GW of energy storage over fifteen years, alongside 14 GW of gas and about 4.5 GW of new nuclear, against a base forecast of winter peak growing more than 10 GW — roughly 30% cumulative. Duke says it has an RFP out for 400 MW of standalone storage in South Carolina and approval for a 1.4 GW combined cycle plant in Anderson County. The South Carolina commission is expected to hold a hearing in April and rule by June. Eighteen and a half gigawatts of solar in the Carolinas is not a number that gets built in industrial parks; it is a number that gets built in counties that have not yet had this argument. The window to shape those ordinances is now, before the applications land and the first township organizes.
  • Watershed Argument: a town of about 1,900 is running the most expensive local-control appeal in the region, and the framing is the reason it has legs. Oakham, Massachusetts prohibited standalone battery storage years ago; the state Energy Facilities Siting Board approved Moraga Storage's 180-megawatt project on a 42-acre former auto salvage yard anyway, with conditions, finding it "would result in public benefits." Oakham appealed to a single justice of the Supreme Judicial Court and allocated $250,000 in legal fees against a typical annual legal budget of $15,000. Note what the opposition is actually arguing. Not property values, not aesthetics — contamination of the Ware River watershed feeding the Quabbin Reservoir, which supplies Boston's drinking water. The MWRA says the site sits outside the watershed. That correction will not travel nearly as far as the claim. A local fight becomes a metropolitan fight the moment someone connects it to the water supply of two million people, and once that frame is loose, a technical rebuttal from a water authority is not a symmetric response. On any BESS site within sight of a reservoir, aquifer or municipal intake, the hydrology work needs to be done, published and independently reviewed before the first hearing — not produced defensively afterward.
  • Plug Into What's Already There: with new interconnection effectively rationed, the fastest capacity in PJM may be capacity that never needs a new connection at all. PJM is taking a second run at its surplus interconnection service rules, which let a new generator or battery connect at an existing plant's interconnection point using that asset's unused capacity rights — skipping most network upgrades. PJM's first reform went nowhere: eight applications since 2023, two approved. MISO is studying 14.8 GW of surplus requests and SPP 14.3 GW, most of it storage, and PacifiCorp had 33 projects totaling 5.2 GW under review as of Aug. 13. Berkeley researchers put PJM's theoretical surplus headroom at roughly 150 GW of solar, wind and storage. PJM staff floated a fix this month letting hybrid resources bid as separate units in energy markets while clearing capacity as one. For engagement work this is a category worth flagging early: a battery added inside an existing plant fence line is a fundamentally easier community conversation than a greenfield site, and it is about to become a much more common ask.
  • Down Round, Up Backlog: long-duration storage got its biggest check of the year and a haircut in the same transaction. Form Energy closed a $750 million Series G led by T. Rowe Price, pushing total equity past $2 billion, with Sequoia, Janus Henderson, Franklin Templeton and PEAK6 joining TPG Rise Climate, Breakthrough Energy Ventures and GE Vernova. The money scales the iron-air factory in Weirton, West Virginia. Backlog has gone from roughly 20 GWh to 80 GWh, including a 30 GWh Xcel Energy installation at a Google data center in Minnesota and a 12 GWh supply agreement with Crusoe. Axios reported the round priced at a $1.75 billion pre-money valuation, down from about $3 billion in October 2024. The valuation is the market repricing risk; the backlog is the market placing orders. Weirton is also the useful detail for our purposes — a hundred-hour storage plant in a West Virginia mill town is the rare energy-transition asset with a genuinely bipartisan local constituency, and that is a template worth studying rather than a one-off.
  • Pay to Plug In: the biggest public power provider in the South just put a price on being a data center. The TVA board approved moving data centers out of the manufacturing service rate class into a separate wholesale rate, raising their power charges roughly 10%, phased over three fiscal years for existing customers, and imposed upfront capacity commitment charges of about $1.5 million per megawatt on new developments, payable over three to five years. CEO Tom Rice framed it as reflecting "the incremental capacity that needs to be added to the grid that's not covered in the base rate." The board also adopted an integrated resource plan concluding TVA's territory needs 11 to 32 GW of additional generation. Data centers were about 20% of TVA industrial demand in early 2026. A separate rate class is the cleanest answer to the question our clients' opponents ask in every hearing — who pays for the upgrades — and once one large utility has drawn that line, the absence of a similar line elsewhere becomes an argument against the project.
Project Updates
  • VAPittsylvania Moves the Goalposts, Legally: the Board of Supervisors voted unanimously Tuesday to move its utility-scale solar siting guidelines out of the zoning ordinance and into the comprehensive plan, after new state law made the county's prior rules unlawful — a five-mile minimum separation between facilities and a 2% cap on any zoning district's acreage. County Attorney Matt Evans said the state code "prevents the county from operating as it has historically," and that housing the guidance in the comprehensive plan gives boards adopted language to base denials on "so it's not arbitrary and it's not in violation of state code." A preemption workaround worth watching, because it is portable.
  • PAA Monster in Bradford: the county planning commission got its first look at Vesper Energy's 100.5-MW Bradford Solar, an 800-acre build across 17 parcels and six landowners in Burlington, Towanda, Franklin and Monroe townships — "this one is a monster," the planning director said. The commission tabled preliminary approval but granted modifications: seven-foot wildlife-friendly fencing instead of the mandated eight, a sewage-facilities waiver, a wetland fill buffer cut from 50 feet to 25, and floodway fill contingent on DEP permits. A resident pushed for a per-project acreage cap and an archaeological survey.
  • NYBear Ridge Hits the Right-of-Way: Niagara County denied all highway work permits for Cypress Creek Renewables' Bear Ridge Solar, with the county manager warning that "should you disturb these areas, we will take enforcement actions." The county's position is that ORES has no authority over county rights-of-way; ORES separately told the developer its proposed minor modifications could proceed. The $220.6 million, 100-MW project covers 516 acres in Cambria and Pendleton. State siting preemption is a powerful tool right up to the edge of the county road, and this is where that edge gets tested.
  • ILTroy Says No, Nervously: Troy's planning commission denied a special-use permit 3-2 for Ironbark Solar, a 5-MW community project on 15.58 acres of light-industrial land, with the three no votes saying they had not had enough time to research it and two members abstaining over conflicts. City attorney guidance flagged that Illinois law has preempted local solar ordinances since 2023, so a denial without articulable grounds invites litigation. It went to City Council for first reading Aug. 17 and returns for second reading and action Aug. 31.
  • GASix Hundred Sixty in South Georgia: three utility-scale projects totaling 660 MW filed through the state's Developments of Regional Impact portal in recent days — NextEra's 200-MW Amber Meadow in Irwin County on about 1,500 acres, with 270 construction jobs and $49.2 million in PILOT payments and Georgia Power taking the output; OCI Energy's 261-MW Peach Wood in Mitchell County on roughly 2,500 acres; and EDF Renewables' 199-MW Project Lightyear in Miller County on about 1,600 acres. Nearly 90% of Georgia's largest solar sits below the Fall Line, and the AJC reports the buildout has rankled residents over tax incentives, aesthetics and soil.
  • AZCopia Files Big in the Harquahala: Copia Power asked Maricopa County to rezone roughly 3,100 acres near Tonopah for its Belmont Energy Center — about 2,860 acres of utility-scale solar plus roughly 320 acres for gas generation, battery storage and data centers, inside a planned 30,000-acre power and digital infrastructure campus an hour west of Phoenix. EQT is separately acquiring Copia, with the deal expected to close later this year. A solar-first application with data centers attached is a different political object than a data center application, and the sequencing here is deliberate.
  • IAClay County Tables All Four: supervisors again tabled temporary moratoriums covering data centers, wind, solar and battery storage at their Aug. 18 meeting in Spencer, directing their planning consultant to assemble examples from other jurisdictions for a workshop first. One supervisor noted only a small number of Iowa counties have enacted similar moratoriums. Bundling all four technologies into one moratorium is the pattern to watch — it means renewables inherit whatever the county decides about data centers.
On the Frontlines
  • OHAshtabula Draws the Map: a standing-room-only crowd in Jefferson pressed county commissioners and Apex Clean Energy on Station Wind, a 300-MW, 50-to-70-turbine project spanning much of eastern Ashtabula County. Apex is targeting 30,000 leased acres inside a roughly 300,000-acre footprint across thirteen townships and has about 12,000 acres under lease. Under SB 52, commissioners have asked each township to identify restricted areas by Sept. 1, after which the county drafts a countywide resolution and map; the board president says the county will act project-by-project until the map is final. That Sept. 1 deadline is the whole ballgame in Ohio wind right now.
  • INCopper Box, Second Strike: the Montgomery County Advisory Plan Commission voted 6-2 to send Copper Box Solar's revised rezoning petition to the commissioners with an unfavorable recommendation — the second this year. The petition covers 17 parcels and roughly 1,800 acres, enabling a system across about 2,620 acres with up to 825 acres of panels; the county logged 14 written comments in support and 46 opposed. Commissioners cited the comprehensive plan, which designates industrial solar an undesirable land use. The developer raised setbacks, added two miles of vegetative screening and lifted its tax estimate from $20 million to more than $45 million — and this time says it will not withdraw, sending it to the Board of Commissioners in late September.
  • NYForty-Five Conditions in Feura Bush: New Scotland's planning board issued its first special-use permit under the town's 2024 BESS law — a 5-MW, five-Megapack facility on a seven-acre parcel, approved after a year of review with 45 conditions attached, with two more applications pending. The supervisor defended the local law as keeping control in the planning board's hands; the board chairman accused outside opponents of "alarmist advocacy," disputing a claim of monthly battery fires. A town that wrote its own ordinance first got to approve on its own terms — the argument for engaging on ordinance drafting rather than waiting for the application.
  • NYAmsterdam Goes to Zero: the city will introduce legislation next month for an outright citywide BESS ban, recommended by an ad hoc committee of the mayor, corporation counsel, fire chief and an alderman — "the consensus of the committee was that the risks outweighed the benefits." The trigger was Carson Power's 5-MW concept on 2.3 acres, revised in May to a 12.5-MW project in the industrial park. The city's existing moratorium would be lifted for solar while the battery ban takes effect. The towns of Amsterdam, Florida and Glen have already banned BESS — a county-scale contagion pattern, and the fire chief's seat on that committee is the tell.
  • WYThe Turbines Nobody Wanted: Chevron has begun dismantling the 11-turbine, 16.5-MW Casper Wind Farm in Natrona County, with four of the 240-foot turbines already sold and shipped. A commissioner says the project was never fully operational because Chevron never secured a purchase agreement with Rocky Mountain Power, running the turbines only long enough each year to keep the permit alive and delivering "absolutely nothing" to the county or ratepayers; a former commissioner who voted to approve it in 2009 says he regrets the vote. Removal runs through end of 2026, reclamation into 2027, remediation possibly to 2029. This is the single most damaging story type in our sector, and it is entirely preventable: a stranded permit is a seventeen-year argument against the next project in that county.
  • AZDeath by a Thousand Cuts: Arizona's solar industry is absorbing sustained legislative and regulatory pressure. Three bills this session — one classifying utility-scale solar within four miles of a residence as a public nuisance, one requiring utilities to draw 85% of generation from "reliable sources" excluding wind and solar, and one barring the State Land Department from using solar score maps — all failed, and drafts of the land department's continuation bill included a solar and wind moratorium on state land through 2029. But one of the sponsors unseated a sitting Corporation Commissioner in the July 21 primary, and on Aug. 12 the commission approved APS and UNS requests to cut rooftop solar export rates 10%. AriSEIA says member ranks are down 25 to 30% in two years.
  • TXOne Hundred Ordinances and Counting: local Texas governments have considered at least 100 ordinances since July 2025 covering data centers — and battery storage and new solar are routinely swept into the same instruments. Austin County adopted a countywide moratorium in July covering AI data centers and battery storage; Mesquite built a framework for both; Milam County's push for stricter statewide standards explicitly names solar plants; El Paso now requires a special use permit and 300-foot residential buffers. The counter-tactic is on the record too: Hill County rescinded its one-year moratorium after a developer sued for more than $100 million, and Tom Green County dropped its own moratorium plan rather than find out.
  • KSReroute Instead of Curtail: FERC on Aug. 19 approved SPP's plan to manage congestion by topology optimization — opening and closing circuit breakers to redirect flow rather than redispatching generation — across its 17-state footprint. A NewGrid, SPP and Brattle study found historical operating-limit violations could be eliminated for 75% of constraints analyzed, worth $18 million to $44 million a year against SPP congestion costs averaging $1.6 billion annually. The number that matters to renewables: SPP curtailed an average of 1,382 MW of wind and solar throughout 2025. MISO's version has saved $95 million so far this year, and ERCOT plans its own early next year.
  • WANine Thousand and a Caveat: the Northwest Power and Conservation Council's draft Ninth Power Plan calls for 9,000 MW of renewables, 5,000 MW of stored energy and 1,200 MW of new natural gas by 2032, plus 1,060 MW of conservation and 590 MW of demand response across Washington, Oregon, Idaho and Montana. The gas line drew the fight: a Climate Solutions director argued "the biggest reason we'll see new natural gas is if we slow-walk the building of wind, solar and storage," while a Washington PUD Association official backed it. The plan itself warns that renewable projects will need significant land and may face public opposition. Comment runs through Oct. 16 — a live opportunity to put siting realism into a document utilities will cite for a decade.
  • VASome of It Comes Back: Dominion Energy says it has sought and begun receiving refunds on tariffs paid building the Coastal Virginia Offshore Wind project, after previously reporting that tariffs added nearly $800 million to CVOW. An analyst estimate puts the recoverable portion at up to $20 million — the money collected under the regime the Supreme Court struck down in February — while the steel and aluminum tariffs, the largest share, stay in federal coffers and on ratepayers. The House Speaker says any refund "should aver back to ratepayers"; Senate leaders want it routed through the general fund. A useful reminder that the cost story on offshore wind now has a live political fight attached to its smallest line item.
  • PABring Your Own Power: Gov. Shapiro signed an executive order Aug. 18 giving preferential DEP permitting to data centers over 25 MW that sign a consent order agreeing to pay all interconnection and service costs their load causes and to source power from new generation in the same PJM zone, with firm clean energy requirements ramping from 10% in January to 32% by 2035. Non-signers wait until every local permit and water authorization is in hand. The order also pulled existing data center projects out of the state's permit fast-track program and barred confidential agreements with developers. For renewables developers in PJM this is the most favorable state-level demand signal of the month — new load is being told to bring new generation with it.
  • USEighty-One and Counting: the National League of Cities launched a Data Center Local Action Tracker on Aug. 19, an interactive database built from ordinances, resolutions, planning documents, meeting records and development agreements, sorted into planning and regulatory actions, project approvals and denials, community engagement and economic policy, with filters for energy standards and civil opposition. At least 81 cities and counties now have data center moratoria, and a Gallup poll cited alongside the launch found seven in ten Americans oppose data centers being built in their area. Assume opposition counsel in our hearings has this open in a browser tab by next month, and assume the ordinance language travels faster now than it did last year.

The week's lesson is procedural and it is expensive. A $33 billion transmission plan is on the ropes over a mailing list; two developers are in court because boards voted no without writing down why; a town of 1,900 is betting $250,000 that a state override can be undone. None of that is about whether these projects are good. It is about whether the record is clean. Build the record like it is going to be read aloud in front of a judge, because increasingly it is.
