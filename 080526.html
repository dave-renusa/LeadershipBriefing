Good morning, team.

For two years the leverage math in Illinois ran one direction: meet the statewide solar standards, and a county that said no could be dragged into court and ordered to issue the permit anyway. Tazewell County spent those years, in its own words, "reluctantly" approving projects it didn't want because the threat of a mandamus suit made no look expensive. Last week that math flipped. On the strength of a recent Fourth District appellate ruling that counties do keep discretion over solar siting, the Tazewell board voted 17-2 to reject a 5-megawatt community-solar farm in Mackinaw Township and, in the same meeting, rewrote its whole renewable ordinance. That's today's Deep Dive, and it matters far past one 30-acre parcel: the ruling covers 41 Illinois counties, and it changes how our clients win — or lose — every solar room in that footprint.

Hold that against the number that keeps the whole business honest. Lazard's new levelized-cost report, out last week, still puts renewables at the cheapest new-build generation on the grid — and in the same week PJM's capacity auction missed its reserve margin for the first time in the market's history, a flashing light that supply is tight and bills are headed up. Cheap generation that the local process won't let get built is the paradox our clients live inside, and it's the through-line of this issue: the constraint isn't the technology or the economics anymore. It's the room. Radar has the market receipts, Project Updates has California weighing ten thousand acres of new solar and the Texas 765-kV fight boiling over, and the standing items are below so we don't relitigate them.

Must Read

Discretion Advised

The most important thing that happened to Illinois solar last week was not a vote count. It was a sentence in a courtroom that changed what a county board is allowed to do. For our clients building utility-scale and community solar in the state, that sentence just reset the entire negotiating table — and Tazewell County was the first board to sit down at the new one and use it.

Start with the vote, because it's a clean illustration of the shift. Tuesday the Tazewell County Board rejected, 17-2, a special-use request from Sol Source Power to build a 5-megawatt community-solar farm on 30 acres of a nearly 50-acre parcel at Lowery and Gresham roads in Mackinaw Township, an agriculture-preservation district. Only Mark Goddard and Kaden Nelms voted yes; Nick Graff voted present. The county's Zoning Board of Appeals and its land-use committee had each already recommended denial unanimously the month before — the land-use committee after meeting past midnight — which the committee's own chair, Russ Crawford, flagged as unusual: "The ZBA doesn't usually do that." Board member Jon Hopkins didn't hedge: "If there was a picture in the dictionary of what is a terrible place for a solar project, this development is that." And when Sol Source sent the board a letter implying it would sue if the permit were denied, Hopkins turned it into the night's rallying cry — "That letter was a threat to us … You don't threaten public officials about a vote." A threat letter that would once have been a credible lever landed, this time, as an insult. That is the tell that the ground has moved.

Here's what moved it. As WCBU reported, the Illinois Fourth District Appellate Court in Springfield — which covers 41 counties, Tazewell among them — recently sided with a county that a developer had sued for denying a solar project, holding that state law "did not prohibit the county from exercising discretion in determining whether to issue permits," and that counties may apply "their traditional, subjective standards used when making local zoning decisions." Translation: meeting the statewide siting checklist is no longer a golden ticket. A board can weigh location, neighbors, watershed, and fit — and a court will not force its hand with a writ of mandamus. That is the exact opposite of the regime that had Tazewell "reluctantly" approving projects "over the past few years because of a threat of litigation if the project met all state standards." The stick that developers had been carrying into these rooms was just taken away, at least in the Fourth District.

And Tazewell didn't stop at one denial. The same meeting adopted a rebuilt renewable framework that our Illinois clients will be living under: a single consolidated ordinance folding commercial solar, wind, and energy storage into one section of the code — strengthening permit and inspection requirements across a project's whole life cycle, requiring developers to file emergency-response plans and coordinate with local responders before a facility can operate, and raising the financial guarantees for decommissioning abandoned sites. A companion amendment lets the county demand higher insurance for larger projects; risk manager Tim Neuhauser walked through why, naming watershed impacts, tornado-debris damage, fire, and herbicide overspray drifting onto a neighbor's crop as the kind of "environmental claims" a pollution policy has to cover. Read together, the denial and the ordinance are one move: the county said no to this project and simultaneously raised the bar for the next one.

One caution before anyone treats this as settled law. The Fourth District's discretion holding runs against a contrary line from the Third Appellate District, which has upheld orders forcing counties to issue permits where a project met the statewide standards. A split like that is the classic setup for the Illinois Supreme Court to take the question and decide it for the whole state. Until it does, the uncomfortable truth is that a solar developer's leverage in Illinois now depends on which appellate district the parcel sits in.

The Takeaway

In 41 Illinois counties, the permit is no longer a checklist you clear — it's a decision you have to win, on the local record, in the room. Three instructions follow. One: retire the litigation-threat letter. It was already a weak play; in the Fourth District it is now a self-inflicted wound. Sol Source's letter didn't intimidate the Tazewell board — it gave the board's most skeptical member his closing argument and turned a zoning vote into a matter of principle. The message a developer wants in a board's inbox before a vote is a decommissioning bond and an emergency-response plan, not a warning. Two: build the record the county now gets to weigh. Discretion means the board can lean on location, drainage, fire, viewshed, and neighbor testimony — so the project that wins is the one that has answered each of those in writing, with the host and the fire chief at the table, long before the hearing. The engagement that used to be optional insurance is now the whole case. Three: bring the ordinance to the table before the county writes it against you. Tazewell just wrote higher decommissioning guarantees, lifecycle inspections, and responder coordination into its code; the developer who shows up already offering those terms isn't conceding ground, they're defining "a good project" before the county defines it for them. And watch the district line — until Springfield or the state Supreme Court resolves the split, the same project can be a right in one Illinois county and a favor to be earned in the next.

Also On Our Radar
  • Cheapest in the Room: the number that answers every board that calls solar a boondoggle. Lazard's newly released 19th Levelized Cost of Energy+ report finds that even with costs rising across every generation technology, renewables remain the most cost-competitive form of new-build generation on an unsubsidized basis and will make up the majority of near-term U.S. capacity additions. The asterisk our storage clients need to carry: Lazard shows standalone battery-storage costs actually ticking up this year, reversing prior declines, as tariffs on lithium-ion imports curtail the low-cost Chinese cell supply that used to drive prices down. So the pitch is intact — renewables are still the cheapest electrons on offer — but the storage side of it now comes with a supply-chain caveat worth saying out loud before a skeptic says it for you.
  • Made in America, Eventually: the same tariff story, one link up the chain. pv magazine flags an emerging domestic-steel bottleneck — surging prices, strict domestic-content rules, and a fragmented scrap-recycling market — that threatens scheduling and compliance delays for utility-scale projects just as the U.S. heads into a record build year. The offsetting data point: median U.S. solar module assembly pricing held steady at $0.30 per watt as Chinese manufacturers divested American assets and domestic producers moved against imports. For our client conversations the lesson is timeline honesty: modules are stable, but steel and cells are where a 2027 in-service date quietly slips, so bake the buffer in and say why.
  • Short by Seven Thousand: the grid-stress signal our whole affordability argument runs on. In its auction for 2028/29 capacity, PJM — which keeps the lights on for 67 million people from the Mid-Atlantic to Chicago — fell short of its required reserve margin for the first time in the market's history, hitting the $329/MW-day price cap and still missing the target by 6,831 MW; PJM estimated it would have cleared near $555/MW-day uncapped, and is now proposing a one-time Reliability Backstop Procurement to fill the gap. Rising capacity prices are the clearest proof a power system is structurally strained — which is exactly the pressure that new local generation relieves. When a client's project gets framed as a favor to the developer, this is the counter: the region is literally short on supply, and their project is part of the fix.
  • Sue for All: the week's one federal thread, and it's about money owed to communities. A coalition of clean-energy nonprofits — Growth Opportunity Partners, Inclusive Prosperity Capital, and the Nebraska-based Center for Rural Affairs — is suing the EPA in the U.S. Court of Federal Claims to recover the full value of their terminated Solar for All grants, arguing the $7 billion program's cancellation broke contracts on obligated funds. They join Colorado and 22 other states, plus Virginia's Department of Energy, already in court. The sharpest new detail: in a related DOE case, the administration acknowledged in a court filing that it canceled $7.6 billion in clean-energy grants "based solely on the political identity" of recipients' states — 16 states that voted for Harris. Keep the temperature low with clients, but know the funding backdrop for low-income and community solar is now a litigation question, not a settled line item.

  • CATen Thousand Acres, Maybe: after more than a decade of study, Santa Barbara County is one Board of Supervisors vote (Sept. 1) away from allowing utility-scale solar of 30 acres or more on non-residential land countywide — a county where the only utility-scale array today is a single 327-acre project in remote Cuyama. The live fights are the ones our clients know cold: a tentative 10,000-acre cap that two supervisors want lifted and one wants held as a "sensible starting point," and how to handle stand-alone storage in the shadow of the 2025 Moss Landing fire, with the county eyeing quarter-acre limits in non-residential zones. Two data points worth stealing for a hostile room: the Electric Power Research Institute finds the global BESS failure rate dropped 99% from 2018 to 2025, and U.S. operators installed a record 3.3 GW of storage in Q1 alone. This is a rare expansion story — carry it as proof that the pro-build case can win a coastal, ag-heavy county, if the safety and acreage questions get answered head-on.
  • CATaxed by Ballot: Solano County supervisors placed Measure E on the November ballot, a proposal that would raise the county's commercial wind-turbine fee for the first time since 1994 and create new levies on solar, battery storage, natural gas, and data centers — roughly $353,000 a year for the general fund. The framing was "a tax to ward off data centers," but read the fine print: solar and BESS get swept into the same instrument as the server halls. It's the pattern we keep naming, now in tax form rather than moratorium form — a blunt tool aimed at one land use that lands on renewables riding alongside it. Worth flagging for any client with a Solano pipeline, and a reminder that "the data-center backlash" increasingly arrives with a renewables surcharge attached.
  • TXHalt and Cross-Examine: update to Monday's Must Read — the $33B, 765-kV Permian Basin plan didn't just draw a crowd, it moved the state's leadership. Days after the 15-hour Senate hearing, Senate Business & Commerce chair Charles Schwertner and Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick formally called on the PUC to reject the current transmission applications and to overhaul the 2023 routing process when the Legislature returns in 2027, with Patrick calling the approval process "broken." Oncor and the Association of Electric Companies of Texas are pushing to proceed, warning of grid reliability if the lines slip. The new information for our files isn't the opposition — we had that Monday — it's that the state's two most powerful Republicans have now sided with the landowners against their own grid operator's plan. When leadership breaks that way on a linear project, every gen-tie and interconnection route in the state inherits a more skeptical PUC.
