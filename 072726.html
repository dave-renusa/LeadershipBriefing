Start the week with the number that ought to frame every host-community conversation we have: in the first quarter, solar and storage together were 91% of all new capacity added to the U.S. grid, the highest combined share ever recorded, and in May solar out-generated coal nationally for the first time in history. Our clients aren't building a niche anymore; they're building the grid. That's Radar below. Hold it in one hand.

A new NYU review of the wind docket, out Friday, lays out how much of the sector is currently alive only because a court said so — an offshore permitting freeze vacated, tax-credit rules restored, stop-work orders enjoined, and a brand-new fight over a Pentagon review pause that has stalled onshore wind. That's our Must Read, and it's a renewables anchor with a hard operational lesson: the projects staying upright are the ones with a state AG, a developer, and a coalition all leaning on the same wall. Meanwhile the local pause wave keeps rolling — this week it swept through data-center dockets in Spokane County (WA), Coffee County (TN), Madison County (FL), Sunbury (OH), Posey County (IN) and Detroit, per the Developer Dispatch roundup — and where it rolls, it keeps writing storage and solar into language drafted for server halls. The through-line of the whole issue: renewables are winning the grid and losing the zoning room at the same time, and the gap between those two facts is exactly where we work.

Must Read

Which Way the Wind

If you want to understand why any onshore or offshore wind project in our footprint is still standing this summer, the honest answer is: because plaintiffs keep winning in court. A Friday review from the State Energy & Environmental Impact Center at NYU Law walks the full docket, and the pattern is unambiguous — the sector is being kept alive case by case, and the wins are stacking up on the developers' side.

The tally so far: a January 2025 executive order freezing all offshore wind permitting was vacated by a Massachusetts federal court in December, and the government dropped its appeal in June, leaving the win in place. A separate Interior order pausing offshore leases drew a preliminary injunction. Two BOEM stop-work orders against Revolution Wind off Rhode Island were enjoined, with the bar running through December. On the money side, a July 2025 order that yanked the standard method most solar and wind projects use to qualify for federal tax credits was struck down in June, restoring the eligibility that makes early-stage projects financeable. And a claim that federal agencies are "systematically" disfavoring wind and solar drew its own injunction in April. Courts have, in short, been the renewables sector's most reliable ally.

But the newest front is the one that should have our onshore developers' attention. In late May, environmental groups sued the Department of Defense in Oregon federal court (Renewable Northwest v. Hegseth) over what they call an unlawful stall of DOD's mandatory reviews of onshore wind — a bureaucratic pause that functions as a de facto freeze on new turbines. On July 16, nineteen states moved to intervene on the plaintiffs' side. That case, and a parallel fight over Interior's deals to cancel and refund offshore leases (challenged by New York and six other states, with California and eight East Coast states filing notices of intent to sue), are both early. Their outcomes will set the terms for onshore wind siting in the states where our clients actually plant turbines. The companion piece the same day — Steven Weissman's "Renewable Energy Policy After the Federal Retreat" — makes the corollary explicit: with Washington in retreat, the defense of these projects has migrated to statehouses, state AGs, and the developers themselves.

The Takeaway

The projects surviving this cycle share a roster, not a legal theory. In every win above, a wind developer, a coalition of state attorneys general, and (usually) an environmental intervenor were pushing on the same case at the same time. The federal government isn't defending these permits anymore — which means the developer is the defense, and the states are the cavalry. For our clients that's a community-engagement instruction as much as a legal one: the relationship you want with a state AG's office, a governor's energy office, and a local coalition is not something you build in the week a stop-work order lands. It's something you build in the eighteen months before, the same way we tell them to build the room before the rezoning. Wind is winning where it is organized and losing where it is alone. Map your allies now, while the sky is clear, because the next order is coming and the only question is who's already standing next to you when it does.

Also On Our Radar
  • Ninety-One and Climbing: while the zoning fights grab the headlines, the build is quietly running away with the grid. New SEIA data, compiled by pv magazine on July 22, shows solar and storage were 91% of all new U.S. generation capacity in Q1 — a record combined share — and that in May, monthly solar generation surpassed coal for the first time in U.S. history, with solar now supplying about 13% of national electricity. The reliability story is the part worth stealing for a host-community deck: on July 9, California's battery fleet discharged a record 12.99 GW, covering 36% of regional demand at the post-sunset peak, and ERCOT, SPP, MISO, ISO-NE and PJM all set new solar records that landed on the same days as their record loads. The underlying SEIA figures answer the "can we count on it in a heat wave" question directly: this summer, the grid already did. When a board treats solar-plus-storage as a hobbyist add-on, this is the counter — it's the resource that kept the lights on in July.
  • Peak Condition: a sharp new front in the storage fight opened in New Jersey, and it flips the usual script. Rather than opposing a battery, an environmental-justice coalition is demanding one. The PEAK Coalition has filed comments urging the NJ DEP to make at least 65 MW / 780 MWh of battery storage a condition of renewing the Title V permit for the Bayonne Energy Center gas peaker, arguing the state's Environmental Justice Law obligates it — a battery that size, the coalition says, would displace 81% of one turbine unit's operating events. The plant's operator is already pursuing a smaller 50 MW / 200 MWh addition of its own, and Governor Sherrill's January orders directing up to 750 MW of new storage set the backdrop. The tactic to file away: storage as the remedy a community asks for, not the hazard it fears. In the right frame — cleaner air, fewer peaker starts, a permit lever already on the table — a battery becomes the thing neighbors demand. That reframing is a product we should be selling.
Project Updates

Editor's note: under our seven-day freshness rule, an item runs only when the proceeding traces to a dated, in-window page. In-footprint local activity this week was overwhelmingly the data-center pause wave; the verified renewable dockets that surfaced were thinner, so the four below pair the week's live storage and solar proceedings with the two clearest data-center actions in our states — and the biggest renewable developments are carried up in Must Read and Radar. We hold data-center items at or below the renewables count by design.

  • TXHill Country Hold: the storage-and-server sweep reaches the Texas Hill Country this morning. Austin County commissioners vote today on a moratorium that would bar county officers — including the fire marshal — from approving any permit or review for a new BESS or data center until a "comprehensive health and safety review" is complete, with injunctive relief available against anyone who starts building without it. It follows a July 13 hearing that drew more than 100 residents, and the framing is pure thermal-runaway fear: batteries lumped in with hyperscalers under a public-health banner. This is the seam we keep working — a storage project here would be frozen not for anything about storage, but for a data-center anxiety it didn't create. When the ordinance gets drafted, the play is to force the board to say out loud why a battery is a fire risk a substation isn't.
  • VACapped and Cornered: Henry County's supervisors act Tuesday on a move that could put the county on a collision course with Richmond — lowering its solar-acreage cap from 2,445 acres to exactly 1,754, the precise total it has already approved, which functions as a ceiling on any new project. The timing is the story: Virginia's new "ban the bans" law (effective July 1) bars outright solar prohibitions and requires localities to report every denial to the state. Sen. Schuyler VanValkenburg, who sponsored it, warned that caps flouting "the spirit of the law" could trigger a "heavier hand" from the General Assembly, with those denial reports as the trigger. The same Cardinal piece flags Culpeper County's recent vote to bar battery storage from agricultural land — over farmers who wanted the lease income — as the storage version of the same instinct. Watch Henry as the test case: it's the first county to answer the new statute by tightening rather than loosening, and the state is watching the reports.
  • TNZoned Out: Coffee County commissioners passed a zoning amendment 16-0 on July 20 restricting future data centers to the county's industrial district and closing a loophole that could have let them into agricultural zones. It's the tidy, defensible version of the pause — not a blanket freeze but a channelization, steering large load to the one district built for it. The reason it matters to our renewable clients: the same zoning-district logic is exactly what gets extended to solar and storage next, and a county that has just drawn a hard "industrial only" line for one land use has a template ready to reuse. Be in the room when the ordinance gets written, not after it's been copied onto the next technology.
  • FLMadison Hits Pause: outside our core geography but on-trend, Madison County commissioners voted unanimously on July 22 for a one-year moratorium on large-scale data centers, buying time for the planning board and staff to write rules before any application lands. We flag it because it's a clean example of the model spreading fastest right now — the no-project-pending, buy-time-to-draft pause — and because the ordinance a county writes during that year is where its permanent posture on all large load, renewables included, actually gets set. The moratorium is the headline; the drafting is the project.

One transmission dispatch that moved is below; Frontlines returns to full strength as the boards come back off summer recess.

  • VAAshburn Asks for a Timeout: the wires story that all this renewable and data-center load eventually rides on took a turn in Loudoun County, where the county has asked the State Corporation Commission to suspend its final order approving a Dominion Energy transmission line through Ashburn and Sterling, citing "meaningful progress" in settlement talks over the route. A joint Board of Supervisors and school board meeting on route selection is set for July 29, per the Developer Dispatch roundup. It's the update to a standing item — Loudoun's monopole fight — and it's instructive: an approved line is not a built line, and a county that engages hard at the corridor stage can pull a "final" order back to the table. Transmission is the renewables story most developers still forget to staff. No wires, no interconnection, no project — and the fight over the wires runs years past the vote that supposedly ended it.

Renewables are winning the grid and losing the zoning room in the same week. Mind the gap — that's the work.
