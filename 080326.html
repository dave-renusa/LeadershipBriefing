Monday morning, team.

The fight moved to the wires this week, and it moved in two states at once. In Central Virginia and the Texas Hill Country, the highest-voltage transmission lines either state has ever tried to build ran into the fiercest, most organized, and — the part worth underlining — most bipartisan landowner revolts we've tracked all summer. Tuesday, roughly a full elementary-school cafeteria of Louisa County, Virginia residents turned out to fight the 765-kV Valley Link line; Wednesday, Burnet County, Texas hauled its opposition to a fifteen-hour Senate hearing at the Capitol. That's the Must Read, and it's the update to our standing Texas STEP item — the $33B Permian plan is no longer a quiet docket, it's a floor fight.

Hold that in one hand. In the other, hold the reminder that our clients' product keeps winning where the politics let it. Virginia spent Thursday tripling its shared-solar program — a bipartisan bill, signed by the governor, that puts community solar on farmers' back-forties and cuts subscriber bills ~10%. And out in Oregon's grass-seed country, a county board voted to write solar off its farmland — over the objection of the farmer who wants the lease. Both are in Radar. The through-line of the issue: the grievance driving all of it, from a 765-kV tower to a 20-acre array, is "why should we carry this so someone else gets the power?" — and answering that question is the whole job.

Standing items, so we don't relitigate them. Illinois's Tazewell County consolidated renewable ordinance and the Mackinaw solar denial went to the full board July 29; as of this morning no verifiable post-vote report had posted, so we hold the outcome rather than guess. Henry County, Virginia's solar cap-at-the-approved-total: no update — Richmond's reaction is the thing to watch, and it hasn't come. Must Read

Line in the Sand

If you want to know where the renewable build actually lives or dies over the next decade, stop watching the panels and watch the wires. Nothing our clients build reaches a customer without transmission, and this week the two largest transmission projects in Virginia and Texas — both 765-kV, the highest voltage either state runs — took the kind of organized, cross-partisan beating that usually only lands on a data center. The lesson isn't that these particular lines are doomed. It's that the argument being used to fight them is the same one aimed at every linear project, including the gen-tie and interconnection lines our clients' solar and wind farms depend on, and it is winning rooms.

Start in Virginia. Tuesday night, residents from Louisa and eight surrounding counties packed Moss-Nuckols Elementary to fight Valley Link — a roughly 115-mile, 765-kV line from Campbell County to Culpeper, a Dominion-led joint venture with towers 135 to 165 feet tall and a price tag near $1 billion, spread across PJM's 13-state ratepayer footprint. Nine county governments are coordinating; Louisa alone has dedicated $250,000 to legal fees, matching Goochland and Buckingham, and has fired nearly thirty questions at the developer — including, pointedly, why Louisa residents should pay for data centers in Northern Virginia. Board Chairman Duane Adams put the whole opposition thesis in one line: "If you need power in Northern Virginia for data centers … build your generation source near where you need it — don't build it 115 miles away." A Red Ribbon Campaign is going up on fenceposts, a Democratic congressional candidate showed up to say he's "in the fight to defeat it," and organizers keep stressing the movement is conservative, liberal, and independent all at once. The State Corporation Commission holds its public hearing this fall.

Now Texas, where the same story runs bigger and older. Wednesday, Burnet County residents — dozens in matching yellow to mark themselves as opponents — waited up to ten hours to testify at a fifteen-hour Senate Committee on Business and Commerce hearing on the state's $33 billion Permian Basin plan: three 765-kV lines, 18-story steel towers every thousand feet, 200-to-300-foot easements cut across generational ranch land. Here's the detail that ought to reframe how we brief this. Per ERCOT's own study, the West Texas load driving the whole $33B build is 59% cryptocurrency, 22% "green hydrogen," 13% other commercial and industrial, and just 6% data centers — Permian demand is projected to jump from 11 GW today to 27 GW by 2038. The PUC chairman told senators flatly, "I do not believe we have an option of doing nothing … we have to get electricity to the Permian customers." The county's own state representative answered him in a headline: "Save the Hill Country; pause the plan." Legislators want the whole thing held until the 2027 session; the PUC is expected to decide by September.

Two 765-kV revolts, two states, one week — and notice what they share. Neither is really an argument about electromagnetic fields or tower height, though both got aired. Both are arguments about who pays and who benefits: rural landowners asked to surrender easements, viewsheds, and generational property so that load somewhere else — data centers, crypto miners, hydrogen plants — gets its power. That framing is bipartisan because it isn't ideological; it's fairness. And it is the exact framing that gets pointed at a solar farm's collector line or a wind project's interconnection route the moment those cross a neighbor's ground.

The Takeaway

The cost-and-benefit question is the whole fight now, and it lands years before the vote that supposedly settles it. Three instructions follow. One: staff the corridor, not just the parcel. Both of these fights were lost in the routing and study phase, not at a final hearing — Burnet County has been organizing since it got its notice in May 2025, and by the time a project reaches the SCC or the PUC the opposition has a war chest, a color to wear, and a theme song. The engagement clock on any line — including our clients' interconnection routes — starts the day a route is even rumored, not the day it's filed. Two: answer "why should we pay for someone else's load" before it's asked. The developers who lose these rooms are the ones who let opponents define the project as an extraction — power taken from here to enrich there. The counter a solar-plus-storage client actually has is real: locally sited generation relieves the exact grid pressure driving these lines, so the project isn't the tax, it's the alternative to one. Say it in those words, early. Three: the messenger matters more than the map. Adams and the Texas ranchers are winning because they speak as landowners defending land, not as opponents of energy. When our clients need a corridor, the voice that carries is the host — the farmer taking the lease, the county that keeps the tax base — not the developer's engineer. Build that roster while the sky is clear, because in Virginia and Texas this week, the side that was already organized is the side that's winning.

Also On Our Radar
  • Shared and Shared Alike: the affordability story our decks keep asking for, now written into law. Thursday at Apex Clean Energy's Charlottesville headquarters, Virginia leaders marked a major expansion of the commonwealth's shared-solar program, following Gov. Spanberger's signing of bipartisan bills sponsored by Sen. Scott Surovell and Del. Rip Sullivan. The Dominion program more than doubles — from a fully-subscribed 200 MW across 52 projects to 525 MW of new community-solar capacity, with a carve-out for low-income subscribers — and Appalachian Power's oversubscribed 50 MW program gets another 50 MW now and 50 more by 2028. Subscribers already save roughly 10%, about $175 a year, without a panel on their roof, and an Aurora Energy Research analysis pegs the expansion's benefit at more than $2.5 billion over 25 years — even for ratepayers who never sign up, explicitly framed as one of the few levers available to push back on PJM-driven bill pressure. Two things to carry. First, "a farmer's back 40 or a warehouse roof" is the siting footprint here — small, distributed, and politically far easier than the utility-scale fight. Second, this is the affirmative answer to the fairness argument in our Must Read: shared solar is generation that lowers the bill of the person hosting it. That's a product story, and Virginia just tripled the market for it.
  • The Back Forty Wants In: the farmland fight, with the twist boards never expect — the farmer is on the developer's side. The Linn County, Oregon commissioners voted 2-1 on July 28 to send a letter opposing the ~1,600-acre Muddy Creek Energy Park, a solar-plus-storage project on Willamette Valley farmland, citing "the precedent and policy" of siting on high-value ground. But the grower whose land it is, John Langdon, wants the lease: he says only 850–900 acres would actually be developed, that the solar revenue would bring 40 years of financial security and five-to-ten times the return of ryegrass on his worst ground, and that agrivoltaics — grazing and cropping between the rows — lets him keep farming. The organized opposition, Friends of Gap Road, has asked the county to ban solar on all its farmland; the commissioners have so far declined to go that far. The lesson for our siting playbook is the inversion: when the fight is "save the farmland," the developer's single best messenger is the host farmer describing solar as the thing that keeps the farm in the family. A token pollinator strip won't carry a room; a farmer explaining his succession plan might.
Project Updates

Editor's note: under our seven-day freshness rule, an item runs only when the proceeding traces to a dated, in-window page. This week's in-footprint local docket was, once again, overwhelmingly the data-center pause wave — but the tell we keep flagging is that these pauses sweep battery storage and solar into ordinances written about server halls, which is exactly our seam. The four below, drawn from the Bantam Developer Dispatch's July 27–31 roundup, are balanced so the storage/solar-exposed proceedings are not outnumbered by data-center-only ones. Several in-geography renewable votes that surfaced in search were checked and dropped for falling outside the window — a Dearborn County, IN solar-and-BESS moratorium (Feb.) and an Eastland County, TX anti-solar resolution (July 13) among them.

  • ORHillsboro Freezes the Batteries Too: the city council unanimously adopted a four-month moratorium on new data centers and related battery-storage systems, called with a single day's notice to head off a rush of applications. Hillsboro already hosts 436 acres of data centers with 50 more under construction, and now has 120 days to weigh permanent limits. Read the drafting language when it posts: a storage project here would be frozen not for anything about storage, but because it got named in the same breath as a server hall. That's the fight to get in front of before the ordinance is written.
  • PARansom Bets on Storage: a proposed 750-megawatt, 930-acre campus from Mudiita Infra would rely on battery storage and solar rather than gas generation — a rare case of large load arriving with a renewable power plan attached. Ransom Township previously rejected a zoning-overlay request for a different project over incomplete information, so the reception is far from settled. Worth watching as a template: when the load itself is packaged with storage and solar, the host-community conversation starts on very different ground than a gas-fired campus.
  • WYPark County Pre-empts: commissioners are proposing a special-use-permit requirement for data centers and other heavy-load electrical users as part of the county's first land-use overhaul in more than a decade — with no project currently proposed and residents described as "100% no." The instrument matters to us because "heavy-load electrical users" is a category that can quietly capture utility-scale storage. When a county writes a permit trigger this broad, the renewable exposure is in the definitions, and the time to shape them is now.
  • INDecatur Draws the Line: the county's Democratic committee is calling for a temporary data-center moratorium and more transparency around discussions in Greensburg while the comprehensive plan is updated. It's the now-familiar sequence — pause first, rewrite the plan second — and Indiana counties have been copying it from one another all summer. The comprehensive-plan rewrite is where the county's posture on all large land uses, renewables included, actually gets set; the moratorium is just the headline.
On the Frontlines

Light by the freshness rule this Monday — most in-geography contested renewable hearings fell outside the seven-day window, and the week's biggest fights are carried up in Must Read (the twin 765-kV revolts) and Radar (Virginia's shared-solar expansion and the Oregon farmland fight). The one contested proceeding that landed with a storage stake attached is below.

  • VALoudoun Kills the Campus, Storage and All: the Loudoun County Planning Commission rejected the proposed 3.25-million-square-foot Spring Valley Technology Park — twelve data-center buildings, three substations, and utility-scale energy storage across roughly 325 acres — while the Board of Supervisors voted 6-1 to study the legality of a temporary moratorium on new applications, returning Sept. 15. The storage angle is the part our clients should sit with: a battery deployment that would have ridden in on the campus dies with it, collateral to a data-center rejection it had nothing to do with. In the county that is ground zero for the whole data-center backlash, the safest place for a storage asset is a project of its own with its own community case — not a hitchhiker on a hyperscaler's site plan. Watch the Sept. 15 moratorium vote for whether standalone storage gets swept in with the servers.

The fight moved to the wires, and the winning argument is fairness. Answer "who pays" before it's asked — and send the host, not the engineer, to say it.
