RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 45 · Wednesday, August 26, 2026 · Version 2

Good morning, team.

The permission layer and the build layer have come apart, and this week you can see daylight between them from four different angles. PJM has now cleared more than 50 gigawatts of solar, wind and batteries through an interconnection review that took some of those projects seven years — and the grid operator's own spokesman says nobody has the authority to make a single megawatt of it get built. In West Virginia, commission staff moved to suspend or dismiss a 107-mile 500-kV application because a late route tweak put three landowners in the path who were never told — the same failure mode that put a $33 billion plan on the ropes in Texas, now running in a second state. In Louisiana, the Iberville Parish Council voted down a 200-megawatt Entergy project 7-4 even though the developer built the thing around the parish's own 2023 solar ordinance, prompting the parish president to note that his council had declined to follow a rule it wrote itself. And nationally, NRDC put a number on the policy environment: 390 to 540 gigawatts of wind, solar and storage that will not get built this decade, replaced by at most nine gigawatts of gas. Add it up and the job description shifts. Winning the vote is no longer the finish line; it is the point at which the harder work — keeping the project financeable, the record clean and the county still willing — actually starts.

Must Read

Permission Is Not a Project

PJM has done the thing everyone in this industry has spent five years demanding, and the result is a warning rather than a win. The grid operator finished interconnection review on a cluster containing projects that had been waiting since 2018, and more than 50 gigawatts of new capacity came out the other side — overwhelmingly solar, wind and batteries, according to RMI's read of the approved set. That is a remarkable fact on its own, because it is not what is going in the front door. Gas dominates new queue applications and the fast-track requests. What cleared is renewables, because renewables are what was sitting in the queue when the music stopped.

Then comes the part that should reorganize how we think about our own scope of work. PJM spokesperson Jeff Shields told Canary Media: "We would love to see all those projects get built, but we don't have the authority to make it happen." That is not a dodge. It is an accurate description of the seam in the American electricity system. An interconnection agreement is a permission slip, not an obligation, and the historical attrition rate on permission slips is brutal — Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory found that 41% of U.S. projects that signed interconnection agreements between 2000 and 2022 later withdrew. Apply anything close to that rate to 50 gigawatts and roughly 20 gigawatts of cleared, studied, network-upgrade-priced capacity evaporates across the thirteen states and the District that PJM serves.

The stakes are quantified. An Aurora Energy Research study commissioned by RMI found that adding just five gigawatts of solar and five gigawatts of wind in PJM by 2028 would save ratepayers $10.9 billion over a decade. GridLab program director Nikhil Kumar put the timing argument bluntly: these are the only resources that can be "deployed fast enough to do something before 2030," and in his view "state governors need to force their utilities to help get more generation online." That is a striking thing for a grid analyst to say out loud, and it tells you where the pressure is about to be applied — not on the queue, which is now moving, but on the gap between a cleared project and a financed one.

For a community-engagement firm, that gap is the whole business, and it is worth being precise about why projects fall out of it. Some die on economics — a network upgrade cost allocation that no offtake can carry, a tax equity market that repriced, a turbine order book that slipped. But a very large share die on the local layer: a host community that soured during the wait, an ordinance rewritten in the four years between the queue application and the construction start, a board that seated three new members who campaigned on the project, a landowner coalition that had time to organize because nothing visible happened for half a decade. Seven years is long enough for a county to change its mind twice. The queue reform that everyone lobbied for has, as a side effect, produced an enormous inventory of projects whose community consent was secured under conditions that no longer exist.

So the practical read of the PJM number is not "50 gigawatts is coming." It is: 50 gigawatts of projects just became simultaneously eligible to compete for the same interconnection-adjacent scarcities — equipment, capital, EPC crews, and local goodwill — and the ones that lose will lose in county rooms as often as in credit committees. The developers who move first will be the ones who never let the community relationship go dormant during the wait.

⚡ The Takeaway

Treat the queue-clearance date as the trigger for a re-engagement campaign, not a victory lap. Three concrete moves. First, audit every cleared project's local consent as if it were expiring: who sits on the board now versus when the application was filed, what has the ordinance done since, which neighboring county has adopted restrictive language that could travel. Second, get visible on the ground before construction mobilization, because the single most common thing a hostile board says is "we approved something years ago and then heard nothing" — silence during a seven-year wait is read as indifference, and it is very hard to un-read. Third, be candid with clients that a 41% historical withdrawal rate means the queue position is the cheap part; if a project is going to die, it is cheaper to learn that from the community in month two than from a lender in month thirty. The firms that turn the PJM backlog into steel will be the ones treating clearance as the start of the engagement clock rather than the end of it.

Also On Our Radar
  • Half of Everything: NRDC published modeling Wednesday putting the ten-year cost of current federal energy policy at 390 to 540 gigawatts of wind, solar and storage that will not be built, attributing the loss to IRA tax credit rollbacks, tariffs and offshore wind lease buybacks. The number that matters most is the replacement figure: "At most, only 9 GW of additional gas capacity is added with Trump's policies in place." Amanda Levin, NRDC's director of policy analysis, told reporters the modeling still shows "significant growth in renewables," but "we lose more than half of everything that we expected to be able to build." NRDC blames turbine supply bottlenecks and fuel price volatility for the thin gas backfill, and argues the drift toward simple-cycle and reciprocating-engine plants is a reason to keep the EPA gas rule the administration intends to repeal. Carry the 9 GW figure into affordability conversations — the counterargument to "renewables are being replaced by reliable generation" is that, per this model, mostly they are being replaced by nothing.
  • Arizona Quietly Wins the Battery Race: the second-largest battery build in the country in the first half of 2026 happened in a state with no deregulated market and no storage mandate. Canary Media's read of EIA data shows Arizona added more battery capacity than any state but Texas — three 250-MW/1-GWh projects named Beehive, Catclaw and Pediment plus several smaller ones — while effectively tying Florida for second-most solar installed nationally, alongside 300 MW of gas and 500 MW of wind. The mechanism is unglamorous and instructive: vertically integrated utilities (APS, Tucson Electric Power, Salt River Project) signing contracts roughly five years ago, per Autumn Johnson, executive director of SEIA's Arizona branch. The forward-looking caveat is that the elected Corporation Commission recently eliminated the state's two-decade-old renewable energy standard, so the current wave reflects procurement decisions made under a policy regime that no longer exists. For siting work, the lesson is that a utility procurement calendar is a better five-year predictor of where the fights will be than any state RPS.
  • Every Inverter with a Wire: the FCC broadened its foreign-inverter restrictions to cover any grid-interactive inverter that contains or is built to accept remote communication hardware — Ethernet and other wired connections now count alongside Wi-Fi, cellular and Bluetooth — after input from the Department of War. The offsetting move is a safe harbor: inverters from manufacturers eligible for Section 45X advanced manufacturing credits will not be treated as foreign-made, an alternative to Buy American content thresholds. Norton Rose Fulbright's summary is the quotable version: "The FCC said the nationality of the company making the inverter is irrelevant," though FEOC rules still bar credits for prohibited foreign entities. The agency also pulled pure AC-to-DC rectifiers and off-grid inverters off the Covered List and will permit security patches on already-installed units. Expect equipment-provenance questions to start appearing in hearing rooms, because "who made the electronics" is now a federal category and opponents read federal categories.
  • The Recycling Problem Opponents Are About to Discover: a white paper from circular-supply-chain analyst Isaac Nichelson of Sustainable Source Studios alleges that roughly 90% of decommissioned U.S. solar panels go to landfills and that most of what gets called recycling is a shred-and-export pipeline — routed, in the paper's telling, through Korea Zinc and its U.S. subsidiaries PedalPoint Holdings and evTerra. "Almost none of what the United States calls 'solar recycling' is closed-loop recovery," Nichelson wrote, projecting one million tons of retired modules by 2030 and about ten million by 2050. The industry pushback is substantive: Solarcycle says more than 80% of panel mass stays in the U.S. and it achieves "97% value recovery," and Solar Panel Recycling LLC says it was never contacted and that its throughput was understated by roughly two-thirds. Note also that S3 discloses consulting for the one company it endorses. None of that will matter at a township meeting. Get decommissioning and end-of-life language into host agreements now, and know that R2v3 Appendix G — the audited PV standard requiring zero-landfill downstream traceability — becomes mandatory for R2-certified facilities on Jan. 31, 2027.
  • Ninety-Seven to One Eighty-Nine: gas-fired generation proposed for direct connection to U.S. data centers roughly doubled in six months, from 97 GW to 189 GW, according to Global Energy Monitor's tally of announced, pre-construction and construction-phase on-site generation. The reason to treat that number skeptically is inside the same analysis: two-thirds of gas capacity in development globally, and more than half of the data-center-tied projects, have no named turbine or engine manufacturer, and nearly a quarter of the data-center projects have no named start year. GEM cites turbine supply constraints, financing uncertainty, local moratoriums and public opposition as reasons much of it will not materialize. This is now the standard rebuttal in load-growth hearings — a headline gigawatt figure with no equipment order behind it is an announcement, not a plan.
  • Bludgeon, Allegedly: two filings landed at FERC this month that turn the who-pays-for-the-upgrades fight into open combat between hyperscalers and utilities. Microsoft told the commission on Aug. 21 that American Transmission Co.'s large-load agreements for its Mount Pleasant, Wisconsin campus "contain significant deficiencies — errors, contradictions and inconsistencies," lack any mechanism to keep retail customers off the hook for facility costs, could make large loads "pay twice," and carry an early-termination fee amounting to "an unjustified windfall"; it asked for settlement judge proceedings. The Wisconsin PSC separately called the agreements "far from fully responsive" to FERC's show cause order. And PowerHouse Hillwood urged FERC to reject ComEd's cancellation of a transmission security agreement tied to a 1.8-GW, $20 billion Joliet campus, warning the commission "must remain vigilant as to the risk that utilities like ComEd will use their monopoly power as a bludgeon." FERC has pushed RTO responses on the large-load show cause orders to mid-November. Every one of these documents is public, and every one of them is ammunition the next time a county asks who is actually paying for the wires.
Project Updates
  • WVThree Landowners, One Application: Public Service Commission staff attorneys and engineers moved to suspend or dismiss NextEra Energy Transmission's 107.5-mile MidAtlantic Resiliency Link, a 500-kV line running from Dunkard Township, Pa., through Monongalia, Preston, Mineral, Hampshire and Jefferson counties to Gore, Va. The trigger was a July 30 route revision supplement — five changes adding about $1 million in cost and putting three landowners in the path who were never notified and missed the June 11 public comment hearing. Staff wrote that NextEra "expects laypeople who are impacted by the line to be able to determine the best way to advocate for their interest in less than 39 days," and recommended tolling the case at least 60 days or dismissing the application as incomplete. Evidentiary hearing is set for Oct. 26 with direct testimony due Sept. 8; Tony Campbell of West Virginians Against Transmission Injustice says the staff memos warrant outright dismissal.
  • AZFifty-Two Conditions in Pinal: the Board of Supervisors was scheduled to vote Wednesday on rezoning more than 3,000 acres about twelve miles outside Eloy for the La Osa Energy Center — eleven data center buildings, a gas plant and a battery storage facility, with total demand capped at one gigawatt after developer Vermaland agreed to scale the project back 80% in the face of community opposition. The county stacked 19 new conditions on top of the 33 discussed in May: a 600 acre-feet-per-year potable water cap, annual public water-use reporting, noise studies, a requirement that Vermaland self-power until a utility proves sufficient supply, and — the one worth copying — mandatory decommissioning of the BESS after 24 months without use. Two planning and zoning commissioners said in a recent special session they still do not know enough about data-center water and energy impacts to recommend approval confidently.
  • INFavorable, With Signs Everywhere: Madison County planning staff issued a favorable recommendation on rezoning two Adams Township parcels from agricultural to High Impact for Jupiter Power's Liberty 1 and Liberty 2 battery projects, conditioned on noise and light mitigation at the Board of Zoning Appeals stage. Jupiter is weighing a $500 million investment for 380 MW, with the sites operational in 2028 and 2031; Indiana & Michigan Power has already signed an agreement to buy power from Liberty 1, which sits next to I&M's largest substation in the county. The county's Technical Review Committee split two favorable and three neutral on Liberty 1 and one favorable and four neutral on Liberty 2, and staff noted flatly that "numerous signs oppose the proposed development" throughout the area. Plan Commission takes it up Sept. 9; the Board of Commissioners holds final approval.
  • ILHancock Approves Over the Objection: the county board voted 9-2 on Aug. 18 to approve special use permits for two solar projects near Augusta — Hwy-61 Sun LLC and Hwy-61 Sun 2 LLC, on land leased from Larry McElroy by Alex Farkas of Cornview Development — after sending the applications back to committee following a July hearing. Board member Steve Finney argued the record was defective: "These permits do not meet the ordinance. The application is inaccurate and incomplete," pointing to a site plan showing no public access roads or turnout locations and an outstanding IDOT highway permit. County energy counsel Andrew Keyt countered that "the application complies with your ordinance, and appears to comply with the state statute," and that road-use agreements normally arrive at the building-permit stage. Four members were absent.
  • IAPage County Lawyers Up: supervisors faced a room of residents Tuesday in Clarinda over their late-July petition to intervene at the Iowa Utilities Commission against MidAmerican Energy's certificate application for Nodaway Solar, filed on the board's behalf by Ahlers & Cooney attorney Tim Whipple. Resident Cindy Van Fosson thanked the board for "listening to the recommendation of the Page County attorney and hiring outside legal counsel" and urged them to keep fighting "against bullies." Jean Wagoner, who lives near the site northeast of Clarinda, laid out the stakes precisely: once the IUC issues a certificate of public convenience, use and necessity, "the utility provider can legally proceed with construction, regardless of local restrictions." A county retaining outside energy counsel to fight a state docket is a different opponent than a county passing a moratorium, and it is becoming the more common one.
  • CASan Diego Drafts Its First Battery Rules: county planning staff took the first-ever draft BESS zoning ordinance to the Campo Lake Morena Community Planning Group on Monday. The draft covers anything above 600 kilowatt-hours and creates three tiers — Tier 1 up to 80 MWh or 5 acres, Tier 2 up to 400 MWh or 10 acres, Tier 3 above that — triggering Site Plan Review, Minor Use Permit or Major Use Permit respectively, with a base 100-foot setback, decommissioning plans and financial assurance. Crucially, county staffer Donna Durckel confirmed the rules apply "only to projects submitted after it becomes effective," exempting the roughly 600-acre Starlight Solar project in Boulevard and its 217-MW battery. Additional public sessions run Aug. 27 and Sept. 17; Planning Commission and Board of Supervisors hearings are tentatively winter or spring 2027. Filing ahead of an ordinance remains the single highest-leverage scheduling decision a developer makes.
  • WAA Gigawatt at the Smelter: Copenhagen Infrastructure Partners has proposed a roughly 1,200 MW, $1 billion battery installation on 120 agricultural acres at the former aluminum smelter site adjacent to John Day Dam in Klickitat County, upstream of the proposed Goldendale pumped-storage lower reservoir and a 1,118 MW data center, sharing a 500-kV interconnection. The project sits in the queue while the county's industrial solar and BESS ordinance awaits legal review and another planning commission hearing, possibly in September. CIP has argued earlier drafts were overly restrictive and would push developers toward Washington's EFSEC, which can override local land use — a threat that is also a negotiating position. A proposed change to the county's Energy Overlay Zone would drop the smelter land from the map entirely.
  • MAHaverhill Punts to September: city councilors postponed their public hearing on battery storage zoning overlay districts to Sept. 15, at the request of Council Vice President John A. Michitson, who chairs the committee developing the siting criteria and was absent. The council has asked the planning department to rank suitable sites; in May the city solicitor drafted an ordinance that would allow a battery farm at the former Dutton Airport off Route 110 near Northern Essex Community College. The city's environmental health technician told councilors most of the 96-acre parcel is off limits because of the endangered Blanding's turtle, but the 15-acre airport footprint itself is flat, clear of the marsh, adjacent to a transmission line and just off I-495. A municipality that pre-identifies its own preferred site is doing half the developer's work — and keeping the veto.
On the Frontlines
  • NYAlle-Catt's Forty Decibels: Cattaraugus County legislator Brenda Hanson says Invenergy's 340-MW Alle-Catt Wind project "backed itself into a corner" by erecting turbines that cannot meet the 40 dBA annual-average nighttime limit in its own certificate, and she has had no answer to a June 24 letter to the state siting board. The board denied the developer's request to strip the pre-construction noise-modeling condition on June 23. Alle-Catt swapped in larger machines to hit 340 MW from 83 turbine sites instead of 118 — 41 of them in Cattaraugus County, blades reaching nearly 600 feet — and county attorney Benjamin Wisniewski filed a July 23 rehearing petition asking that no turbine in Centerville, Rushford or Farmersville be commissioned until a revised noise assessment is approved. Invenergy counsel John W. Dax called the petitions "premised on mischaracterizations." A condition nobody fought over at certification is now the whole fight at commissioning.
  • NYThirty Million, Two Jobs: Madison County town halls overflowed this week over a 20-year PILOT worth more than $30.4 million in exemptions for the 18-turbine Hoffman Falls Wind project, which already holds state siting approval across Fenner, Nelson, Eaton and Smithfield. Every seat was filled at the Nelson hearing; IDA Executive Director Kipp Hicks asked people who had already spoken to step outside so others could get in. One speaker distilled the opposition frame into a single sentence: "This is about giving 30 million dollars in lieu of taxes to a company who will create two permanent jobs in our county." Hicks put the investment at nearly $430 million and noted the IDA is not limited to a straight yes or no. The county Board of Supervisors has formally asked the IDA to reject it; the seven-member board meets Sept. 10 in Canastota. State preemption wins the siting question and then hands opponents a second venue with a much better sound bite.
  • ILSix Hundred Sixty-Three in Peoria: Repsol Renewables filed a special use permit application on Aug. 14 for the Four Creeks Wind Project — 113 turbines, 663 MW, more than 27,000 acres of northwest Peoria County north of Brimfield and west of Princeville, with machines reaching almost 660 feet. Illinois statute gives the county 60 days to complete hearings and 30 more to decide, and planning and zoning director Andrew Braun has booked a hotel ballroom for Sept. 14 with overflow dates running through Oct. 1: "We anticipate that those hearings will last several days." Repsol development manager Carson Robers estimated more than $5 million in first-year revenue for the Princeville and Brimfield school districts. Braun's own forecast is the sobering part — "it very well could be 2031 before any construction commences."
  • VASix Fifty on a Superfund-Adjacent Slab: Tenaska subsidiary Pelican Energy Storage wants 650 MW of batteries on 220 acres of the abandoned BASF site in the Grove community of James City County, land crossed by Dominion's Skiffes Creek line that currently produces under $30,000 a year in taxes. Pelican's pitch: about $90 million in county taxes over 25 years, more than $1 billion into the local economy, roughly 100 construction jobs plus 178 indirect, and five to 15 vehicle trips a week once operating. County staff wrote that the use "would minimally disturb the site and put it to productive use" — the parcel has been dormant more than 25 years with soil and groundwater contamination from Dow and BASF acrylic fiber operations under an EPA and Virginia DEQ mitigation agreement. Planners did warn Pelican that Grove residents may object to two years of construction disruption, and Carter's Grove Plantation sits a quarter mile away. The brownfield-siting logic that got Ohio to yes on a strip mine is now being run on a contaminated riverfront parcel in Tidewater.
  • LALouisiana Turns: two solar fights in one week, and both cut against the developer. The Iberville Parish Council voted 7-4 to reject Entergy's 200-MW, 1,600-acre Cypress Harvest Solar on former sugarcane land five miles outside Plaquemine, despite Entergy regional manager Seth Schilling telling the council the company had spent "millions of dollars" designing to the parish's own 2023 solar ordinance: "We are not asking for special treatment or changes to the rules." Landowner Klein Kirby of A. Wilbert Sons warned the vote sets "a precedent that requires landowners to submit every lawful use of private property to a public vote"; Parish President Chris Daigle noted the council "decided to not follow an ordinance they put in place," and the parish now has 30 days to put its reasons in writing. Meanwhile in Calcasieu Parish, Orion Renewable's 4,700-acre, 1-GW Persimmon Energy Center drew organized opposition before an application was even filed, with Rep. Brett Geymann drafting both a setback bill and a moratorium bill after his HB 615 failed 47-52 on the House floor last year. Geymann's frame is a comparison — Orion's 300-foot residential setback under Act 279 guidance versus Iberia Parish's 2,640 feet — and comparisons are the most portable argument in this business.
  • AZThree Points Organizes Early: hundreds of residents packed a meeting southwest of Tucson to fight Repsol subsidiary CG Three Points' proposed 5,554-acre, 500-MW solar farm with a 250-MW battery, a $575 million State Land Department application with a 35-year term. The land department says no lease has been auctioned and Repsol "has not submitted any due diligence"; Pima County confirms no formal county application exists. In other words, the opposition organized before the process began — and its leader, surgeon-rancher Doug Lowell of the newly formed Open Space Alliance Southern Arizona, has already named the strategy out loud: "We are, 1,000 votes out of 15,000 maybe, that vote in our district. What we have to do to get from here to killing the project is to generalize the issue outside of our small community." That is a sophisticated read of local political math, and it is the tell that this group has professional help or very good instincts. A landowner mailer sent July 31 with no community meeting attached bought them six weeks of uncontested framing.
  • WAGrant County's First: Invenergy and Grant County PUD broke ground Aug. 18 on the 120-MW Quincy Solar Energy Center, the county's first utility-scale solar plant, sited a few miles northwest of Big Bend Community College near Moses Lake. Invenergy lead developer Mateo Gomez put it at roughly 25,000 homes powered, "over 200 union construction workers" at peak and "over $27 million in economic investment" through taxes, lease payments and local spending; state Rep. Alex Ybarra framed it against Washington's 2045 zero-carbon mandate. The detail worth stealing is small and cheap: Invenergy handed the PUD a $20,000 check for its Share the Light bill-assistance program, which the PUD says "will help over 600 families keep the lights on." A ratepayer-assistance contribution at groundbreaking answers the affordability objection in the only currency that registers locally.
  • CAPlug and Play Clears the House: the Assembly passed SB 868 on Tuesday, sending Sen. Scott Wiener's Plug and Play Solar Act toward the governor's desk. The bill legalizes portable solar devices up to 1,200 watts that plug into an ordinary household receptacle, exempting users from interconnection agreements and utility permission — though amendments sunset both exemptions on Jan. 1, 2030, and the law would take effect Jan. 1, 2027. Passage would make California the eleventh state with a plug-in solar law. EWG California vice president Bernadette Del Chiaro said the state "is poised to fling its doors wide open to a real, tangible affordability solution for everyday consumers." pv magazine's caution is worth noting: the bill "would not likely survive a veto," and California has not overridden one since 1979.
  • NJTwo Hundred a Kilowatt: the Board of Public Utilities published a straw proposal targeting up to 150 MW of behind-the-meter storage paying customers up to $200/kW per year over ten years to discharge during dispatch events called by Atlantic City Electric, JCP&L, PSE&G and Rockland Electric. It is the first capacity block of Phase 2 of the Garden State Energy Storage Program, which requires 2 GW statewide by 2030; the state is halfway there on the strength of 1 GW of transmission-connected procurement earlier this year. BPU President Ben Hertz-Shargel tied it to Gov. Sherrill's Executive Order No. 2. Notably, regulators set the incentive deliberately below the cost of the battery, reasoning that "many consumers have some willingness to pay for resilience and thus do not require an incentive high enough to render the net cost of battery back-up power to zero." Stakeholder meeting Sept. 3, comments due Sept. 10.

Fifty gigawatts cleared a queue this year and nobody is obligated to build any of it. Three unnotified landowners can stall a 107-mile transmission line. A parish council can vote down a project designed to its own ordinance and then spend thirty days explaining why. The permission we win is real, and it is also perishable — it decays during the wait, it can be revoked on procedure, and it means nothing if the county has stopped taking our calls. Keep the relationship warm in the years when nothing is happening. That is the whole job.
