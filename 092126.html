RenUSA Leadership Brief

Volume 52 · Monday, September 21, 2026

Happy Monday, all.

The week's decisions all turned on the same question — who has to ask permission, and for how much. Virginia's governor signed an executive order Friday that would end by-right approval for any data center drawing more than 25 megawatts, converting a permit-counter transaction into a public hearing in the state that hosts more of them than anywhere on earth. Whitman County, Washington — which banned commercial wind outright in March — spent the summer writing the opposite instrument, a hard 5,000-acre countywide ceiling on utility-scale solar that does not deny any single project and quietly forecloses most of them. Texas regulators approved another 370 miles of 765-kV across 14 counties and told the builders to go back and renegotiate the route with landowners after the fact. San Diego supervisors approved 588 acres of solar and 868 megawatt-hours of batteries unanimously, over a room full of people who drove an hour to say no. Permission granted, permission withheld, permission redefined — and in every case the fight was over the threshold, not the project.

Must Read

By Right, No More

Gov. Abigail Spanberger signed Executive Order 22 on Friday, Sept. 18, alongside a legislative package her office calls the Data Center Accountability Framework — described from the podium as the most comprehensive such effort in the country. The framework rests on five pillars: transparency, environmental protections, lowering energy costs, promoting clean energy, and local workers. The order itself puts the pieces the executive branch can reach into motion immediately; the rest goes to the General Assembly in January.

Three provisions matter more than the rest. First, the framework would end by-right approval for data centers drawing more than 25 megawatts, requiring a local discretionary approval for projects that in much of Northern Virginia have been clearing on a zoning administrator's signature. Second, it bans non-disclosure agreements — EO 22 bars state agencies from entering or requiring them on data center projects outright, and the legislative package would extend that to agreements between localities and developers. Third, and least covered, it directs utilities to allocate a more equitable share of transmission and generation costs to data centers and other large-load customers, with higher standards for emissions, water use, noise and land use, including a move to Tier IV or better backup generators. EO 22 also stands up a Virginia AI Task Force on workforce displacement, data privacy and cybersecurity.

Read the first provision the way a developer's land team will read it. Virginia did not ban anything. It moved a large and previously invisible category of project from the administrative track to the political one — which means every one of those projects now needs a public record, a hearing, a room, and someone in it who is not the applicant's lawyer. That is not a restriction on data centers. It is the mass production of the exact proceedings our business exists to work.

The second provision is the one that changes how those rooms behave. The single most reliable accelerant of organized opposition in a siting fight is the discovery that a deal was negotiated under an NDA — it converts a land-use argument into a trust argument, and trust arguments do not have technical answers. Fredericksburg is the case study sitting in our own backyard: the council killed the 1500 Gateway project 7-0 after midnight in August, and the through-line of that record was not megawatts, it was whether the city had been told the truth about what was coming and where. Take the NDA away and the developer loses a shield it was mostly using to cut itself.

The third provision is the one that reaches our clients' projects directly. If Virginia genuinely reallocates transmission and generation cost responsibility toward large load, the ratepayer-impact argument that opponents have used against solar — you are building this for someone else's server farm and putting it on my bill — loses its strongest premise in the biggest market in PJM. That argument has been traveling fast. It showed up in Ohio's power-bill fight, in the Northern Virginia transmission hearings, and in half the county-board rooms we have been in this year. Virginia is about to test whether pricing the load honestly takes the air out of it.

The caveat is real: the order is an order. A future governor can rescind it, and the pieces that need statute — the cost allocation, the locality NDA ban, the environmental standards with teeth — need a General Assembly that has said no to smaller versions of this before. Between now and January, the fight will be over which pillars survive drafting.

The Takeaway

Two moves out of this, and one warning. First, if the 25-megawatt threshold holds, Virginia becomes the highest-volume discretionary-approval market in the country roughly overnight, and the constraint will not be developer demand — it will be the supply of localities that know how to run the hearing. There is a real opening in getting to county staff and planning commissions before the applications land, with the record, the fire-safety answer and the cost math already built. Second, the NDA ban should change our default posture on every file, not just Virginia's: assume disclosure is coming, and stop letting clients buy short-term quiet at the price of a trust argument we cannot win later. The warning is that this framework was written about data centers and will be read about everything. The same "more than 25 megawatts triggers local approval" language is one amendment away from catching utility-scale solar and storage, and the groups drafting the county-level copies of it are not making the distinction. Watch the General Assembly drafting in January for exactly that verb — facility versus data center.

Also On Our Radar
  • The Cap Is the Ban: the Whitman County, Washington planning commission has written two numbers into its draft utility-scale solar ordinance — a 5,000-acre countywide ceiling and a 500-acre limit on any single facility — after commissioners split for months over whether to cap projects individually at all. The same draft imports the county's wind-code decommissioning language wholesale, adds an "irrevocable standby trust fund" definition, and requires a surety bond or equivalent instrument to guarantee removal. County mapping put roughly 11,600 acres as potentially eligible under the draft setbacks, so the cap does not bind the map — it binds the second and third projects. At the Sept. 2 meeting a resident asked the commission to consolidate applicant/owner/operator definitions, require full organizational disclosure, protect hilltops, ridgelines and scenic byways, ban long-term herbicide use on panel sites, and have counsel review the state agrivoltaics statutes against the local code. The county's commissioners banned commercial wind outright in March on a 2-1 vote. A cap is a far more durable instrument than a moratorium: it never expires, it never denies anyone, and it never generates the single dramatic no that draws a lawsuit.
  • Route Four, Mod One: the Public Utility Commission of Texas approved the Howard-to-Solstice 765-kV line on Sept. 11 — roughly 370 miles running from the San Antonio area out to the Permian Basin across 14 counties including Bexar, Medina, Kerr and Uvalde — and then directed the two transmission companies to keep working with landowners on further route modifications. It is the second approval in as many weeks, following more than 400 miles of Oncor 765-kV cleared on Aug. 28, both under the Permian Basin Reliability Plan. Note the sequencing, because it is the whole story: the commission granted the certificate first and assigned the landowner negotiation as a condition afterward. Every easement conversation on that corridor now happens with the approval already in hand and the landowner's leverage spent.
  • Boulevard Says No, the Board Says Yes: San Diego County supervisors unanimously approved a major use permit for the Starlight Solar project on Sept. 16 — 588 acres of panels plus an 868-megawatt-hour battery system in the rural East County community of Boulevard — after dozens of residents made the hour-long drive to the county administration center to oppose it. The planning commission had already signed off 7-0 in July. Objections ran to lithium fire risk and the toxic gas it produces, groundwater protection, and the alteration of the rural landscape. Two unanimous votes against a full room is the outcome developers say they want and the one that most reliably produces the next ordinance: nobody in Boulevard got a vote they could win, which is precisely the condition under which counties start writing caps.
  • Lawyer Up, Then What: Ranger Power's Shipyard Solar would put roughly 1,800 acres of panels across Wexford and Grand Traverse counties in northern Michigan — 375 megawatts of solar, plus a 375-megawatt battery facility in the Grant Township portion — which would make it the largest solar farm in the state and lift Michigan's solar output by more than 10%. The Wexford joint planning commission recommended denial of the enabling zoning amendment 8-0. Grant Township retained outside counsel on Sept. 1 because its general counsel had no renewable-siting experience, and Grand Traverse County moved a day later to secure a voice in a project it has no authority to block. Public Act 233 lets the developer go straight to the Michigan Public Service Commission. The legal spend here is not about stopping the project — it is about buying standing in a proceeding the township was written out of, and that is the budget line every host community in a preemption state is about to discover.
  • Forty and Seventy-Six: a study released Sept. 15 finds the share of Iowa counties with a solar ordinance on the books rose from 33% to 40% in a single year, and wind ordinances from 61% to 76%, with a large majority now carrying decommissioning provisions. Read that as the end of the greenfield era in a state where three-quarters of counties now have a wind rule waiting for you. The ordinances are not uniformly hostile — decommissioning language is the most common provision, not setbacks — but the window in which a county's first renewable application also wrote the county's first renewable rules is closing fast, and with it the single best opportunity we have to shape a code from the inside.
  • Four Uses, One Motion: Cedar County, Missouri commissioners imposed a 12-month moratorium on new large solar, wind, battery-storage and data-center development outside city limits, effective Sept. 8. The drafting is the point, and it is now the dominant pattern: solar, wind and storage are swept into the same ordinance as data centers, in a county where the political energy was almost certainly about data centers. Nobody in that room campaigned against a battery. Storage got banned anyway, because it was in the same sentence — and a year from now the county will write permanent rules from a draft that treats all four as one land use.
  • Four Days of Testimony, Twenty-Five Thousand Acres: the Peoria County, Illinois zoning board of appeals ran hearings Sept. 15 through 18 on the Four Creeks wind project — more than 100 turbines across roughly 25,000 acres in Brimfield, Millbrook, Princeville and Jubilee townships — and continued the hearing to Sept. 28. Attorney Rick Porter is appearing for more than 30 landowners arguing the application should have been rejected as filed. A multi-week evidentiary hearing with opposing counsel is now the standard shape of a large Illinois wind case, not the exception, and it is a different exercise from a public comment night: what gets said on the record here is what the circuit court reads if this is appealed either direction.
On the Frontlines
  • NYWalked In, Passed Same Night: the Dunkirk Common Council unanimously approved a six-month moratorium on new battery energy storage applications on Sept. 16 — as a walk-in resolution that never appeared on the agenda. The planning board asked for it while holding a live application for a BESS behind a Brigham Road warehouse; an earlier approved project further back on the same road is unaffected. The resolution cites "unique land use, fire safety and environmental considerations" the zoning code does not address, and the city wants time to work NYSERDA's model BESS law into its own code "without the pressure of pending applications." Residential systems under 20 kWh are exempt. A walk-in moratorium with a live application on the desk is the fastest instrument in municipal government and the hardest to see coming.
  • WIRules Before the Ask: the Village of Paddock Lake adopted solar energy facility and battery storage site ordinances on Sept. 17, in a corner of Kenosha County where the county board has had a BESS moratorium running since spring. Writing the code before an application lands remains the only version of this that ends with a permit pathway rather than a pause — and the villages doing it now are doing it because the county above them stopped.
  • ILFourth and Final: the Will County Board voted 14-7 to grant a fourth and explicitly final 180-day extension to a 4-megawatt solar farm near Peotone tangled in ownership and permit disputes. Four extensions on a 4-megawatt project is not a siting fight, it is an administrative one — and it is the reason boards that have been through it start writing sunset clauses that do not renew.
  • SCFive to Three, No Pause: Darlington County Council voted 5-3 against a proposed nine-month moratorium on new solar and wind projects on Sept. 10, after dozens of residents spoke on both sides at a public hearing. Existing rules stand. The pressure came from neighbors of a proposed 417-acre solar farm who wanted the pause as leverage to tighten the ordinance; the council declined to give it to them. A moratorium vote that fails is still a warning — it tells you the ordinance rewrite is coming through the front door instead.
  • CTContinued to the 28th: the Farmington plan and zoning commission took extensive public comment on draft ground-mounted solar regulations on Sept. 15 and continued the hearing to Sept. 28. Connecticut siting runs through the state Siting Council for most utility-scale work, which makes town-level ground-mount rules the fight over everything below that threshold — community solar, municipal arrays, the projects that build local familiarity before the big one arrives.
  • TXOne Point Four Billion, Ten Projects: Jupiter Power closed $1.4 billion in project financing across four transactions covering ten utility-scale battery projects in Texas and Michigan totaling 1,500 MW / 3,600 MWh. Two things travel from this. Financing at that scale for Michigan storage says lenders have priced Public Act 233 as a workable pathway rather than a risk. And ten projects funded in one package means ten community conversations that start on the developer's schedule, not the county's.
  • OKA Decision by Thanksgiving: District Judge Douglas Kirkley told an early-September hearing he expects to rule in October or November on NextEra subsidiary Persica Solar's suit against Wagoner County, which rejected a 5,000-acre, $336 million array. The county is defending a denial against a developer with the balance sheet to litigate it for years — the asymmetry that decides most of these cases before the merits do.
  • NYExtended, Not Expired: the Byron town board in Genesee County held public hearings Sept. 9 on extending its temporary moratoriums on both renewable energy projects and battery storage. New York carries more active BESS pauses than any other state by a wide margin, and the extension — not the original enactment — is where they become permanent in practice: nobody who voted for six months has ever been punished for voting for six more.
  • ILDeny and Get Sued, Again: the Madison County zoning board voted against a special-use permit for a solar array near Troy on Sept. 10. The county is already defending multiple suits over prior solar denials, all turning on the 2023 amendment to the Illinois Counties Code barring local standards more restrictive than the state's — and it has already reversed itself on at least one denial under litigation pressure. Boards that keep voting no in a preemption state are not stopping projects. They are choosing to approve them later, in a courtroom, with legal fees attached.
  • PAYes, With Four Strings: Piatt Township supervisors in Lycoming County granted conditional use for a solar energy facility with the county planning commission's endorsement, attaching four conditions: planted and maintained vegetative screening, a 40-decibel noise cap, a decommissioning agreement backed by financial security, and a post-construction environmental assessment. That is close to the model condition set — each one answers a specific objection with a verifiable obligation rather than a promise, and none of them is a number the project cannot hit. Worth keeping as a template for boards asking what a responsible yes looks like.
  • AZSecond Only to Texas: Arizona installed more battery capacity than any state but Texas in the first half of 2026, bringing online three projects at 250 MW / 1 GWh each — Beehive, Catclaw and Pediment — plus several smaller ones. The contrast with New York, where storage is the most-paused use in the country, is not a technology story or a fire-risk story. It is a siting-authority story, and it is the cleanest available argument that BESS deployment tracks permitting structure far more closely than it tracks local sentiment.

Virginia is about to manufacture thousands of discretionary hearings where there were none, Whitman County is about to cap a use it has no intention of denying, and Cedar County banned batteries nobody was arguing about because they were in the same sentence as something else. The drafting is doing the work this fall, not the voting — so the ask for this week is to read the ordinances, not the outcomes. Where a code is being written, we should be in the room before the application is; where one just passed, we should know what verb it used.
